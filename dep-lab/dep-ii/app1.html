Differential Equations in Physics

🌊 The 1-D Time-Dependent Schrödinger Equation

The Schrödinger equation is a complex-valued, linear, dispersive PDE: unlike a non-dispersive wave equation, different wavenumber components travel at different phase speeds, so a localized wave packet doesn't just propagate — it spreads, interferes with itself, and in a bound potential can periodically refocus.

This tab evolves a Gaussian wave packet $\psi(x,t)$ in two canonical 1-D potentials: the infinite square well (particle in a box) and the quantum harmonic oscillator (QHO). The next two tabs reuse this exact same 1-D machinery as a building block for separable problems in higher dimensions and other coordinate systems.

General Time Evolution

Any initial state expands in the eigenbasis, $\psi(x,0)=\sum_n c_n\varphi_n(x)$, and since each stationary state simply acquires a phase:

$$\psi(x,t)=\sum_n c_n\,\varphi_n(x)\,e^{-iE_n t}$$

Rather than compute the $c_n$ explicitly, this module evolves $\psi(x,0)$ directly by finite differences — mathematically equivalent, and the same strategy generalizes cleanly to the harder eigenbases used in Tabs 2 and 3 (Bessel, Laguerre, Legendre) without ever needing those special functions in closed form.

A minimum-uncertainty Gaussian wave packet, centered at $x_0$ with mean momentum $k_0$.

Why Not Explicit FTCS?

Naive explicit time-stepping of the Schrödinger equation is unconditionally unstable — the $i$ in front of $\partial_t$ makes every Fourier mode behave like an undamped oscillator, not a decaying one. A stable explicit scheme needs the real and imaginary parts interleaved in time.

Visscher's Staggered Leapfrog Algorithm

Writing $\psi=\psi_R+i\psi_I$ splits the equation into two coupled real PDEs, $\partial_t\psi_R=H\psi_I$, $\partial_t\psi_I=-H\psi_R$, with $H\psi\equiv-\tfrac12\psi''+V\psi$. Visscher (1991) staggers $\psi_R$ at integer steps and $\psi_I$ at half-steps:

$$\psi_R^{n+1} = \psi_R^{n} + \Delta t\,H\psi_I^{\,n+1/2}, \qquad \psi_I^{\,n+3/2} = \psi_I^{\,n+1/2} - \Delta t\,H\psi_R^{n+1}$$

The probability density at integer steps uses the sandwiching half-steps, which conserves norm to high accuracy: $|\psi_i^{\,n}|^2 \approx (\psi_R^{n})^2 + \psi_I^{\,n-1/2}\psi_I^{\,n+1/2}$.

Stability Condition

$$\Delta t\left(\frac{2}{\Delta x^2}+V_{\max}\right)\leq 2$$

Every simulation in this module clamps $\Delta t$ to satisfy this bound for its grid and potential — the same generic engine (grid, potential, packet, and stepper) is reused unchanged for the 2-D and 3-D / radial problems in the next two tabs, just with a different effective potential fed in.

🌀 The 2-D Schrödinger Equation in Two Coordinate Systems
$$i\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{1}{2}\nabla^2\psi + V(\mathbf r)\psi$$

In two dimensions the same equation admits two natural coordinate choices depending on the symmetry of $V$: Cartesian $(x,y)$ for a separable potential $V(x,y)=V_x(x)+V_y(y)$ (a rectangular box or a 2-D harmonic oscillator), and polar $(\rho,\varphi)$ for a circularly-symmetric potential $V(\rho)$ (a circular well or a radial harmonic well) that additionally conserves angular momentum.

∂ Reduction to 1-D Problems
Cartesian separation

For $V(x,y)=V_x(x)+V_y(y)$, try $\psi(x,y,t)=X(x,t)Y(y,t)$. Substituting shows the full 2-D equation is solved exactly whenever $X$ and $Y$ each independently solve their own 1-D Schrödinger equation:

$$i\partial_t X = -\tfrac12 X'' + V_x(x)X, \qquad i\partial_t Y = -\tfrac12 Y'' + V_y(y)Y$$

because $i\partial_t(XY) = (i\partial_tX)Y+X(i\partial_tY) = \left[-\tfrac12X''+V_xX\right]Y+X\left[-\tfrac12Y''+V_yY\right] = -\tfrac12\nabla^2(XY)+V(XY)$. A separable Gaussian initial state $\psi(x,y,0)=\chi(x,0)\eta(y,0)$ therefore stays a product for all time: $\psi(x,y,t)=\chi(x,t)\,\eta(y,t)$, with $|\psi|^2(x,y,t)=|\chi(x,t)|^2\,|\eta(y,t)|^2$.

Practically: the 1-D Visscher engine from Tab 1 is run twice, once for $x$ and once for $y$, and the 2-D density is their outer product. No new numerical method is needed.

Polar separation

For $V=V(\rho)$, write $\psi(\rho,\varphi,t)=R(\rho,t)\,e^{im\varphi}$. The 2-D Laplacian in polar form, $\nabla^2=\dfrac{1}{\rho}\partial_\rho(\rho\partial_\rho)+\dfrac{1}{\rho^2}\partial_\varphi^2$, then gives an effective 1-D radial equation with a repulsive centrifugal barrier:

$$i\,\partial_t u = -\frac12 u'' + \left[V(\rho)+\frac{m^2-\tfrac14}{2\rho^2}\right]u, \qquad u(\rho,t)\equiv\sqrt{\rho}\,R(\rho,t)$$

(the substitution $u=\sqrt{\rho}R$ removes the first-derivative term, turning the radial equation into a standard 1-D Schrödinger form — the same trick used for the 3-D spherical case in Tab 3, where the analogous substitution is $u=rR$.) This is again exactly the 1-D engine from Tab 1, with $x\to\rho\ (\rho\geq0)$ and an extra $m$-dependent term added to the potential.

Circular-well and radial-harmonic eigenstates

For $V=0$ inside a disk of radius $R_{\max}$, the eigenfunctions are Bessel functions $R_{n}(\rho)=J_m(k_{n}\rho)$ with $k_n$ fixed by the boundary condition $J_m(k_nR_{\max})=0$. For $V=\tfrac12\omega^2\rho^2$, the eigenfunctions involve associated Laguerre polynomials in $\rho^2$. Both families solve the same radial equation above — this module evolves that equation directly by finite differences rather than building the Bessel/Laguerre basis explicitly, exactly as noted for the 1-D case.

💫 Quasi-Bound States and Complex Resonance Energies

A quasi-bound state arises when a particle is trapped behind a finite barrier — here, the centrifugal barrier $l(l+1)/2r^2$ combined with an attractive well — rather than an infinite wall. It is not truly bound: it leaks out by tunneling, decaying in time. This is described by allowing the energy eigenvalue itself to become complex:

$$E = E_r - i\frac{\Gamma}{2}$$

where $E_r$ is the resonance position and $\Gamma$ the decay width. The time dependence $e^{-iEt}=e^{-iE_rt}e^{-\Gamma t/2}$ makes $|\psi|^2\propto e^{-\Gamma t}$ — exponential decay with lifetime $\tau=1/\Gamma$ (natural units $\hbar=1$), directly connecting a stationary-state calculation to a decay rate.

Realistic Two-Body Systems

Up to now the module used natural units ($\hbar=m=1$) purely for pedagogical simplicity. A real quasi-bound state is a two-body problem — two nuclei of masses $m_1,m_2$ and charges $Z_1,Z_2$ orbiting their common center of mass. Exactly as in ordinary two-body mechanics, this reduces to a one-body problem in the relative coordinate $r$, with the single-particle mass replaced by the reduced mass:

$$\mu c^2 = \frac{m_1c^2\,m_2c^2}{m_1c^2+m_2c^2}, \qquad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}u''(r) = \left[E - V(r)\right]u(r)$$

Working in nuclear-physics units — energies in MeV, lengths in fm — the combination $\hbar^2/2\mu$ becomes $(\hbar c)^2/2\mu c^2$ with $\hbar c = 197.3269804$ MeV·fm, so no explicit factors of $\hbar$ ever need to appear numerically.

∂ From the Radial TISE to a Complex Eigenvalue Problem
Radial equation with centrifugal barrier

Exactly as in Tab 3's spherical reduction, $\psi=R(r)Y_l^m$, $u(r)\equiv rR(r)$ satisfies a 1-D Schrödinger form:

$$-\frac12 u''(r) + \left[V(r)+\frac{l(l+1)}{2r^2}\right]u(r) = E\,u(r)$$

with $V(r)$ an attractive Woods–Saxon well, $V(r)=-V_0/\big(1+e^{(r-R)/a}\big)$. For $l\geq1$ the centrifugal term creates a barrier outside $R$ even though $V(r)\to0$ there — the geometry that supports a quasi-bound state.

Why a real-energy bound-state search fails

For $E>0$, the usual bound-state condition "$u\to0$ as $r\to\infty$" has no real-energy solution — every real $E>0$ radial solution is an oscillating standing wave (mixture of incoming and outgoing) at large $r$, never purely decaying. Trapping shows up instead as a rapid buildup of probability inside the barrier at special energies.

The Siegert (purely outgoing) boundary condition

Gamow and Siegert's resolution: keep the same differential equation, but demand a purely outgoing wave at large $r$ and let $E$ go complex:

$$u(r) \xrightarrow{r\to\infty} C\,e^{ikr}, \qquad k=\sqrt{2E},\quad E=E_r-i\frac{\Gamma}{2}$$

Because $E$ has negative imaginary part, $k=k_r-ik_i$ also picks up a negative imaginary part, and $e^{ikr}=e^{ik_rr}\,e^{k_ir}$ grows exponentially with $r$. This is the price of demanding a purely outgoing wave with no incoming component: the state is not normalizable, and $|u|^2$ should only be trusted in and near the well.

Matching condition

Equivalently, the logarithmic derivative of $u$ at the boundary of the integration domain $R_{\max}$ must equal that of a pure outgoing wave:

$$F(E)\;\equiv\; u'(R_{\max};E) - i\,k(E)\,u(R_{\max};E) \;=\;0$$

$F(E)$ is an analytic (holomorphic) function of the complex variable $E$ once $u(r;E)$ is obtained by integrating the ODE outward from $u(0)=0$. Its complex roots are exactly the Gamow/Siegert resonance energies — this converts the boundary-value problem into a complex root-finding problem.

Coulomb Barrier and the Sommerfeld-Corrected Outgoing Wave

Charged fragments ($Z_1Z_2\neq0$) add a long-range Coulomb term. Treating each nucleus as a uniformly charged sphere of radius $R_C$ (standard nuclear-physics form, finite at the origin unlike a bare point charge):

$$V_C(r)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{Z_1Z_2e^2}{2R_C}\left(3-\dfrac{r^2}{R_C^2}\right), & r\le R_C\\[2mm] \dfrac{Z_1Z_2e^2}{r}, & r>R_C\end{cases}\qquad e^2=1.4400\ \text{MeV·fm}$$

The long $1/r$ Coulomb tail means the true asymptotic outgoing wave is not the plane wave $e^{ikr}$ used earlier — it is the outgoing Coulomb (Gamow) wave, whose phase runs logarithmically: $\theta(r)\approx kr-\eta\ln(2kr)-\tfrac{l\pi}{2}+\sigma_l$, where $\eta$ is the (complex, since $E$ is complex) Sommerfeld parameter:

$$\eta(E)=\frac{Z_1Z_2e^2\mu c^2}{(\hbar c)^2\,k(E)}, \qquad k(E)=\sqrt{\frac{2\mu c^2}{(\hbar c)^2}E}$$

Differentiating the phase gives $\dfrac{d}{dr}\ln u \to i\!\left(k-\dfrac{\eta}{r}\right)$ for large $r$ — the leading-order (WKB) Coulomb correction to the matching condition used in Step 4:

$$F(E)\;=\;u'(R_{\max};E)\;-\;i\left[k(E)-\frac{\eta(E)}{R_{\max}}\right]u(R_{\max};E)\;=\;0$$

This reduces exactly to the earlier centrifugal-only condition when $Z_1Z_2=0$ (e.g. the deuteron). A full treatment would replace $e^{ikr}$ with the exact outgoing Coulomb-Hankel function $H_l^+(\eta,kr)$; the WKB correction used here is the leading term of that expansion and is accurate once $R_{\max}$ is well outside the barrier, which every preset below satisfies.

∫ Complex Numerov Shooting + Newton Root Search

Outward integration

Starting from $u(0)=0$ and the small-$r$ behavior $u(r)\sim r^{l+1}$, the standard Numerov recursion for $u''=g(r)u$, $g(r)=-2[E-V_{\rm eff}(r)]$, is applied with ordinary complex arithmetic (it is purely algebraic and does not require $g$ to be real):

$$u_{i+1} = \frac{2\left(1+\tfrac{5h^2}{12}g_i\right)u_i - \left(1-\tfrac{h^2}{12}g_{i-1}\right)u_{i-1}}{1-\tfrac{h^2}{12}g_{i+1}}$$

Complex Newton iteration

Starting from a guessed $E^{(0)}=E_{r,\rm guess}-i\Gamma_{\rm guess}/2$, refine using a one-sided complex-step derivative (valid since $F$ is holomorphic — a single directional derivative fixes the full complex slope):

$$E^{(k+1)} = E^{(k)} - \frac{F(E^{(k)})}{F'(E^{(k)})}, \qquad F'(E)\approx\frac{F(E+\delta)-F(E)}{\delta}$$

Iterated to convergence ($|F|<10^{-9}$ or 40 steps), with $\mathrm{Im}(E)$ clamped negative at every step (physical resonances decay, so the pole must sit in the lower half-plane).

Node counting

The radial quantum number $n$ is read off after convergence as the number of sign changes of $\mathrm{Re}[u(r)]$ for $r$ inside and just past the well — the direct analogue of counting nodes for a real bound state.

⚠ Newton's method here is local: it converges to whichever resonance pole is nearest the initial guess $(E_{r,0},\Gamma_0)$, or fails to converge at all for a poor guess (e.g. $l=0$, where no true resonance exists). Because $u(r)$ diverges by construction beyond the barrier, only the density near/inside the well is physically meaningful — the display truncates the plotted envelope accordingly.

Root-Finding: Newton vs. Muller

The complex Newton iteration used above is fast but needs a numerical derivative and can misstep in regions where $F(E)$ varies rapidly (exactly the ultra-narrow-resonance regime flagged above). Muller's method — the standard choice in production Gamow/R-matrix codes for this exact problem — instead fits a quadratic through the last three iterates $E_{k-2},E_{k-1},E_k$ and jumps to that parabola's root, needing no derivative and converging superlinearly (order ≈1.84) even when $F$ is poorly behaved:

$$q=\frac{E_k-E_{k-1}}{E_{k-1}-E_{k-2}},\quad a=qF_k-q(1+q)F_{k-1}+q^2F_{k-2}$$
$$b=(2q+1)F_k-(1+q)^2F_{k-1}+q^2F_{k-2},\quad c=(1+q)F_k$$
$$E_{k+1}=E_k-(E_k-E_{k-1})\frac{2c}{b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}\quad\text{(sign chosen to maximize }|{\rm denominator}|)$$

This module seeds Muller's method with three points bracketing the initial guess ($E_0,\ E_0+\delta,\ E_0-i\delta$) and lets both methods run to $|F|<10^{-9}$ or 60 iterations, whichever comes first — try both on a given preset and compare.

Radial Quantum Number $n_r$

The radial quantum number (number of internal nodes of $\mathrm{Re}[u(r)]$ between the origin and the edge of the well) is displayed as $n_r$ alongside the converged pole. Ground-state resonances (⁸Be, the Hoyle state) have $n_r=0$; raising the energy guess past a node typically locks onto the next radial excitation. This module does not automatically target a requested $n_r$ — it reports whichever $n_r$ the converged pole actually has, so mismatches between your target and the readout are a useful diagnostic that the guess needs adjusting.

Root-Finding: Contour Integral (Argument Principle)

Both Newton and Muller are local methods: each converges to whichever pole happens to be nearest the initial guess, or fails outright for a poor guess — exactly the failure mode flagged for the ultra-narrow α+α and Hoyle-state resonances. The argument principle instead counts and locates poles directly, with no dependence on how good the initial guess is, by exploiting that $F(E)$ is holomorphic wherever $u(r;E)$ is finite:

$$N = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C \frac{F'(E)}{F(E)}\,dE \;\in\;\mathbb{Z}$$

where $C$ is a small rectangular contour in the complex-$E$ plane, built automatically around the guessed $(E_{r,0},\Gamma_0)$. $N$ counts the number of roots of $F$ enclosed by $C$ (rounded to the nearest integer as a numerical sanity check — a non-integer result signals the contour didn't cleanly enclose a simple pole). A first moment integral then estimates the enclosed root's position directly, without iteration:

$$E_{\rm root} \approx \frac{1}{2\pi i N}\oint_C E\,\frac{F'(E)}{F(E)}\,dE$$

Both integrals are evaluated together as a single trapezoidal sweep around $C$, using the same one-sided complex-step derivative for $F'$ as the Newton method. Because this moment estimate is only exact for a single simple pole strictly inside $C$, it is treated as a high-quality starting point rather than a final answer: a handful of ordinary Newton steps then polish $E_{\rm root}$ to the usual $|F|<10^{-9}$ tolerance.

💡 The practical payoff is diagnostic as much as numerical: the reported root count $N$ tells you whether the contour actually bracketed a resonance before any refinement happens. $N\approx1$ means a genuine simple pole was found nearby; $N\approx0$ means the guess window missed it entirely (widen $E_{r,0}$/$\Gamma_0$ or recentre the guess); $N\geq2$ means the window is too wide and straddles more than one resonance, so the moment estimate is unreliable. This upfront count is exactly the information Newton and Muller cannot provide — they will silently converge (or silently fail) with no signal about which of these three situations occurred.

Continuity at each interface

Matching $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at $x=0$, $x=a$, $x=a+b$ gives 6 linear equations for the 6 unknown amplitudes $(R_{\rm amp},C,D,F,G,T_{\rm amp})$.

Transfer matrix per interface

$$\begin{pmatrix}A_L\\B_L\end{pmatrix}=M(k_L,k_R,x_0)\begin{pmatrix}A_R\\B_R\end{pmatrix},\quad M=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}\left(1+\frac{k_R}{k_L}\right)e^{i(k_R-k_L)x_0} & \left(1-\frac{k_R}{k_L}\right)e^{-i(k_R+k_L)x_0}\\[2mm] \left(1-\frac{k_R}{k_L}\right)e^{i(k_R+k_L)x_0} & \left(1+\frac{k_R}{k_L}\right)e^{-i(k_R-k_L)x_0}\end{pmatrix}$$

Chaining the three interface matrices, $M_{\rm tot}=M(k_0,k_1,0)\,M(k_1,k_2,a)\,M(k_2,k_0,a+b)$, relates region-I amplitudes directly to region-IV's, skipping the intermediate linear solve entirely.

Extracting R and T
$$\begin{pmatrix}1\\R_{\rm amp}\end{pmatrix}=M_{\rm tot}\begin{pmatrix}T_{\rm amp}\\0\end{pmatrix}\ \Rightarrow\ T_{\rm amp}=\frac{1}{(M_{\rm tot})_{11}},\quad R_{\rm amp}=\frac{(M_{\rm tot})_{21}}{(M_{\rm tot})_{11}}$$

with $R=|R_{\rm amp}|^2$, $T=|T_{\rm amp}|^2$ (no extra $k$-ratio factor needed since region I and IV share the same $k_0$).

Why R+T=1 is guaranteed

The matching conditions used to build each $M$ are exactly the statement that the probability current $j\propto\mathrm{Im}[\psi^*\psi']$ is continuous across every interface. Since $k_0$ is real and identical in regions I and IV, current conservation there reduces precisely to $R+T=1$ — true for any real $E$, including deep tunneling ($k_2$ imaginary), with no separate case needed since the complex arithmetic handles both regimes uniformly.

The scattering (standing-wave) boundary condition

Tab 5 demanded a purely outgoing wave at $R_2$ and let $E$ go complex. Here $E$ is real and physical, so instead the exterior solution is the general free-particle standing wave, shifted in phase by the interaction:

$$u(r) \xrightarrow{r>R_2} D\,\sin(k_3r+\delta_0), \qquad r\ge R_2$$

$\delta_0$ is exactly zero for a free particle ($V_0=V_b=0$); any nonzero $\delta_0$ is entirely due to scattering off the well+barrier.

Matching condition — solving directly for $\delta_0$

Continuity of the logarithmic derivative at $R_2$ requires $L_2=k_3\cot(k_3R_2+\delta_0)$. Since $\cot(\theta)=L_2/k_3$ is equivalent to $\tan(\theta)=k_3/L_2$, this inverts directly to:

$$\delta_0(E) = \operatorname{atan2}(k_3,\,L_2) - k_3 R_2 \pmod{\pi}$$

swept continuously in $E$ and unwrapped by multiples of $\pi$ (since $\cot$ is $\pi$-periodic) to give a smooth $\delta_0(E)$ curve rather than one that jumps every time it crosses a branch cut.

The S-matrix

Writing the standing wave as a sum of incoming and outgoing spherical waves, $\sin(k_3r+\delta_0)\propto e^{-i(k_3r+\delta_0)}-e^{i(k_3r+\delta_0)}$ up to normalization, shows the outgoing wave picks up exactly the phase $e^{2i\delta_0}$ relative to the incoming one. This ratio defines the S-matrix:

$$S_0(E) \equiv e^{2i\delta_0(E)}$$

Because $\delta_0$ is real for a real (non-absorptive) potential, $S_0$ is confined to the unit circle in the complex plane for every $E$ — the geometric statement of probability/flux conservation.

Cross section from the S-matrix

The s-wave partial-wave elastic cross section follows directly from either $\delta_0$ or $S_0$ — two equivalent forms of the same result:

$$\sigma_0(E)=\frac{4\pi}{k_3^2}\sin^2\delta_0 \;=\; \frac{\pi}{k_3^2}\,\big|1-S_0\big|^2$$

The maximum possible s-wave cross section, $4\pi/k_3^2$, occurs exactly when $\delta_0=\pi/2$ — the unitarity limit, reached precisely at the center of a narrow resonance.

Reusing the interface matrix from Tab 8

Every boundary in this module — well↔barrier, barrier↔well, or lead↔lattice — is matched by the same flux-conserving $2\times2$ complex matrix $M(k_L,k_R,x_0)$ first introduced in Tab 6 and reused in Tab 8, which propagates the pair of plane-wave amplitudes (rightward, leftward) across a single interface at position $x_0$ where the local wavenumber changes from $k_L$ to $k_R$:

$$M(k_L,k_R,x_0)=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}\left(1+\frac{k_R}{k_L}\right)e^{i(k_R-k_L)x_0} & \left(1-\frac{k_R}{k_L}\right)e^{-i(k_R+k_L)x_0}\\[2mm] \left(1-\frac{k_R}{k_L}\right)e^{i(k_R+k_L)x_0} & \left(1+\frac{k_R}{k_L}\right)e^{-i(k_R-k_L)x_0}\end{pmatrix}$$

Because this matrix comes directly from continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$ — the same current-conservation statement used in every earlier tab — it satisfies $\det M=1$ for any real $E$, a fact used explicitly in Step 4 below.

Single-cell transfer matrix $M_{\rm cell}(E)$

Chaining two interface matrices — well→barrier at $x=a$, then barrier→(next) well at $x=a+b=L$ — gives the matrix that propagates the amplitude pair across exactly one period:

$$M_{\rm cell}(E) = M(k_b,k_w,\,a{+}b)\;M(k_w,k_b,\,a)$$

$M_{\rm cell}$ depends only on $E$ and the four cell parameters $(V_w,a,V_b,b)$ — not on where in the lattice you are, since every cell is identical. This is the entire content of Bloch periodicity at the level of the transfer matrix: one matrix describes every period.

Bloch's theorem as an eigenvalue problem

A Bloch eigenstate satisfies $\psi(x+L)=e^{iqL}\psi(x)$ for some real $q$ — physically, the wave looks identical from cell to cell up to an overall phase. In transfer-matrix language this means the amplitude pair $\mathbf{v}$ in one cell must be an eigenvector of $M_{\rm cell}$:

$$M_{\rm cell}\,\mathbf v = \lambda\,\mathbf v,\qquad \lambda \equiv e^{iqL}$$

Since $\det M_{\rm cell}=1$ (product of unit-determinant matrices), the two eigenvalues $\lambda_{1,2}$ of the $2\times2$ matrix multiply to 1: $\lambda_1\lambda_2=1$. Combined with the characteristic polynomial $\lambda^2-{\rm Tr}(M_{\rm cell})\lambda+\det M_{\rm cell}=0$, this gives:

$$\lambda^2 - {\rm Tr}(M_{\rm cell})\,\lambda + 1 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda + \lambda^{-1} = {\rm Tr}(M_{\rm cell})$$

Writing $\lambda=e^{iqL}$ makes $\lambda+\lambda^{-1}=2\cos(qL)$ automatically, so the eigenvalue condition collapses to the single scalar equation used throughout this tab:

$$\boxed{\cos(qL) = \tfrac12\,{\rm Tr}\big(M_{\rm cell}(E)\big)}$$
Why the trace controls allowed vs. forbidden

The trace of $M_{\rm cell}$ is always real for real $E$ (it is built from real $k$'s or purely-imaginary $k$'s in physically self-consistent combinations). The equation $\cos(qL)=\tfrac12{\rm Tr}(M_{\rm cell})$ only has a solution with real $q$ when the right-hand side lies in $[-1,1]$, since $\cos$ of a real number is bounded that way:

  • $|\tfrac12{\rm Tr}(M_{\rm cell})|\le 1$: $q$ is real. A genuine propagating Bloch wave exists at this energy — it lies in an allowed band.
  • $|\tfrac12{\rm Tr}(M_{\rm cell})|>1$: the only solutions have $q=q_r+iq_i$ with $q_i\neq0$, so $e^{iqL}$ decays or grows exponentially from cell to cell rather than merely oscillating — no bounded propagating state exists in an infinite lattice. This energy lies in a forbidden gap.

Sweeping $E$ and plotting $\tfrac12{\rm Tr}(M_{\rm cell}(E))$ therefore maps out the entire band structure without ever solving for $q$ explicitly — band edges are exactly the energies where the curve crosses $\pm1$.

Finite chain: the same interface matrix, looped $N$ times

A physically realizable sample has $N$ cells, not infinitely many, and is attached to field-free leads ($V=0$) on both sides. The total transfer matrix is built by chaining interface matrices left to right: lead→well, then $(N-1)$ repeats of well→barrier→well, then a final barrier→lead:

$$M_{\rm tot}= M(k_b,k_0,\,NL)\left[\prod_{n=N-1}^{1} M(k_w,k_b,\,nL{+}a)\,M(k_b,k_w,\,nL)\right] M(k_w,k_b,\,a)\,M(k_0,k_w,\,0)$$

with $k_0=\sqrt{AE}$ the free-lead wavenumber. This is structurally identical to Tab 8's double-barrier chain — the only difference is that the loop runs $N$ times instead of a fixed two. Reflection and transmission amplitudes follow exactly as in Tab 8, from the bottom row of $M_{\rm tot}$, and $R+T=1$ is guaranteed by the same current-conservation argument used since Tab 6.

∂ Shooting for a Doubly-Decaying Solution

Parameter Glossary for This Derivation

Why $E<0$ forces exponential (not oscillatory) leads

Outside the lattice, $V=0$, so $k_0=\sqrt{AE}$. For $E>0$ this is real and the lead solution oscillates — a scattering state, exactly Tab 9's regime. For $E<0$, $k_0$ is purely imaginary, $k_0=i\kappa_0$ with $\kappa_0=\sqrt{-AE}>0$, and the two basis functions $e^{ik_0x}=e^{-\kappa_0x}$, $e^{-ik_0x}=e^{\kappa_0x}$ are genuine real exponentials — one decays as $x\to+\infty$, the other decays as $x\to-\infty$. A bound state must decay on both sides, which is only possible by keeping exactly one of these two terms in each lead.

Left boundary condition

In the left lead, write $u(x)=A_Le^{ik_0x}+B_Le^{-ik_0x}=A_Le^{-\kappa_0x}+B_Le^{\kappa_0x}$. As $x\to-\infty$, $e^{-\kappa_0x}\to\infty$ while $e^{\kappa_0x}\to0$. Decay therefore requires:

$$A_L=0,\qquad u(x\to-\infty)\big|_{\rm left\ lead}=B_L\,e^{\kappa_0x}\to0$$

This fixes the starting amplitude pair uniquely up to overall normalization: $(A_L,B_L)=(0,1)$.

Forward propagation through the lattice

Starting from $(0,1)$ in the left lead, the same interface matrix $M(k_u,k_v,x_0)$ used throughout Tabs 6–9 is applied once per interface, in sequence: lead→well$_1$, well$_1$→barrier$_1$, barrier$_1$→well$_2$, …, well$_N$→barrier$_N$, barrier$_N$→right lead — exactly the $2N{+}1$ interfaces of Tab 9's finite chain, just with a different starting vector:

$$\begin{pmatrix}A_R\\B_R\end{pmatrix} = M(k_b,k_0,NL)\Big[\textstyle\prod\Big] M(k_0,k_w,0)\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$$

Because every interface matrix is built from ordinary continuity of $u,u'$ (Tab 6, Step 3), this is exact for any complex $k$'s — no distinction needs to be coded between classically-allowed ($k$ real) and classically-forbidden ($k$ imaginary) cells; the matrix algebra handles both automatically, exactly as in Tabs 6–9.

Right boundary condition — the matching function

In the right lead, $u(x)=A_Re^{ik_0x}+B_Re^{-ik_0x}=A_Re^{-\kappa_0x}+B_Re^{\kappa_0x}$. As $x\to+\infty$, $e^{-\kappa_0x}\to0$ but $e^{\kappa_0x}\to\infty$. Decay on the right therefore requires the coefficient of the growing term to vanish:

$$F(E)\;\equiv\;B_R(E)\;=\;0$$

$F(E)$ is obtained after propagating forward — no iteration or re-integration is needed to evaluate $F$ at a single trial $E$, only the fixed sequence of matrix multiplications in Step 3. This converts the bound-state problem into a one-dimensional real root search, simpler than the complex Newton/Muller searches of Tabs 4–5 because $E$ is confined to the real axis by construction (the physical potential is real, so a genuinely bound solution's energy must be real — there is no separate imaginary part to solve for, unlike the complex Gamow poles of Tab 4).

Node theorem and level ordering

As in every earlier tab, the number of internal sign changes of $\mathrm{Re}[u(x)]$ across the lattice fixes the node count $n$, and — exactly as for a single finite well — the levels are strictly ordered by node count: the ground state ($n=0$) has the most negative energy, and each successive level has one more node and a higher (less negative) energy, up to the top of the well depth $V_w$ where the spectrum runs out of bound states entirely.

∫ Scanning, Bisecting, and Reconstructing Each Bound State

Step-by-step algorithm

Search window

Bound states can only occur in $E\in(V_{w}-\Delta,\,0)$: below the lead threshold $0$ (or no exponential decay is possible), and not much below the well floor $V_w$ (deeper than that, no solution exists either). $\Delta$ is a small user-controlled search margin so shallow chains with $V_w$ close to $0$ are still fully covered.

Coarse scan for sign changes

$F(E)=B_R(E)$ (real part) is evaluated on a dense uniform grid across the search window. Because $F$ is continuous in $E$ and each bound state corresponds to an isolated zero crossing, every sign change of $\mathrm{Re}[F]$ between adjacent grid points brackets exactly one bound-state energy (for a fine enough grid).

Bisection refinement

Each bracketed interval is refined by ordinary bisection on $\mathrm{Re}[F(E)]$ to a tolerance of $10^{-8}$ eV or 60 iterations, whichever comes first — simpler and more robust here than Newton, since $F$ can vary rapidly near a level and bisection needs no derivative and cannot diverge.

Wavefunction reconstruction

Once $E_n$ is converged, the same forward propagation of Derivation Step 3 is repeated once more, this time recording the amplitude pair at every interface, so that $u(x)$ can be evaluated in closed form region by region across the whole plotted domain (leads included, out to a few decay lengths $1/\kappa_0$ on each side).

Normalization and display

$u(x)$ is normalized numerically, $\int|u|^2dx=1$, by trapezoidal integration over the plotted domain (the exponential lead tails contribute a small, finite, correctly-decaying share of the norm). $\mathrm{Re}[u(x)]$ — which should carry essentially all of $u$'s magnitude once the correct real energy is found, since $F(E_n)=0$ removes the only source of complex character — is what's plotted as the physical bound-state wavefunction, with $\mathrm{Im}[u(x)]$ displayed alongside purely as a numerical-noise check.

∂ From the WKB Connection Formula to the Gamow Factor
WKB tunneling probability

For a slowly-varying barrier, the WKB approximation gives the transmission probability through the classically forbidden region $R\le r\le b$ (where $V(r)>E$) as:

$$T \approx \exp\!\left[-2\int_R^b \kappa(r)\,dr\right], \qquad \kappa(r)=\frac{\sqrt{2\mu\big(V(r)-E\big)}}{\hbar},\quad V(r)=\frac{Z_1Z_2e^2}{r}$$
Closed-form Gamow factor

Substituting $r=b\sin^2\theta$ turns the integral into an elementary trig integral (the standard Gamow 1928 result):

$$G\equiv2\int_R^b\kappa\,dr = \frac{2b}{\hbar c}\sqrt{2\mu c^2E}\;\Big[\arccos(\sqrt x)-\sqrt{x(1-x)}\Big],\qquad x\equiv R/b$$

with $T=e^{-G}$. All quantities are evaluated exactly as in Tabs 4–6's reduced-mass, MeV/fm convention: $\hbar c=197.3269804$ MeV·fm, $e^2=1.4399764$ MeV·fm.

Thick-barrier limit — the Geiger–Nuttall law

When $R\ll b$ (true for essentially every real α-emitter), $\arccos\sqrt x-\sqrt{x(1-x)}\to\tfrac\pi2-2\sqrt x+O(x^{3/2})$, so:

$$G\approx \frac{\pi Z_1Z_2e^2}{\hbar c}\sqrt{\frac{2\mu c^2}{E}} \;-\; \frac{4}{\hbar c}\sqrt{2\mu c^2 Z_1Z_2e^2R}$$

The first term dominates and is $\propto Z_1Z_2/\sqrt E$: since $\log_{10}T=-G/\ln10$, this is the empirical Geiger–Nuttall law, derived rather than fitted.

Assault frequency and decay constant

Modeling the α as bouncing non-relativistically inside the well at speed $v=c\sqrt{2E/\mu c^2}$ (using $E$ itself as a rough stand-in for the internal kinetic energy — the traditional Gamow approximation), it strikes the barrier wall with frequency $f=v/2R$. Each attempt succeeds with probability $T$:

$$\lambda = f\,T, \qquad t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{\lambda}, \qquad \Gamma_{\rm WKB}=\hbar\lambda$$

using $\hbar=6.582119569\times10^{-22}$ MeV·s, exactly the constant already used for $\tau=1/\Gamma$ in Tabs 4–5 — so $\Gamma_{\rm WKB}$ can be compared directly to their converged Gamow–Siegert widths.

∂ The WKB Wavefunction, Connection Formulas, and Quantization
WKB ansatz

Writing $u(r)=e^{iS(r)/\hbar}$ and expanding $S=S_0+\hbar S_1+\dots$ in the radial equation, the leading order gives the eikonal equation $S_0'=\pm p(r)$, $p(r)=\sqrt{2\mu(E-V_{\rm eff})}$, and the next order fixes the amplitude — the classic semiclassical wavefunction:

$$u(r)\approx\frac{C}{\sqrt{p(r)}}\exp\!\left[\pm\frac{i}{\hbar}\int p(r')\,dr'\right]\quad\text{(allowed region, }E>V_{\rm eff}\text{)}$$
$$u(r)\approx\frac{D}{\sqrt{|p(r)|}}\exp\!\left[\mp\frac1\hbar\int|p(r')|\,dr'\right]\quad\text{(forbidden region, }E

The $1/\sqrt{p}$ prefactor is exactly what keeps the local probability current constant — it's why the schematic wavefunction in this tab's plot grows near a turning point (where $p\to0$) before the connection formula takes over.

Failure at the turning points, and the connection formula

Both forms above diverge exactly at $p(r)=0$ — the turning point — since the WKB expansion parameter $\hbar S_0''/S_0'^2$ blows up there. The standard fix (Kramers 1926, via the Airy-function solution of the linearized potential near the turning point) patches an oscillatory solution smoothly onto a decaying one, with a fixed $\pi/4$ phase offset:

$$\frac{2}{\sqrt{p(r)}}\cos\!\left[\frac1\hbar\int_r^{r_a}p\,dr'-\frac\pi4\right] \;\longleftrightarrow\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{|p(r)|}}\exp\!\left[-\frac1\hbar\int_{r_a}^{r}|p|\,dr'\right]$$

This connection formula is what makes the naive divergence at $r_a,r_b$ in this tab's schematic plot unphysical — a full treatment (Airy functions) smooths it into a finite peak, which the plot approximates by capping the displayed amplitude.

Gamow's tunneling formula

Applying the connection formula at both $r_a$ and $r_b$ and matching an incoming/outgoing wave outside the barrier to a decaying solution inside gives the transmission probability in the thick-barrier limit — Gamow's original 1928 result, generalized here to an arbitrary $V_{\rm eff}(r)$ rather than a pure Coulomb tail:

$$T\approx\exp\!\left[-2\int_{r_a}^{r_b}\kappa(r)\,dr\right]\equiv e^{-G},\qquad \kappa(r)=\frac{\sqrt{2\mu\big(V_{\rm eff}(r)-E\big)}}{\hbar}$$
Assault frequency and decay width

The classical period of motion inside the well $[r_{\rm in},r_a]$ gives the "assault frequency" — exactly Tab 11's picture, but now with $r_{\rm in},r_a$ found from the real potential rather than assumed:

$$\tau_{\rm cl}=2\int_{r_{\rm in}}^{r_a}\frac{dr}{v(r)},\quad v(r)=\frac{\hbar k(r)}{\mu},\qquad f=\frac1{\tau_{\rm cl}},\qquad \Gamma_{\rm WKB}=\hbar f\,T$$
Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization

Demanding the connection-formula phase be single-valued around one full well oscillation (there and back) gives the semiclassical quantization rule — historically the bridge from the old quantum theory to full quantum mechanics, and the $\hbar\to0$ limit of an exact bound-state condition:

$$\oint p\,dr = \left(n+\frac12\right)h \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Phi(E_n)\equiv\int_{r_{\rm in}}^{r_a}k(r)\,dr=\left(n+\frac12\right)\pi,\quad n=0,1,2,\dots$$

The two $+\tfrac12$'s (one per turning point) come directly from the $-\pi/4$ phase in the connection formula of Step 2, applied at both ends of the well. This is the semiclassical analogue of Tab 10's exact node-counting bound-state search — same physical content, cruder method.

⚡ The Equation Behind Every Coulomb Potential in This Module

Every Coulomb term $V(r)=Z_1Z_2e^2/r$ used from Tab 4 onward was introduced as a given — a formula plugged into the radial equation without derivation. It comes from a single statement of Gauss's law in differential form. Combined with $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi$, Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$ becomes the Poisson equation:

$$\nabla^2\phi = -\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}$$

In charge-free regions ($\rho=0$) this reduces to the Laplace equation $\nabla^2\phi=0$. Unlike every other equation in this module, Poisson/Laplace has no time derivative at all — it is a genuinely static, elliptic boundary-value problem, and the same equation (with $\rho/\epsilon_0\to4\pi G\rho_{\rm mass}$) governs Newtonian gravitational potentials, which is why this tab's solver applies equally to charge distributions and mass distributions.

∂ Multipole Expansion and the Discretized Relaxation Equation
From Gauss's law to Poisson's equation

Gauss's law in differential form, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$, combined with the electrostatic field being conservative ($\nabla\times\mathbf E=0 \Rightarrow \mathbf E=-\nabla\phi$), gives directly:

$$\nabla\cdot(-\nabla\phi)=\rho/\epsilon_0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \nabla^2\phi=-\rho/\epsilon_0$$

This is the single equation underlying every $V_{\rm coulomb}(r)$ used without derivation since Tab 4.

Taylor expansion of the point-source Green's function

For a source point $\mathbf r'$ and field point $\mathbf r$, expanding $1/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|$ in powers of $r'/r$ (the standard multipole Taylor expansion, valid for $r\gg r'$) and integrating over a charge distribution gives the Cartesian multipole series:

$$\phi(\mathbf r) = k\left[\frac{Q}{r} + \frac{\mathbf p\cdot\hat r}{r^2} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\frac{Q_{ij}\hat r_i\hat r_j}{r^3} + \cdots\right], \quad k\equiv\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}$$

with monopole $Q=\sum_a q_a$, dipole $\mathbf p=\sum_a q_a\mathbf r_a$, and traceless quadrupole tensor $Q_{ij}=\sum_a q_a(3x_{a,i}x_{a,j}-r_a^2\delta_{ij})$.

On-axis simplification (this tab's charge configurations)

For charges placed along the $z$-axis and a field point also on that axis ($\hat r=\hat z$), $r_a=|z_a|$ so $Q_{zz}=\sum_a q_a(3z_a^2-z_a^2)=2\sum_a q_a z_a^2$, and the series collapses to a simple sum evaluated purely along one coordinate:

$$\phi(z) \approx k\left[\frac{Q}{z} + \frac{p_z}{z^2} + \frac{\sum_a q_a z_a^2}{z^3}\right], \qquad Q=\sum_a q_a,\;\; p_z=\sum_a q_a z_a$$

Each successive term is suppressed by an additional power of $(z_a/z)$, which is exactly why a dipole ($Q=0$) falls off as $1/z^2$ and a linear quadrupole ($Q=p_z=0$) falls off as $1/z^3$ — the multipole order sets the leading power-law decay.

Discretizing the Laplacian on a grid

Approximating $\nabla^2\phi$ at grid point $(i,j)$ by the standard 5-point finite-difference stencil (the same discretization strategy used for $\partial^2/\partial x^2$ in Tab 1's Visscher stepper, just extended to two dimensions with no time index) and solving for $\phi_{i,j}$ gives the update every relaxation method is built on:

$$\phi_{i,j} = \frac14\left[\phi_{i+1,j}+\phi_{i-1,j}+\phi_{i,j+1}+\phi_{i,j-1} + h^2\rho_{i,j}/\epsilon_0\right]$$

This says $\phi$ at any point equals the average of its four neighbors plus a local source correction — precisely the discrete statement that harmonic functions ($\rho=0$) have no local extrema, the numerical fingerprint of the maximum principle for Laplace's equation.

Jacobi → Gauss-Seidel → Successive Over-Relaxation

Applying Step 4's formula at every non-fixed grid point using only old values (Jacobi) converges, but slowly. Using already-updated neighbor values within the same sweep (Gauss-Seidel) roughly doubles the convergence rate. SOR pushes further by overshooting each Gauss-Seidel update by a factor $\omega$:

$$\phi_{i,j}^{\rm new} = \phi_{i,j}^{\rm old} + \omega\left(\phi_{i,j}^{\rm GS} - \phi_{i,j}^{\rm old}\right), \qquad 1<\omega<2$$

For a square grid of $N\times N$ interior points, the theoretically optimal choice — the value that minimizes the number of sweeps needed — has a closed form:

$$\omega_{\rm opt} = \frac{2}{1+\sin(\pi/N)}$$

This tab lets $\omega$ be set by hand or locked to $\omega_{\rm opt}$, and Plot 2 in the Simulation section compares Jacobi-equivalent ($\omega=1$), a user-chosen $\omega$, and $\omega_{\rm opt}$ side by side — directly analogous to Tab 12's WKB-vs-exact convergence comparison, but for an iterative linear solver instead of a physical approximation.

Ψ⁻ The Naive Relativistic Generalization of Schrödinger

Every equation in Tabs 1–13 is built on the non-relativistic energy relation $E=p^2/2m$. The obvious relativistic replacement is Einstein's $E^2=p^2c^2+m^2c^4$. Substituting the same operators used to derive the Schrödinger equation, $E\to i\hbar\partial_t$, $\mathbf p\to-i\hbar\nabla$, and applying them to a scalar field $\phi$ (natural units $\hbar=c=1$ throughout this tab) gives the Klein–Gordon equation:

$$\left(\Box+m^2\right)\phi=0,\qquad \Box\equiv\partial_t^2-\nabla^2,\qquad\text{(1+1D: } \partial_t^2\phi-\partial_x^2\phi+m^2\phi=0\text{)}$$

This looks like a small step from Schrödinger, but it is second order in time rather than first order — and that single change breaks the probabilistic interpretation that made $|\psi|^2$ meaningful. This tab builds the equation, derives its actual conserved current, and shows exactly where and why that current fails to be positive-definite — the historical problem that motivated Dirac's search for a first-order relativistic equation (bridged to in a later tab).

∂ From Plane Waves to a Non-Positive Current
Two energy branches per momentum

Substituting the plane-wave ansatz $\phi=e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ into $(\Box+m^2)\phi=0$ gives $\omega^2=k^2+m^2$, so $\omega=\pm E_k$ with $E_k=+\sqrt{k^2+m^2}$. Both signs are equally valid solutions — unlike Schrödinger's $E=k^2/2m$, which has only one branch, relativistic energy-squared unavoidably admits a negative-energy partner for every positive-energy mode.

The conserved current

Multiplying the KG equation by $\phi^*$, subtracting the complex conjugate equation multiplied by $\phi$, and integrating by parts gives a genuine continuity equation $\partial_t\rho+\partial_x j=0$ with:

$$\rho(x,t)=i\left(\phi^*\dot\phi-\phi\dot\phi^*\right),\qquad j(x,t)=-i\left(\phi^*\phi'-\phi\phi'^*\right)$$

This is the unique bilinear, first-order-in-time-derivative density consistent with the second-order field equation — there is no way to build a positive-definite conserved density using only $\phi$ and $\dot\phi$ linearly, which is precisely why $|\phi|^2$ (always $\ge0$) is not conserved and is not the right object.

Why $\rho$ can go negative

For a single plane wave $\phi=Ce^{i(kx\mp E_kt)}$, direct substitution gives $\rho=\pm2E_k|C|^2$ — positive for the $E_k$ branch, negative for the $-E_k$ branch. A wave packet built purely from one branch has $\rho\ge0$ (Step 4). But once the two branches are superposed — which happens automatically whenever a packet is localized to within about a Compton wavelength $1/m$, or whenever admixture $\alpha\neq0$ is introduced by hand — cross terms between the branches oscillate at frequency $2E_k$ (the Zitterbewegung frequency) and can drive $\rho$ locally negative, even though $Q=\int\rho\,dx$ stays fixed.

Building a definite-charge packet

A packet built only from the $+E_k$ branch, $\phi(x,t)=\int dk\,A(k)\,e^{i(k(x-x_0)-E_kt)}$ with real Gaussian weight $A(k)$, has a manifestly positive-definite $\rho$ for all $t$ — this is exactly what "particle, no antiparticle content" means at the level of the field. Mixing in an amount $\alpha$ of the $-E_k$ branch, $\phi\to\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}\,\phi_++\alpha\,\phi_-$, is the controlled way to dial in exactly the interference that makes $\rho$ misbehave.

Minimal coupling and the Klein step

Coupling to an external scalar potential via $i\partial_t\to i\partial_t-V(x)$ (the same minimal-coupling prescription used for a charged particle in an electrostatic potential) turns the free equation into $\big[(i\partial_t-V)^2+\partial_x^2-m^2\big]\phi=0$. For a step $V(x)=V_0\,\theta(x)$, plane-wave matching in each region uses the local relation $(E-V)^2=k^2+m^2$, so the local wavenumber is:

$$k_1=\sqrt{E^2-m^2}\ \ (x<0), \qquad k_2=\sqrt{(E-V_0)^2-m^2}\ \ (x>0)$$

Matching $\phi,\phi'$ at $x=0$ gives the same-looking reflection/transmission formulas as Tab 6's non-relativistic step (Solution section) — but $k_2^2>0$ reopens for two separate reasons: the ordinary case $E-V_0>m$, and the anomalous case $E-V_0<-m$ (i.e. $V_0>E+m$), where the "kinetic energy" $E-V_0$ has flipped past $-m$ into the lower continuum. That second case is the Klein paradox.

∫ Two Numerical Modes: Packet Interference and Step Scattering

Mode A — analytic wave-packet synthesis

Because every Fourier component of the free Klein–Gordon equation is an exact plane wave, $\phi(x,t)$ and $\dot\phi(x,t)$ are evaluated directly by spectral synthesis — summing a discrete grid of $k$-modes weighted by a Gaussian $A(k)\propto e^{-(k-k_0)^2\sigma^2}$ — rather than by finite-difference time-stepping, avoiding any numerical dispersion error:

$$\phi(x,t)=\sum_k A(k)\Big[\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}\;e^{i(k(x-x_0)-E_kt)}+\alpha\;e^{i(k(x-x_0)+E_kt)}\Big]\Delta k$$

$\dot\phi$ follows by differentiating each term analytically (multiply by $\mp iE_k$), and $\rho(x,t)=2\big(\mathrm{Im}[\phi]\,\mathrm{Re}[\dot\phi]-\mathrm{Re}[\phi]\,\mathrm{Im}[\dot\phi]\big)$ is then evaluated pointwise — exact to the discretization of the $k$-sum, with no time-stepping instability of the kind Tab 1 had to guard against with the Visscher scheme.

Mode B — Klein-paradox step scattering

$k_1,k_2$ are computed from the real-energy dispersion relations of Derivation Step 5, using the same generic complex square root as Tabs 4–9 (which automatically returns an imaginary result for tunneling, $k_2^2<0$). In the Klein regime ($E-V_0<-m$), the sign of $\mathrm{Re}(k_2)$ is flipped by hand to select the branch with rightward-directed group velocity in region II — the correct physical requirement for a transmitted wave, and the step that exposes the anomaly:

$$r=\frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2},\qquad t=\frac{2k_1}{k_1+k_2},\qquad R=|r|^2,\qquad T=\frac{\mathrm{Re}(k_2)}{k_1}|t|^2$$

In the normal and tunneling regimes this reproduces $R+T=1$ exactly, just as in Tab 6. In the Klein regime, $T$ comes out negative and $R>1$; the correct conservation law becomes $R-T=1$ — read as: the reflected current exceeds the incident current because the step itself supplies additional outgoing particles (interpreted in QFT as spontaneous pair creation at the step), not because probability is being destroyed or created for free.

⚠ Mode B is a single-particle stationary-scattering calculation, exactly as far as the original Klein/Sauter treatment goes — it correctly reproduces the paradox but cannot resolve it. The resolution (pair production rate, vacuum decay) requires quantizing $\phi$ as a field, which is outside the scope of this tab.

Ψ⁺ Factoring the Klein–Gordon Equation

Tab 14 showed that squaring the relativistic energy relation gives a second-order equation with no positive-definite density. Dirac's 1928 fix was to look for a genuinely first-order equation whose square reproduces Klein–Gordon — which forces the coefficients to become matrices, not numbers. In 1+1 dimensions the minimal representation uses 2×2 matrices, and the field $\psi$ becomes a two-component spinor:

$$i\partial_t\psi = \big(-i\alpha\,\partial_x+\beta m\big)\psi,\qquad \alpha=\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ \beta=\sigma_z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$$

Squaring this operator reproduces $(\Box+m^2)\phi=0$ component-wise, exactly as required — but the price of first-order-in-time is that $\psi$ now carries an internal two-valued index (spin, in 3+1D; a formal doubling here in 1+1D) rather than being a single complex number.

∂ Plane-Wave Spinors and the Two Energy Branches
Eigenvalue problem at fixed momentum

For $\psi=u(k)e^{i(kx-Et)}$, the Dirac equation becomes $H(k)u=Eu$ with $H(k)=k\alpha+m\beta=\begin{pmatrix}m&k\\k&-m\end{pmatrix}$, giving $E=\pm E_k$, $E_k=\sqrt{k^2+m^2}$ — the same two branches as Klein–Gordon, now attached to explicit spinors rather than to a single scalar mode.

Normalized spinors
$$u_+(k)=\mathcal N\begin{pmatrix}k\\E_k-m\end{pmatrix},\qquad u_-(k)=\mathcal N\begin{pmatrix}k\\-E_k-m\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathcal N=\big[k^2+(E_k\mp m)^2\big]^{-1/2}$$

As $k\to0$: $u_+\to(1,0)^T$ (pure upper component — the non-relativistic particle state), confirming the Tab 1 limit quoted in the regime table.

Manifestly positive density
$$\rho=\psi^\dagger\psi=|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2\ \ge 0\ \text{always},\qquad j=\psi^\dagger\alpha\psi=2\,\mathrm{Re}(\psi_1^*\psi_2)$$

Continuity $\partial_t\rho+\partial_xj=0$ follows directly from the first-order equation and its conjugate — no bilinear time-derivative construction is needed, unlike Tab 14's $\rho$.

Zitterbewegung from branch mixing

A packet built from $\sqrt{1-\alpha_{\rm mix}^2}\,u_++\alpha_{\rm mix}\,u_-$ at each $k$ has $\langle x\rangle(t)$ containing a term oscillating at $2E_k\approx2m$ (for $k\ll m$) — Zitterbewegung — while $\rho(x,t)\ge0$ is preserved at every instant, since it is built from squared spinor components regardless of the mixing.

Step potential and the sign-flipped Klein paradox

Minimal coupling $i\partial_t\to i\partial_t-V(x)\theta(x)$ gives region-II wavenumber $k_2=\sqrt{(E-V_0)^2-m^2}$, exactly as in Tab 14. Matching both spinor components at $x=0$ (rather than $\phi,\phi'$) and computing $R,T$ from the conserved current $j=2\mathrm{Re}(\psi_1^*\psi_2)$ gives, for $V_0>E+m$: $T>1$ and $R<0$, with $T-R=1$ — the mirror image of Tab 14's $R-T=1$.

∫ Spectral Synthesis and Spinor Matching

Mode A — wave packet

As in Tab 14, $\psi(x,t)$ is built by exact spectral synthesis over a $k$-grid — summing $u_\pm(k)$ plane waves with a Gaussian weight — rather than by time-stepping, so there is no finite-difference dispersion error. $\rho(x,t)$ and $\langle x\rangle(t)=\int x\rho\,dx/\int\rho\,dx$ are then evaluated directly.

The incident, reflected, and transmitted spinors are $u_+(k_1)$, $u_+(-k_1)$, $u_+(k_2)$ respectively (with $k_2$ possibly complex/sign-flipped in the Klein regime, exactly as in Tab 14). ∂ Separating the Dirac Equation in a Coulomb Field
Radial spinor ansatz

Central symmetry lets the 4-spinor separate into radial functions times spin-angular harmonics, with a single new quantum number $\kappa=\mp(j+\tfrac12)$ for $j=l\pm\tfrac12$ replacing $l$ as the operator that commutes with $H$:

$$\psi = \frac{1}{r}\begin{pmatrix}G(r)\,\Omega_{\kappa m}\\ iF(r)\,\Omega_{-\kappa m}\end{pmatrix}$$
Coupled first-order radial equations

Substituting into the Dirac equation with $V(r)=-Z\alpha\hbar c/r$ gives two coupled first-order ODEs for $G,F$ — no second derivatives anywhere, unlike the Schrödinger radial equation:

$$\frac{dG}{dr} = -\frac{\kappa}{r}G + \frac{E-V(r)+mc^2}{\hbar c}F,\qquad \frac{dF}{dr} = \frac{\kappa}{r}F - \frac{E-V(r)-mc^2}{\hbar c}G$$
Small-r behavior

Near $r=0$ the Coulomb term dominates both equations, giving power-law solutions $G,F\propto r^\gamma$ with $\gamma=\sqrt{\kappa^2-(Z\alpha)^2}$ — replacing the integer $r^{l+1}$ behavior of the non-relativistic radial equation with a non-integer power, and fixing the small-$r$ ratio used to seed the numerical integration below:

$$\frac{F}{G}\bigg|_{r\to0} = \frac{\gamma+\kappa}{Z\alpha}$$
Exact (Sommerfeld) energy formula

Demanding normalizability at $r\to\infty$ quantizes the energy in closed form — the relativistic generalization of the Bohr formula, exact to all orders in $Z\alpha$:

$$E_{n\kappa} = mc^2\left[1+\left(\frac{Z\alpha}{n-|\kappa|+\gamma}\right)^2\right]^{-1/2},\qquad \gamma=\sqrt{\kappa^2-(Z\alpha)^2}$$

Expanding to order $(Z\alpha)^4$ reproduces the textbook Bohr energy $-13.606\,Z^2/n^2$ eV plus the familiar fine-structure correction $\propto Z^4\alpha^2/n^3$ — this tab uses the full closed form, not the expansion.

Supercritical breakdown

$\gamma$ is only real while $Z\alpha<|\kappa|$. For the most tightly bound states ($\kappa=\pm1$: s₁/₂, p₁/₂) this fails once $Z\gtrsim137$ — the point-charge Dirac bound state ceases to exist ("diving into the negative-energy continuum"), the origin of the famous $Z=137$ discussions. Real nuclei have finite size, which pushes the true critical charge to $Z\approx173$ and replaces the point-charge singularity with a regular solution, but that correction is outside this tab's point-charge model.

∫ Numerical Radial Integration (RK4 Shooting, Exact Energy)

Because $E_{n\kappa}$ is already known exactly in closed form (Derivation, Step 4), no energy search is needed — unlike Tab 4/10's shooting methods, which search for $E$. Instead, the exact eigenenergy is plugged directly into the coupled radial equations, which are integrated outward with a standard 4th-order Runge–Kutta stepper, the same numerical strategy used for the nuclear bound-state search in Tab 10.

Procedure

Seed near the origin

Starting at a small $r_0$ (a fixed tiny fraction of the integration domain), $G(r_0)=r_0^\gamma$ and $F(r_0)=G(r_0)\cdot\dfrac{\gamma+\kappa}{Z\alpha}$, from Derivation Step 3.

Integrate outward

RK4 steps carry $(G,F)$ from $r_0$ to $r_{\max}$, sized adaptively from the exact relativistic decay constant $\lambda=\sqrt{(mc^2)^2-E^2}/\hbar c$ so the domain scales correctly with both $n$ and $Z$, including near-critical ions where the orbit contracts dramatically.

Normalize and count nodes

$(G,F)$ are rescaled so $\int_0^{r_{\max}}\!\big(G^2+F^2\big)\,dr=1$ by trapezoidal integration; $G(r)$'s sign changes are counted and checked against the exact radial quantum number $n_r=n-|\kappa|$ as a numerical sanity check, exactly as node-counting verifies Tab 10's bound states.

⚠ Plugging in the exact eigenenergy makes the physically decaying solution dominate, but any residual growing component (numerical truncation error) still exists in principle and can eventually overwhelm the decaying part far enough out — the same caveat noted for Tab 4/5's outgoing-wave states. The integration domain here is sized to stay well inside the region where this is not yet visible.

Normalization by Simpson's Rule

Both the numerical and analytical radial functions are normalized by the same rule, so the two are directly comparable rather than each carrying a different truncation error. On the uniform grid $r_0,\dots,r_N$ with spacing $h$:

$$\int_0^{r_N}\!\big(G^2+F^2\big)\,dr \approx \frac{h}{3}\Big[f_0+4f_1+2f_2+4f_3+\cdots+4f_{N-1}+f_N\Big],\qquad f_i=G_i^2+F_i^2$$

$(G,F)$ are then divided by the square root of this integral. Simpson's rule is exact for cubics and integrates the oscillatory $G,F$ noticeably more accurately than the trapezoid rule at the same $N$, which is what this tab now uses in place of the earlier trapezoidal normalization.

Normalized Analytical (Closed-Form) Solution

The exact Dirac-Coulomb radial functions are known in closed form in terms of generalized Laguerre polynomials $L_{n}^{\alpha}$:

$$G(r)=C\,x^{\gamma}e^{-x/2}\Big[(N-\kappa)L_{n_r}^{2\gamma+1}(x)-n_rL_{n_r-1}^{2\gamma+1}(x)\Big]$$
$$F(r)=-C\,Z\alpha\,x^{\gamma}e^{-x/2}\Big[(N-\kappa)L_{n_r}^{2\gamma+1}(x)+n_rL_{n_r-1}^{2\gamma+1}(x)\Big]$$

with $x=2\lambda r$, $\lambda=\sqrt{m^2c^4-E^2}/\hbar c$, $\gamma=\sqrt{\kappa^2-(Z\alpha)^2}$, $n_r=n-|\kappa|$, and $N=\sqrt{n_r^2+2n_r\gamma+\kappa^2}$. Rather than carry the textbook Gamma-function prefactor $C$ analytically, this tab fixes $C$ the same way it normalizes the numerical solution — Simpson-integrate $G^2+F^2$ on the identical grid and rescale — so both curves are normalized by the same method and can be overlaid without a separate accounting for each one's constant.

∂ Separation of Variables and the Radial Equation
Separation ansatz

For a central potential $V(r)$, $\psi(r,\theta,\varphi)=R(r)\,Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ separates the Schrödinger equation exactly, with $Y_l^m$ the spherical harmonics satisfying $L^2Y_l^m=\hbar^2l(l+1)Y_l^m$ — the same angular reduction used in Tab 3.

Radial equation with centrifugal barrier
$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) + \left[V(r)+\frac{\hbar^2 l(l+1)}{2\mu r^2}\right]R = E\,R$$

Substituting $u(r)=rR(r)$ removes the first-derivative term, exactly as in Tab 3 and Tab 4:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}u'' + \left[-\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}+\frac{\hbar^2 l(l+1)}{2\mu r^2}\right]u = E\,u$$
Dimensionless form and quantization

Introducing $\rho = 2r/(na_\mu)$ with $a_\mu=\dfrac{4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar^2}{\mu Z e^2}$ turns the radial equation into the associated Laguerre differential equation. Normalizability as $r\to\infty$ forces the power series to terminate, which requires $n-l-1$ to be a non-negative integer — the origin of the quantization $l\le n-1$.

Closed-form radial solution
$$R_{nl}(r) = -\sqrt{\left(\frac{2Z}{na_\mu}\right)^3\frac{(n-l-1)!}{2n\,[(n+l)!]^3}}\;e^{-\rho/2}\,\rho^{\,l}\,L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}(\rho), \qquad \rho=\frac{2Zr}{na_\mu}$$

$L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}$ is a generalized (associated) Laguerre polynomial — the same three-term-recursion function used for the Dirac radial solution in Tab 16, evaluated here with the non-relativistic index convention.

Full normalized wavefunction
$$\psi_{nlm}(r,\theta,\varphi) = R_{nl}(r)\,Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi), \qquad \int_0^\infty\!\!R_{nl}^2\,r^2\,dr = 1,\quad \int|Y_l^m|^2\,d\Omega=1$$

Since the two factors are each independently normalized, $\psi_{nlm}$ is normalized over all space: $\int|\psi_{nlm}|^2\,d^3r=1$. This module normalizes $R_{nl}$ numerically by Simpson's rule on the actual plotted grid (rather than trusting the closed-form prefactor alone), exactly as Tab 16 does for the Dirac radial functions — this guards against truncation error on a finite domain.

🧲 Hydrogen in External Electric & Magnetic Fields

An external uniform electric field $\mathbf F=F\hat z$ and magnetic field $\mathbf B=B\hat z$ add two perturbations to the field-free Hamiltonian of Tab 17:

$$H = H_0 \;-\; eFz \;+\; \frac{\mu_B B}{\hbar}L_z \;+\; \frac{e^2B^2}{8m_e}\big(x^2+y^2\big)$$

The first extra term is the Stark perturbation (linear in $F$); the last two are the Zeeman terms — a paramagnetic term linear in $B$ and a diamagnetic term quadratic in $B$. This tab is spinless and non-relativistic (same footing as Tab 17), so only the normal (orbital) Zeeman effect appears; the spin-dependent anomalous Zeeman effect and fine structure live in Tab 16's Dirac treatment.

∂ Degenerate Perturbation Theory in Parabolic Coordinates
Why spherical $|nlm\rangle$ fail for Stark

$-eFz$ does not commute with $L^2$ (it mixes different $l$ at fixed $n$), so degenerate states with different $l$ get mixed at first order. The perturbation does commute with $L_z$ and with the Runge–Lenz vector component along $z$, so the right degenerate basis is parabolic coordinates $(\xi,\eta,\varphi)$, labeled by $(n_1,n_2,m)$ with $n_1+n_2+|m|+1=n$.

Linear Stark shift

In this basis $-eFz$ is already diagonal, giving the classic result (in these eV/nm/V·nm⁻¹ units, with $a_\mu$ the reduced-mass Bohr radius of Tab 17):

$$\Delta E_{n_1n_2m} = \frac32\,n\,(n_1-n_2)\,a_\mu\,F \equiv \frac32\,n\,k\,a_\mu F$$

Each $n$ therefore fans into $2n-1$ equally spaced levels, $k=-(n-1),\dots,(n-1)$, degenerate in $m$'s sign.

Ground state: no linear term

1s has no degenerate partner at $n=1$, so $\langle 1s|z|1s\rangle=0$ by parity and the leading Stark shift is second order — the atom acquires an induced dipole $\propto \alpha F$, giving $\Delta E=-\tfrac12\alpha F^2$. The exact hydrogen polarizability is $\alpha=\tfrac92 a_0^3$ (in Hartree-atomic-unit form), which converts to real units as:

$$\Delta E_{1s} = -\frac94\left(\frac{a_\mu}{a_0}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{F}{F_{\rm au}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{Ha},\qquad F_{\rm au}=\frac{\mathrm{Ha}}{a_0}\approx514.2\ \text{V/nm}$$
Zeeman: paramagnetic term is already diagonal

Since $[H_0,L_z]=0$ and $|nlm\rangle$ are already $L_z$ eigenstates, first-order degenerate PT is trivial here — the shift is exact at this order:

$$\Delta E^{\rm para}_{m} = \mu_B B\,m,\qquad \mu_B=\frac{e\hbar}{2m_e}=5.78838\times10^{-5}\ \text{eV/T}$$
Diamagnetic term

$\rho^2=x^2+y^2=r^2\sin^2\theta$ separates into a radial and angular expectation value in $|nlm\rangle$, evaluated numerically (Simpson's rule) using the same $R_{nl}(r)$ and $Y_l^m(\theta)$ machinery as Tab 17:

$$\Delta E^{\rm dia}_{nlm} = \frac{e^2B^2}{8m_e}\langle\rho^2\rangle_{nlm},\qquad \langle\rho^2\rangle=\Big[\!\int_0^\infty\! r^4R_{nl}^2\,dr\Big]\Big[2\pi\!\int_{-1}^{1}\!|Y_l^m|^2(1-c^2)\,dc\Big]$$

using $\int R_{nl}^2r^2dr=1$ and $\int|Y_l^m|^2d\Omega=1$ (both already Simpson-normalized on the Tab 17 grid).

⟳ How Quantum States Move Between Each Other in Time

Every earlier tab found stationary states — eigenstates of a fixed Hamiltonian, resonances, or scattering amplitudes at fixed energy. This tab asks a different question: given a system sitting in one state, and a perturbation $H'(t)$ switched on, how does the probability of finding it in another state grow with time? Two limits of this problem are both exactly tractable and both foundational: a coherent, exactly-solvable two-level system driven by an oscillating field (the Rabi problem), and a discrete state weakly coupled to a dense continuum of final states (Fermi's Golden Rule), where the golden rule emerges as the large-time, many-final-state limit of the same underlying formula.

∂ From the Interaction Picture to Two Exact Results
General setup: expand in the unperturbed eigenbasis

Writing $|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_n c_n(t)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle$ and substituting into $i\hbar\partial_t\psi=(H_0+H'(t))\psi$ gives the exact (no approximation yet) coupled equations for the amplitudes:

$$i\hbar\,\dot c_m(t) = \sum_n H'_{mn}(t)\,e^{i\omega_{mn}t}\,c_n(t), \qquad \omega_{mn}\equiv\frac{E_m-E_n}{\hbar}$$
Two-level Rabi problem: exact equations

For two levels with $H'_{12}(t)=H'_{21}(t)=d\cos(\omega t)$ and $E_1=0,\,E_2=E_0$, this module integrates the exact Schrödinger equation (no rotating-wave approximation) directly by RK4 in the Schrödinger picture:

$$i\hbar\dot c_1 = d\cos(\omega t)\,c_2, \qquad i\hbar\dot c_2 = d\cos(\omega t)\,c_1 + E_0\,c_2$$
Rotating-wave approximation: the closed-form Rabi formula

Moving to the interaction picture ($\tilde c_2=c_2e^{iE_0t/\hbar}$) and writing $\cos\omega t=\tfrac12(e^{i\omega t}+e^{-i\omega t})$, one term rotates slowly (near-resonant) and one rapidly; dropping the fast term (RWA) leaves a constant-coefficient 2×2 system that solves in closed form:

$$\Omega_R\equiv\frac{d}{\hbar},\quad \Delta\equiv\omega-\omega_0,\quad \Omega\equiv\sqrt{\Omega_R^2+\Delta^2}$$
$$P_2(t) = \frac{\Omega_R^2}{\Omega^2}\,\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right)$$

This is compared throughout the Simulation tab against the exact numerical integration of Step 2 — agreement is excellent for $d\ll E_0$ and visibly breaks down (Bloch–Siegert shift) once $d$ becomes a sizable fraction of $E_0$.

Weak-coupling, single-final-state limit: first-order perturbation theory

Returning to Step 1 with a constant perturbation $H'_{f i}=V$ switched on at $t=0$ (no oscillation), and treating $c_i\approx1$ throughout (valid while the transition probability stays small — the defining assumption of first-order PT), the sum collapses to a single integral:

$$c_f(t) = -\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t V\,e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}\,dt' = -\frac{V}{\hbar}\cdot\frac{e^{i\omega_{fi}t}-1}{\omega_{fi}}$$
$$P_f(t) = |c_f(t)|^2 = \frac{4V^2}{\hbar^2}\cdot\frac{\sin^2(\omega_{fi}t/2)}{\omega_{fi}^2}, \qquad \omega_{fi}=\frac{E_f-E_i}{\hbar}$$

This sinc²-shaped function is sharply peaked at $E_f=E_i$ with height $\propto t^2$ and width $\propto1/t$ — exactly the emerging statement of energy conservation.

Fermi's Golden Rule: summing over a continuum

Using $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sin^2(xt/2)/x^2\,dx=\pi t/2$, the sinc² function's integral over final-state energy grows exactly linearly in $t$ once its width has narrowed below the scale on which $\rho(E)$ varies. Summing $P_f(t)$ over a continuum of final states with local density of states $\rho(E_i)$ (states per unit energy) gives the celebrated result:

$$\Gamma \;=\; \frac{d}{dt}\sum_f P_f(t) \;=\; \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,V^2\,\rho(E_i), \qquad P_{\rm total}(t)\approx\Gamma t$$

This module verifies the transition to this linear regime directly: it sums Step 4's exact sinc² formula over a finite, discretized set of nearby levels (spacing $\delta=1/\rho$) and compares the running total to the Golden Rule's straight-line prediction $\Gamma t$.

A finite set of $N$ evenly-spaced levels (spacing $\delta=1/\rho$, centered on the initial state's energy) stands in for the true continuum. hν The Photoelectric Effect as a Golden-Rule Bound→Continuum Transition

Tab 19 applied Fermi's Golden Rule to a discrete final state and to an idealized flat continuum. Here the initial state is a genuine hydrogen-like bound state $\psi_{nlm}=R_{nl}(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ from Tab 17, and the final state is a real ionized electron of kinetic energy $E_{\rm kin}=\hbar\omega-I_n$ propagating away from the nucleus — Einstein's photoelectric relation, recovered here as a transition rate rather than assumed as a postulate.

$$\Gamma_{i\to f} = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,\big|\langle f|H'|i\rangle\big|^2\,\rho(E_f), \qquad H' = eFz\cos(\omega t)\ \text{(dipole coupling to the light field)}$$

Absorption of one photon requires $\hbar\omega=E_f-E_i$, i.e. $\hbar\omega=E_{\rm kin}+I_n$ — exactly Einstein's 1905 equation, here derived as the energy-conservation condition built into the Golden Rule's $\delta$-function (the same $\mathrm{sinc}^2\to\delta$ narrowing used in Tab 19).

∂ From the Dipole Matrix Element to a Cross Section
Partial-wave expansion of the outgoing plane wave

The escaping electron is approximated as a plane wave $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$ (Born approximation — the true final state is a Coulomb wave, exact but far more expensive). Expanding in partial waves:

$$e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r} = 4\pi\sum_{l'm'} i^{l'} j_{l'}(kr)\,Y_{l'}^{m'*}(\hat k)\,Y_{l'}^{m'}(\hat r)$$

with $j_{l'}$ the spherical Bessel functions — the same radial special functions used for the circular-well eigenstates referenced back in Tab 2.

Dipole selection rule

The dipole operator $z=r\cos\theta$ is proportional to $Y_1^0$. The angular integral $\int Y_l^{m*}Y_1^0Y_{l'}^{m}\,d\Omega$ vanishes unless $l'=l\pm1$ — exactly the same selection rule that governs which atomic spectral lines are dipole-allowed. Only these two partial waves of the outgoing electron contribute.

Radial dipole matrix element

What survives after the angular integration is a purely radial overlap between the bound $R_{nl}(r)$ (Tab 17, Simpson-normalized) and the outgoing partial wave:

$$I_{l\to l'}(k) = \int_0^\infty R_{nl}(r)\,j_{l'}(kr)\,r^3\,dr$$

evaluated here in atomic units (lengths in $a_0$) so that $\alpha$, the fine-structure constant, is the only remaining dimensionful bookkeeping constant in the final formula.

Golden-rule cross section

Combining the matrix element with the free-electron density of states $\rho(E)\propto k$ and the incident photon flux (which cancels the field-strength dependence exactly as in Tab 19) gives the standard length-gauge dipole photoionization cross section:

$$\sigma_{nl}(\omega) = \frac{4\pi^2\alpha\,\hbar\omega}{3(2l+1)}\sum_{l'=l\pm1} l_>\,\big|I_{l\to l'}(k)\big|^2, \qquad l_>\equiv\max(l,l')$$

with all quantities in atomic units (energy in Hartree, length in $a_0$), then converted to eV/nm/Mb for display — the same unit-conversion pattern used for the Stark/Zeeman formulas in Tab 18.

∫ Numerical Procedure
Bound-state radial function

$R_{nl}(r)$ is built exactly as in Tab 17 (t17BuildRadial), Simpson-normalized on a uniform grid in nm, then rescaled into atomic units: $R_{\rm au}(r_{\rm au}) = R_{nl}(r)\,a_0^{3/2}$, $r_{\rm au}=r/a_0$ — the standard rescaling that keeps $\int R_{\rm au}^2 r_{\rm au}^2\,dr_{\rm au}=1$.

Spherical Bessel functions

$j_{l'}(x)$ for $l'=0,1,2,\dots$ is evaluated by the standard upward three-term recursion from $j_0=\sin x/x$ and $j_1=\sin x/x^2-\cos x/x$. This recursion is well-behaved for the $x=kr$ ranges used here, but can lose accuracy for very high $l'$ at small $x$ — a standard numerical caveat for this recursion, flagged rather than hidden.

Radial integral and cross section

$I_{l\to l'}(k)$ is evaluated by Simpson's rule on the same atomic-unit grid, for each allowed $l'\in\{l-1,l+1\}\cap\{l'\ge0\}$, then combined via the boxed formula above. The result in $a_0^2$ is converted to nm² ($\times a_0^2$) and then to megabarns ($1\ \text{nm}^2 = 10^4$ Mb).

Threshold and sweep

Sweeping $\hbar\omega$ from just above $I_n$ up to several times $I_n$ traces $\sigma_{nl}(\omega)$; the two partial-wave contributions ($l'=l-1$ and $l'=l+1$) are also plotted separately, showing which one dominates (for $s$-states only $l'=1$ contributes; for $p,d,\dots$ states both channels compete, and their relative size shifts with photon energy).

⚠ The plane-wave Born approximation ignores the outgoing electron's attraction to the residual ion — a real photoelectron leaving a bare-Coulomb residual ion accelerates as it departs (the exact final state is a Coulomb wave, not a plane wave). This makes the Born approximation systematically least reliable near threshold (slow electron, strong residual attraction) and progressively better at high photon energy (fast electron, attraction negligible on the timescale of escape) — exactly the pattern in the Regimes table above.
