The Legendre Differential Equation

Standard Form

$$(1-x^2)y'' - 2xy' + l(l+1)y = 0, \qquad x\in[-1,1]$$

Here l is the degree. When the underlying problem is fully three-dimensional rather than axisymmetric, an extra term appears, giving the associated Legendre equation for $P_l^m(x)$:

$$(1-x^2)y'' - 2xy' + \left[l(l+1) - \frac{m^2}{1-x^2}\right]y = 0$$

Derivation via Separation of Variables

Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates, $\nabla^2V=0$, separates as $V(r,\theta,\varphi)=R(r)\Theta(\theta)\Phi(\varphi)$. The polar-angle factor obeys:

$$\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\,\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right) + \left[l(l+1)-\frac{m^2}{\sin^2\theta}\right]\Theta = 0$$

Substituting $x=\cos\theta$, $y(x)=\Theta(\theta)$ turns this directly into the (associated) Legendre equation above — exactly as the radial part of the Helmholtz equation in cylindrical coordinates produces Bessel's equation.

Frobenius Series & Polynomial Truncation

A power-series solution about $x=0$ converges for $|x|<1$, but for generic $l$ it diverges logarithmically at the natural singular points $x=\pm1$ — the physical poles of the sphere. Demanding regularity at both poles forces the series to terminate, which happens if and only if:

$$l \in \{0,1,2,3,\dots\}$$

The truncated series is then a degree-$l$ polynomial — a Legendre polynomial $P_l(x)$. Non-terminating solutions remain singular at the poles and are excluded whenever the full sphere $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ is part of the domain.

Rodrigues' Formula

$$P_l(x) = \frac{1}{2^l\,l!}\frac{d^l}{dx^l}\left(x^2-1\right)^l$$

A remarkably compact closed form: differentiating the simple polynomial $(x^2-1)^l$ exactly $l$ times automatically builds in the normalization $P_l(1)=1$ and, as Tab 3 shows, the orthogonality of the whole family.

Generating Function

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2xt+t^2}} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} P_l(x)\,t^l, \qquad |t|<1$$

This single function packages every $P_l$ at once. It is also, almost unchanged, the electrostatic potential of a unit point charge: set $x=\cos\gamma$ and $t=a/r$ and the left side becomes $r/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|$ — the seed of the multipole expansion built interactively in Tab 4.

Legendre Functions of the Second Kind $Q_l(x)$

$$Q_0(x) = \frac{1}{2}\ln\!\frac{1+x}{1-x}, \qquad Q_1(x) = xQ_0(x)-1$$

Singular at $x=\pm1$ (logarithmic divergence) — the second, linearly independent solution of the same ODE, built from the same recurrence relation used for $P_l$ in Tab 2. Physically relevant only when the domain excludes one or both poles — e.g. conical boundaries, or the region between two cones, where regularity at $\theta=0,\pi$ is no longer required.

Properties of Legendre Functions

Recurrence & Derivative Relations

$$(l+1)P_{l+1}(x) = (2l+1)\,x\,P_l(x) - l\,P_{l-1}(x)$$
$$(1-x^2)P_l'(x) = l\left[P_{l-1}(x) - xP_l(x)\right]$$
$$(2l+1)P_l(x) = P_{l+1}'(x) - P_{l-1}'(x)$$

The first relation alone generates the entire family from $P_0=1$, $P_1=x$ — exactly how the plotter and the polynomial table in Tab 1 build every $P_l$, without ever evaluating Rodrigues' formula directly.

Parity & Special Values

$$P_l(-x) = (-1)^l P_l(x)$$
$$P_l(1) = 1, \qquad P_l(-1) = (-1)^l$$

$P_l$ contains only even powers of $x$ when $l$ is even, and only odd powers when $l$ is odd — visible directly in the polynomial table in Tab 1. At $x=0$, $P_l(0)$ vanishes for every odd $l$.

Associated Legendre Functions $P_l^m(x)$

$$P_l^m(x) = (-1)^m(1-x^2)^{m/2}\frac{d^m}{dx^m}P_l(x), \qquad |m|\le l$$

For $m=0$ this reduces to the ordinary $P_l(x)$. Negative orders are related by:

$$P_l^{-m}(x) = (-1)^m\frac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}P_l^m(x)$$

The factor $(1-x^2)^{m/2}=\sin^m\theta$ forces $P_l^m\equiv0$ whenever $|m|>l$, and makes $P_l^m$ vanish at both poles for any $m\neq0$ — the angular nodes seen in the spherical-harmonic shapes below trace directly back to this factor.

Spherical Harmonics $Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$

$$Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi) = \sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\frac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}}\;P_l^m(\cos\theta)\,e^{im\varphi}$$

The complete, orthonormal basis for functions on the sphere: $\Theta(\theta)=P_l^m(\cos\theta)$ supplies the polar shape, $\Phi(\varphi)=e^{im\varphi}$ the simple harmonic azimuthal part. Every multipole moment, every atomic-orbital angular shape, and every map of a field on a sphere is expanded in this basis.

Orthogonality of Legendre Functions

Orthogonality Relation

$$\int_{-1}^{1} P_l(x)\,P_n(x)\,dx = \frac{2}{2l+1}\,\delta_{ln}$$

Legendre polynomials are orthogonal on $[-1,1]$ with unit weight. This contrasts with Bessel functions, whose orthogonality on $[0,R]$ carries a weight $r$ inherited from cylindrical geometry — the difference traces back to the Sturm–Liouville form of each ODE: Bessel's equation has $w(r)=r$, Legendre's has $w(x)=1$.

Legendre (Legendre–Fourier) Series

Any piecewise-smooth $f(x)$ on $[-1,1]$ expands as:

$$f(x) = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} c_l\,P_l(x)$$
$$c_l = \frac{2l+1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} f(x)\,P_l(x)\,dx$$

The angular analogue of a Fourier series — built from polynomials rather than sines and cosines, and naturally suited to functions defined by their values on a sphere's polar angle.

Sturm–Liouville Form

$$\frac{d}{dx}\!\left[(1-x^2)\frac{dy}{dx}\right] + l(l+1)\,y = 0$$

A self-adjoint eigenvalue problem on $[-1,1]$ with weight $w(x)=1$ and eigenvalues $\lambda_l=l(l+1)$. Self-adjointness with regular boundary conditions at $x=\pm1$ guarantees real eigenvalues and mutually orthogonal eigenfunctions — the same guarantee used for the Bessel Sturm–Liouville problem, just with a different weight.

Orthogonality on the Sphere

$$\int_{-1}^{1} P_l^m(x)\,P_{l'}^m(x)\,dx = \frac{2}{2l+1}\frac{(l+m)!}{(l-m)!}\,\delta_{ll'}$$
$$\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi} Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)\,Y_{l'}^{m'*}(\theta,\varphi)\,\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi = \delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'}$$

Full spherical-harmonic orthogonality combines the associated-Legendre relation above (polar integral, weight $\sin\theta\,d\theta=-dx$) with the trivial orthogonality of $e^{im\varphi}$ over a full turn — together reconstructing the solid-angle weight $\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi$ that appears whenever a field is expanded over a sphere.

Application: Electrostatic Multipole Expansion

Potential of an Arbitrary Charge Distribution

For a point charge $q$ at position $\mathbf r'$ (distance $a=|\mathbf r'|$ from the origin), the exact potential at a field point $\mathbf r$ with $r>a$ expands — via the generating function from Tab 1 — into an infinite series of Legendre polynomials:

$$\frac{1}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} \frac{a^l}{r^{l+1}}P_l(\cos\gamma)$$

where $\gamma$ is the angle between $\mathbf r$ and $\mathbf r'$. Summing over every charge in a distribution $\rho(\mathbf r')$:

$$V(\mathbf r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{l+1}}\int \rho(\mathbf r')\,r'^{\,l}\,P_l(\cos\gamma)\,d^3r'$$

Each term is a multipole moment: $l=0$ the total charge (monopole), $l=1$ the dipole moment projected along $\hat{\mathbf r}$, $l=2$ the quadrupole, and so on — each successive order falling off one extra power of $1/r$ faster than the last.

Why Legendre Polynomials Appear Here — Key Points

  • The generating function is the multipole expansion: set $x=\cos\gamma$, $t=a/r$ in $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2xt+t^2}}=\sum_l P_l(x)t^l$ from Tab 1, and the left side is exactly $r/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|$ — no separate derivation is needed once the generating function is known.
  • Convergence requires $r>a$: the series is only valid outside the smallest sphere enclosing every source charge; inside that sphere it diverges, mirroring why a Bessel–Fourier series only converges within the physical boundary in cylindrical problems.
  • Falloff hierarchy: monopole $\sim 1/r$, dipole $\sim 1/r^2$, quadrupole $\sim 1/r^3$ — far from a neutral, non-polar distribution only the lowest surviving moment matters, which is why charge-neutral, dipole-free molecules are still detectable via their quadrupole field at moderate range.
  • Off-axis sources need $P_l^m$: the simple $P_l(\cos\gamma)$ form above is exact for any single point charge, but combining many charges at different positions into global multipole moments for a non-axisymmetric distribution requires the addition theorem and the full $P_l^m$/$Y_l^m$ basis from Tab 2.
  • The same mathematics, different fields: identical $P_l(\cos\gamma)/r^{l+1}$ towers describe the Newtonian gravitational multipoles of a lumpy mass distribution and the multipole radiation fields of antennas — only the physical meaning of the moments changes.

Gravitational Potential & Geodesy

Geopotential Series Expansion

$$U(r,\theta) = \frac{GM_E}{r}\left[1 - \sum_{l=2}^{\infty} J_l\left(\frac{R_E}{r}\right)^{\!l} P_l(\cos\theta)\right]$$

The gravitational potential exterior to any axisymmetric body is completely encoded in its zonal geopotential coefficients $J_l$. Each coefficient is the $l$-th multipole moment of the mass distribution, projected onto $P_l(\cos\theta)$ where $\theta$ is colatitude from the North Pole. Because the monopole term $GM_E/r$ already carries ~99.999% of Earth's potential, successive $J_l$ terms are progressively smaller corrections — each also falling off one extra power of $R_E/r$ faster than the last, making them negligible at large altitude.

Pinpoints — Zonal Coefficients $J_l$

  • $J_1 = 0$ — Dipole vanishes when origin = centre of mass (by definition).
  • $J_2 \approx 1.083\times10^{-3}$ — Earth's equatorial bulge (oblateness). Largest correction. Causes $\approx0.03°$/day nodal precession of satellite orbits.
  • $J_3 \approx -2.53\times10^{-6}$ — Earth's "pear shape": slight North–South mass asymmetry. Perturbs inclination slowly.
  • $J_4 \approx -1.62\times10^{-6}$ — Next zonal harmonic, from inhomogeneous mantle density.
  • $J_5, J_6, \ldots$ — Increasingly localised anomalies; detected via precise satellite tracking (GRACE, GOCE).
  • All odd $J_l$ small — Exact N–S symmetry would zero them; real Earth has only slight asymmetry.

$J_2$ & Satellite Nodal Precession

$$\dot\Omega = -\frac{3}{2}\frac{n\,J_2\,R_E^2}{a^2(1-e^2)^2}\cos i$$

The equatorial bulge exerts a torque on any inclined orbit, rotating its ascending node at $\dot\Omega$. At GPS altitude ($a\approx4.2R_E$, $i=55°$) this is $\approx-0.03°$/day. At Sun-synchronous inclination ($i\approx97°$, $\cos i < 0$) $\dot\Omega$ becomes positive — the node advances $\approx+0.99°$/day, keeping the orbit aligned with the Sun year-round. In both cases the $J_2$ Legendre correction $P_2(\cos\theta)$ is the direct physical source; without it, satellites would not precess at all.

$$\dot\omega = \frac{3nJ_2R_E^2}{2a^2(1-e^2)^2}\!\left(\frac{5}{2}\sin^2 i - 2\right)$$

Apsidal precession of the argument of perigee $\omega$ — zero at the "frozen orbit" inclination $i=63.43°$ (Molniya orbits exploit this).

Tidal Expansion — Moon's Potential Near Earth

$$V_\text{tidal}(\mathbf r) = -\frac{GM_M}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r_M|} = -\frac{GM_M}{r_M}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\!\left(\frac{r}{r_M}\right)^{\!l}\!P_l(\cos\angle(\hat r,\hat r_M)), \quad r < r_M$$
  • $l=0$: Uniform shift — produces no force on Earth as a whole (rigid-body translation only).
  • $l=1$: Linear gradient — the dominant tidal force (stretches Earth along the Earth–Moon axis). Same order as the Moon's mean gravitational acceleration at Earth's surface.
  • $l=2$: $P_2(\cos\gamma)$ quadrupole — raises the oceans' twice-daily tides; also flexes the solid Earth by $\sim50$ cm.
  • $l\ge3$: Negligible at Earth's surface ($r/r_M\approx1/60$), but grow rapidly closer to the Moon — relevant inside the Roche limit ($\sim2.5R_M$) where tidal forces exceed self-gravity.

Application: Earth–Moon Gravitational Landscape

Effective Potential in the Co-rotating Frame

$$\Phi_\text{eff}(x,y)= -\frac{GM_E}{r_E}\!\left[1-J_2\!\left(\frac{R_E}{r_E}\right)^{\!2}\!P_2(\cos\theta)\right]-\frac{GM_M}{r_M}-\frac{1}{2}\omega^2\!\left[(x-x_\text{cm})^2+y^2\right]$$

In the frame rotating at $\omega=\sqrt{G(M_E+M_M)/d^3}$, the saddle points of $\Phi_\text{eff}$ are the Lagrange points. L1 sits between Earth and Moon — the inner Roche lobe gateway. The figure-8 equipotential through L1 is the Roche lobe boundary: material inside Earth's lobe is gravitationally bound to Earth, material in the Moon's lobe to the Moon. L4 and L5 form the equilateral triangle vertices — local maxima of $\Phi_\text{eff}$, stable against perturbations via the Coriolis force for $q=M_M/M_E\lesssim0.039$.

Partial-Wave Expansion in Scattering Theory

The Partial-Wave Series

$$f(\theta) = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (2l+1)\,a_l\,P_l(\cos\theta)$$

For any spherically symmetric scattering potential, the amplitude $f(\theta)$ depends only on the angle $\theta$ between the incoming and outgoing directions — never on azimuth — so it expands in exactly the same axisymmetric basis $P_l(\cos\theta)$ used throughout Tabs 1–5. Each partial wave $l$ contributes through a single complex coefficient $a_l$, the partial-wave amplitude.

$$a_l = e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l, \qquad \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(\theta)|^2$$

A spherically symmetric potential cannot mix different $l$ — it can only delay or advance each incoming partial wave in phase by a single real number $\delta_l(E)$, the phase shift. Because $f(\theta)$ is a coherent sum over $l$ before it is squared, different partial waves interfere with one another in the cross section — the interference animated in the simulation below.

Where the $P_l$ Come From — Rayleigh's Plane-Wave Expansion

$$e^{ikz} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (2l+1)\,i^l\,j_l(kr)\,P_l(\cos\theta)$$

The incoming plane wave is itself already a sum of partial waves, built from the spherical Bessel functions $j_l(kr)$ — the same radial functions from the Bessel-functions reference — multiplying the identical $P_l(\cos\theta)$ angular factor seen in every earlier tab. A spherically symmetric scatterer leaves this angular factor untouched; it only reshapes the radial part and attaches the phase shift $\delta_l$, producing the outgoing $a_l\,P_l(\cos\theta)$ term above.

Unitarity & the Partial Cross Section

$$\sigma_l = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l, \qquad \sigma = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sigma_l$$

Since $|a_l|=|\sin\delta_l|\le1$, each partial wave has a hard unitarity limit $\sigma_l^{\max}=\frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)$, saturated exactly at resonance, $\delta_l=\pi/2$. The contribution table below reports $\sigma_l$ for every active $l$ so the approach to this limit is visible directly.

Pinpoints — Partial Waves as Orbital Shapes

  • $l=0$ (s-wave): $P_0=1$ — perfectly isotropic. The lowest partial wave alone can never produce a forward- or backward-peaked pattern.
  • $l=1$ (p-wave): $P_1(\cos\theta)=\cos\theta$ — a two-lobe, forward/backward-symmetric pattern, identical in shape to the $m=0$ p-orbital plotted in Tab 2's spherical-harmonic viewer.
  • $l=2$ (d-wave) and higher: four-, six-, eight-lobe polar patterns — again exactly the $P_l(\cos\theta)$ shapes of the corresponding $m=0$ orbitals in Tab 2.
  • Same function, two meanings: switch to Tab 2, set $m=0$, and slide $l$ — the lobe pattern shown there is precisely the shape each partial wave imprints on the scattering amplitude here, before it is even weighted by $a_l$.
  • Why $l_\max$ tracks energy: a classical projectile with impact parameter $b$ carries angular momentum $\sim\hbar k b$; a wave can only "use" angular momentum $l$ if $l\lesssim ka$ for a scatterer of range $a$. Raising the energy slider unlocks higher-$l$ lobes one at a time — the semiclassical condition $l_\max\sim ka$.

Application: Hard-Sphere Partial-Wave Scattering

The Model

A rigid, impenetrable sphere of radius $a=1$ forces the wavefunction to vanish at $r=a$ for every $l$, fixing each phase shift exactly:

$$\tan\delta_l = \frac{j_l(ka)}{y_l(ka)}$$

where $j_l$ and $y_l$ are spherical Bessel and spherical Neumann functions. Raise the scattering energy below (units $\hbar=2m=1$, so $k=\sqrt{E}$) and watch the pattern sharpen into the classic forward-peaked Fraunhofer shape of a "black disk" — the same physics behind the forward diffraction peak in heavy-ion elastic-scattering angular distributions. In that strong-absorption limit the total cross section approaches $\sigma\to2\pi a^2$ — twice the geometric cross section.

Heat Conduction: Laplace's Equation on a Sphere

Steady-State Heat Conduction ⇔ Laplace's Equation

$$\nabla^2 T = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^2\frac{\partial T}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial T}{\partial\theta}\right) = 0$$

In steady state, with no heat sources and an axisymmetric (φ-independent) boundary condition, the temperature $T(r,\theta)$ obeys Laplace's equation. Separating $T=R(r)\Theta(\theta)$ gives exactly the angular equation already solved in Tab 1 — $\Theta(\theta)=P_l(\cos\theta)$, eigenvalue $l(l+1)$ — this time paired with a genuine radial equation rather than appearing alone.

Radial Euler Equation

$$r^2R'' + 2rR' - l(l+1)R = 0$$
$$R(r) = A_l\,r^l + B_l\,r^{-(l+1)}$$

A power-law (Euler) equation with two independent solutions per $l$: a growing piece $r^l$ and a decaying piece $r^{-(l+1)}$. The full axisymmetric harmonic function is:

$$T(r,\theta)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\Big[A_l r^l + B_l r^{-(l+1)}\Big]P_l(\cos\theta)$$

Regularity Selects Interior vs Exterior

$$T_{\text{in}}(r,\theta)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty} A_l\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{l} P_l(\cos\theta), \quad r\le R$$
$$T_{\text{out}}(r,\theta)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty} B_l\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{l+1} P_l(\cos\theta), \quad r\ge R$$

$r^{-(l+1)}$ diverges at the origin, so it's discarded inside the sphere; $r^l$ diverges at infinity, so it's discarded outside — exactly the same regularity argument that selected $P_l$ over the singular $Q_l$ at the poles in Tab 1, now applied to the radial coordinate instead of the angular one.

Matching the Boundary Condition — Legendre Series

$$T(R,\theta) = f(\theta) = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} c_l\,P_l(\cos\theta), \qquad A_l = B_l = c_l$$
$$c_l = \frac{2l+1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} f(\cos\theta)\,P_l(x)\,dx, \qquad x=\cos\theta$$

The exact same coefficient formula from Tab 3's orthogonality relation. Continuity across $r=R$ is automatic: both $(r/R)^l$ and $(R/r)^{l+1}$ equal exactly $1$ at $r=R$, so $T_{\text{in}}(R,\theta)=T_{\text{out}}(R,\theta)=f(\theta)$ for any coefficients — the interior and exterior series are forced to agree on the boundary by construction, not by extra tuning.

Pinpoints — Why the Sphere Smooths the Pattern Inward and Outward

  • $l=0$, the mean-value property: the centre of the sphere sits at exactly the average boundary temperature $c_0$, no matter how sharply $T$ varies on the surface — a direct consequence of $(r/R)^0=1$ for every $r$, including $r=0$.
  • High-$l$ terms vanish fastest moving inward: $(r/R)^l\to0$ as $r\to0$, so a sharp boundary feature (a hot spot, a step) is smoothed away near the centre — the same mechanism behind the maximum principle: an interior point can never be hotter or colder than every boundary point.
  • Outside, the same falloff runs in distance: $(R/r)^{l+1}$ kills high-$l$ detail fastest far away, leaving only the lowest surviving moment visible — the identical falloff hierarchy from the multipole expansion (Tab 4) and the $J_l$ geopotential coefficients (Tab 5), now describing heat instead of charge or mass.
  • Discontinuous boundary data never converges exactly: a step-function or polar-cap profile needs infinitely many $l$; any finite $l_{\max}$ leaves Gibbs-phenomenon ripples near the jump — watch this directly in the profile chart below.
  • Parity check: the hemisphere-step preset is odd, $f(-x)=-f(x)$; by the parity property from Tab 2, every even $c_l$ must vanish — confirm it in the coefficients table.

Electrostatic Analysis of Charged Oblate & Prolate Spheroidal Conductors

Geometry of Oblate and Prolate Spheroids

A spheroid is an ellipsoid of revolution: rotate an ellipse about one of its own axes. With equatorial semi-axis $a$, polar semi-axis $b$, and focal distance $c=\sqrt{|a^2-b^2|}$:

Oblate (flattened, disc-like) — rotation about the minor axis, $b

$$\frac{x^2+y^2}{a^2}+\frac{z^2}{b^2}=1$$

The degenerate limit $b\to0$ is a flat conducting disc of radius $a$.

Prolate (elongated, needle/cigar-like) — rotation about the major axis, $a>b$:

$$\frac{x^2+y^2}{b^2}+\frac{z^2}{a^2}=1$$

The degenerate limit $b\to0$ is a thin conducting needle of length $2a$.

Coordinate Systems

Cartesian, Cylindrical & Spherical — reference

$$x,\,y,\,z$$
$$x=\rho\cos\varphi,\ y=\rho\sin\varphi,\ z=z$$
$$x=r\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ y=r\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ z=r\cos\theta$$

Oblate Spheroidal Coordinates $(\xi,\eta,\varphi)$

$$x=c\sqrt{(1+\xi^2)(1-\eta^2)}\cos\varphi,\quad y=\dots\sin\varphi,\quad z=c\,\xi\eta$$

$\xi\in[0,\infty)$, $\eta\in[-1,1]$. Surfaces $\xi=\text{const}$ are confocal oblate spheroids; $\xi=0$ degenerates to the flat focal disc of radius $c$ in the $z=0$ plane (its rim is the focal ring).

Prolate Spheroidal Coordinates $(\xi,\eta,\varphi)$

$$x=c\sqrt{(\xi^2-1)(1-\eta^2)}\cos\varphi,\quad y=\dots\sin\varphi,\quad z=c\,\xi\eta$$

$\xi\in[1,\infty)$, $\eta\in[-1,1]$. Surfaces $\xi=\text{const}$ are confocal prolate spheroids; $\xi=1$ degenerates to the focal line segment $-c\le z\le c$ on the axis.

Laplace's Equation Separates — Same Legendre Equation, New Argument

Separating $\nabla^2V=0$ in either spheroidal system gives the angular factor $H(\eta)=P_l^m(\eta)$ — identical to Tabs 1–2 — while the radial factor obeys Legendre's equation with $\eta\to\xi$. For oblate coordinates the substitution $\xi\to i\xi$ is needed, so the radial solutions are $P_l(i\xi)$, $Q_l(i\xi)$; for prolate coordinates $\xi$ is already real and $\ge1$, so the radial solutions are simply $P_l(\xi)$, $Q_l(\xi)$ — exactly the second-kind functions introduced (but left unused) in Tab 1.

Charged Conducting Discs and Spheroids

An isolated conductor held at potential $V_0$, shaped as a spheroid $\xi=\xi_0$, is axisymmetric and up–down symmetric — so by uniqueness only the $l=0$ angular term ($P_0=1$) survives. Regularity at infinity (the exterior potential must decay) eliminates the growing $P_0(i\xi)$ / $P_0(\xi)$ solution, leaving only the $l=0$ second-kind term:

$$V_{\text{oblate}}(\xi)=V_0\,\frac{\operatorname{arccot}\xi}{\operatorname{arccot}\xi_0},\qquad \xi\ge\xi_0$$
$$V_{\text{prolate}}(\xi)=V_0\,\frac{Q_0(\xi)}{Q_0(\xi_0)}=V_0\,\frac{\ln\frac{\xi+1}{\xi-1}}{\ln\frac{\xi_0+1}{\xi_0-1}},\qquad \xi\ge\xi_0$$

using $Q_0(i\xi)=-i\operatorname{arccot}\xi$. Inside the conductor ($\xi<\xi_0$) the field vanishes and $V=V_0$ everywhere — same as any conductor.

$$C_{\text{oblate}}=\frac{4\pi\epsilon_0c}{\operatorname{arccot}\xi_0}\ \xrightarrow{\xi_0\to0}\ 8\epsilon_0a\ \ \text{(flat disc, classic result)}$$
$$C_{\text{prolate}}=\frac{4\pi\epsilon_0c}{Q_0(\xi_0)}=\frac{8\pi\epsilon_0c}{\ln\frac{\xi_0+1}{\xi_0-1}}\ \xrightarrow{\xi_0\to1^+}\ \text{thin-needle limit}$$

Both formulas reduce to $C=4\pi\epsilon_0a$ as the shape approaches a sphere ($\xi_0\to\infty$).

Surface Charge Distributions

$$\sigma_{\text{oblate}}(\eta)=\frac{Q}{4\pi c^2\sqrt{1+\xi_0^2}\,\sqrt{\xi_0^2+\eta^2}}$$
$$\sigma_{\text{prolate}}(\eta)=\frac{Q}{4\pi c^2\sqrt{\xi_0^2-1}\,\sqrt{\xi_0^2-\eta^2}}$$

Oblate: minimised at the poles ($\eta=\pm1$), maximised at the equator ($\eta=0$, the disc's rim) — diverging there as $\xi_0\to0$, recovering the classic disc result $\sigma(\rho)\propto1/\sqrt{a^2-\rho^2}$, the edge effect. Prolate: the opposite — maximised at $\eta=\pm1$ (the sharp tips), diverging as $\xi_0\to1^+$ — the lightning-rod / corona effect that concentrates charge at pointed conductors.

Electric Field Components

$$\mathbf E=-\frac{1}{h_\xi}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\xi}\hat{\boldsymbol\xi}-\frac{1}{h_\eta}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\eta}\hat{\boldsymbol\eta}$$

Oblate metric coefficients: $h_\xi=c\sqrt{\frac{\xi^2+\eta^2}{1+\xi^2}}$, $h_\eta=c\sqrt{\frac{\xi^2+\eta^2}{1-\eta^2}}$. Prolate: $h_\xi=c\sqrt{\frac{\xi^2-\eta^2}{\xi^2-1}}$, $h_\eta=c\sqrt{\frac{\xi^2-\eta^2}{1-\eta^2}}$. Because the isolated-conductor solution above depends only on $\xi$, $E_\eta\equiv0$ — field lines run purely normal to the confocal spheroids, the direct generalisation of radial field lines from a point charge.

Multipole Expansion — Spheroidal Generalisation of Tab 4

$$V(\xi,\eta)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}A_l\,P_l(\eta)\,\mathcal{Q}_l(\xi)$$

where $\mathcal Q_l(\xi)$ is the appropriate decaying radial function ($Q_l(i\xi)$ rescaled real, oblate; $Q_l(\xi)$, prolate), falling off as $1/\xi^{l+1}$ — the spheroidal analogue of $1/r^{l+1}$ in Tab 4. A symmetric isolated conductor keeps only $A_0$; a non-uniform charge on (or external field polarising) the same spheroid activates $A_1,A_2,\dots$ exactly as additional point charges activated higher $l$ in the spherical multipole builder.

Antenna Radiation Patterns & Electric Multipole Expansion

Why Associated Legendre Functions Appear in Radiation Theory

Antenna fields satisfy Maxwell's equations, which in the far field reduce to the vector wave equation — separable in spherical coordinates. The angular factor of each radiation mode obeys exactly the same associated Legendre equation seen throughout this reference. For an electric multipole of order l, the far-field E-field amplitude is:

$$E_\theta(r,\theta,t) \;\propto\; \underbrace{P_l^1(\cos\theta)}_{\displaystyle =\,\frac{d}{d\theta}P_l(\cos\theta)} \cdot \frac{\cos(kr-\omega t)}{r}$$

using the identity $\dfrac{d}{d\theta}P_l(\cos\theta)=P_l^1(\cos\theta)$. The radiated power per solid angle is therefore:

$$\frac{dP}{d\Omega} = |f(\theta)|^2 \;\propto\; \left|P_l^1(\cos\theta)\right|^2$$

This is the m=1 row of the spherical-harmonic shape shown in Tab 2 — the same family used in the electrostatic multipole expansion (Tab 4), the geopotential (Tab 5), and the partial-wave cross section (Tab 6).

Pinpoints — Radiation Multipoles vs. Static Multipoles

  • l=1 (electric dipole): $P_1^1(\cos\theta)=-\sin\theta$; pattern $\propto\sin^2\theta$ — the textbook donut. No radiation along the antenna axis.
  • l=2 (quadrupole): $P_2^1=-3\sin\theta\cos\theta$; four-lobe pattern, nodal cone at $\theta=90°$.
  • l=3 (octupole): six lobes; two nodal cones. Each step up in $l$ adds one nodal cone and one lobe pair.
  • Nodal cones = zeros of $P_l^1(\cos\theta)$ — zero radiation at angles where Tab 2's m=1 polar plot crosses zero. Tab 2, set m=1, slide l — the shape there is exactly the pattern here.
  • Physical antennas (half-wave dipole, linear arrays) decompose into many multipole orders. Their Legendre expansion extracts each $c_l$ as the electric multipole moment — directly analogous to the moment table in Tab 4.

Legendre Expansion of Any Radiation Pattern

Any azimuthally symmetric pattern $f(\theta)$ expands as:

$$f(\theta) = \sum_{l=1}^{\infty} c_l\,P_l^1(\cos\theta)$$
$$c_l = \frac{(2l+1)}{2}\frac{(l-1)!}{(l+1)!}\int_{-1}^{1} f(\theta)\,P_l^1(x)\,dx$$

The coefficient $c_l$ is the electric multipole moment of order $l$. For the Hertzian dipole, only $c_1\neq0$. For the half-wave dipole, all odd $c_l$ are non-zero — the sharp forward lobe of a Yagi array is the constructive interference of many $P_l^1$ contributions.
