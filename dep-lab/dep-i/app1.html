🍎 Falling Object with Air Resistance

When an object falls through air (or any fluid), two forces act on it: gravity pulling it downward and drag opposing its motion. The drag force depends on the object's velocity, giving us a first-order linear ordinary differential equation.

This is the simplest physical ODE, but it contains a profound idea: the system reaches a terminal velocity — a steady state where drag exactly balances gravity. Raindrops, skydivers, and dust particles all obey this equation.

∂ Derivation from Newton's Second Law
Identify Forces

On a falling object, two forces act along the vertical axis (taking downward as positive):

$$F_{\text{gravity}} = mg \quad (\downarrow)\qquad F_{\text{drag}} = -bv \quad (\uparrow, \text{ opposing motion})$$
Newton's Second Law

The net force equals mass times acceleration:

$$F_{\text{net}} = ma \implies m\frac{dv}{dt} = mg - bv$$
Rearrange as Standard Form

Divide by $m$ and move the $v$ term to the left:

$$\frac{dv}{dt} + \frac{b}{m}v = g \qquad \text{(1st-order linear, inhomogeneous ODE)}$$
Integrating Factor Method

For $\frac{dv}{dt} + P(t)v = Q(t)$ with $P = b/m$ (constant), the integrating factor is:

$$\mu(t) = e^{\int P\,dt} = e^{(b/m)t}$$

Multiply both sides:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\!\left[e^{bt/m}\,v\right] = g\,e^{bt/m}$$
Integrate Both Sides
$$e^{bt/m}\,v = \frac{mg}{b}\,e^{bt/m} + C$$
$$v(t) = \frac{mg}{b} + C\,e^{-bt/m}$$
Apply Initial Condition $v(0) = v_0$
$$v_0 = \frac{mg}{b} + C \implies C = v_0 - \frac{mg}{b} = v_0 - v_T$$
$$\boxed{v(t) = v_T\!\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right) + v_0\,e^{-t/\tau}}$$

where $v_T = mg/b$ is the terminal velocity and $\tau = m/b$ is the time constant.

Integrate for Position
$$x(t) = x_0 + v_T t + (v_0 - v_T)\tau\!\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$$

As $t\to\infty$: position grows linearly as $x\approx v_T t + \text{const}$ — pure terminal velocity.

Physical Interpretation

Long-Time Behavior

As $t \gg \tau$, the exponential terms vanish:

$$v(t) \xrightarrow{t\to\infty} v_T = \frac{mg}{b}$$

The object always approaches terminal velocity, regardless of initial conditions. This is a stable fixed point of the ODE.

Energy Analysis

Power dissipated by drag: $P_{\text{drag}} = bv^2$. At terminal velocity, power input from gravity $P_g = mgv_T$ exactly equals the drag power:

$$mg v_T = b v_T^2 \;\checkmark$$

The gravitational potential energy is entirely converted to heat.

∫ Analytical Solution
$$v(t) = \underbrace{v_T}_{\text{terminal}} \left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right) + \underbrace{v_0\,e^{-t/\tau}}_{\text{IC decay}}$$
$$x(t) = x_0 + v_T t + (v_0 - v_T)\,\tau\!\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$$

where $v_T = mg/b$, $\tau = m/b$.

The harmonic oscillator is the most important equation in physics. It describes any system displaced from a stable equilibrium: springs, pendulums (small angle), LC circuits, acoustic cavities, molecular bonds, and quantum vacuum fluctuations. 📈 Logistic Equation — Population with Carrying Capacity

The logistic equation corrects the naive Malthusian model of unlimited exponential growth by introducing a carrying capacity $K$ — the maximum sustainable population given available resources. As the population $N$ approaches $K$, the growth rate is suppressed by the factor $(1 - N/K)$, which goes to zero exactly at $N=K$, creating a stable equilibrium.

Proposed by Pierre François Verhulst in 1838, the logistic model is the foundation of ecology, epidemiology, tumor growth modeling, technology adoption curves, and neural network activation functions (the sigmoid). It is the simplest nonlinear autonomous ODE that still admits a closed-form solution.

∂ Derivation & Exact Solution via Partial Fractions
Start from Malthus (Exponential Growth)

The simplest population model assumes per-capita growth rate $r$ is constant:

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = rN \implies N(t) = N_0 e^{rt}$$

This predicts unlimited growth — unrealistic for any finite environment.

Introduce Density Dependence

Resources are finite. As $N$ increases, competition suppresses the effective growth rate. Verhulst proposed the per-capita rate decreases linearly with $N$:

$$\frac{1}{N}\frac{dN}{dt} = r\!\left(1 - \frac{N}{K}\right)$$

At $N \ll K$: growth $\approx rN$ (exponential). At $N = K$: growth $= 0$ (equilibrium). At $N > K$: growth is negative (population falls).

The Logistic ODE
$$\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\!\left(1 - \frac{N}{K}\right) = rN - \frac{r}{K}N^2$$

This is a Bernoulli ODE (nonlinear, but exactly solvable by separation of variables).

Separate Variables
$$\frac{dN}{N(1 - N/K)} = r\,dt$$

The left side requires partial fraction decomposition:

$$\frac{1}{N(1-N/K)} = \frac{K}{N(K-N)} = \frac{1}{N} + \frac{1}{K-N}$$
Integrate Both Sides
$$\int\!\left(\frac{1}{N} + \frac{1}{K-N}\right)dN = \int r\,dt$$
$$\ln N - \ln(K-N) = rt + C_0 \implies \ln\!\frac{N}{K-N} = rt + C_0$$
Exponentiate and Apply Initial Condition
$$\frac{N}{K-N} = A\,e^{rt}, \quad \text{at }t=0: A = \frac{N_0}{K-N_0}$$

Solve for $N$:

$$\boxed{N(t) = \frac{K}{1 + \left(\dfrac{K - N_0}{N_0}\right)e^{-rt}}}$$

This is the classic logistic (sigmoid) curve, valid for all $N_0 > 0$.

Inflection Point — Maximum Growth Rate

The growth rate $dN/dt = rN(1-N/K)$ is maximized at $N = K/2$:

$$\left.\frac{dN}{dt}\right|_{\max} = \frac{rK}{4}, \quad \text{achieved at } t^* = \frac{1}{r}\ln\!\left(\frac{K-N_0}{N_0}\right)$$

The inflection point is always at half the carrying capacity, independent of $r$ and $N_0$.

Stability Analysis

Fixed points: $dN/dt = 0$ gives $N^* = 0$ and $N^* = K$. Linearize $f(N) = rN(1-N/K)$:

$$f'(N^*) = r - \frac{2r}{K}N^* \implies f'(0) = r > 0 \text{ (unstable)},\quad f'(K) = -r < 0 \text{ (stable)}$$

The carrying capacity $K$ is a globally stable equilibrium for all $N_0 > 0$.

∫ Analytical Solution & Derived Quantities
$$N(t) = \frac{K\,N_0}{N_0 + (K - N_0)\,e^{-rt}}$$

🐺 Predator–Prey: Lotka–Volterra Equations

The Lotka–Volterra equations model the interaction between two species: a prey population $x$ (e.g., rabbits) and a predator population $y$ (e.g., foxes). Proposed independently by Alfred Lotka (1925) and Vito Volterra (1926), they are the paradigm of coupled nonlinear ODEs in mathematical biology.

The model produces perpetual oscillations: prey grow unchecked → predators thrive → prey crash → predators starve → prey recover. The populations cycle indefinitely through a family of closed orbits in phase space, and the cycle period and amplitude depend on initial conditions — an unusual non-generic behavior explained by a hidden conserved quantity.

🔬 Hidden conserved quantity: The quantity $$V(x,y) = \delta x - \gamma\ln x + \beta y - \alpha\ln y = \text{constant}$$ is conserved along every trajectory. This is why phase-space orbits are closed curves, and why the model produces perpetual (undamped) oscillations — it is a conservative system in disguise.

∂ Derivation, Fixed Points & Linear Stability
Prey Equation — Births Minus Predation

In the absence of predators ($y=0$), prey reproduce exponentially: $\dot x = \alpha x$. Predation events are proportional to the product $xy$ (encounters per unit time). Each encounter kills prey at rate $\beta$:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = \underbrace{\alpha x}_{\text{birth}} - \underbrace{\beta xy}_{\text{predation}}$$
Predator Equation — Growth from Prey, Natural Death

In the absence of prey ($x=0$), predators die: $\dot y = -\gamma y$. Each prey encounter creates new predators at efficiency $\delta$:

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \underbrace{\delta xy}_{\text{growth from hunting}} - \underbrace{\gamma y}_{\text{natural death}}$$
Fixed Points

Set $\dot x = 0$ and $\dot y = 0$:

$$x(\alpha - \beta y) = 0 \quad\text{and}\quad y(\delta x - \gamma) = 0$$
$$\text{Trivial: }(x^*, y^*) = (0, 0)$$ $$\text{Coexistence: }(x^*, y^*) = \left(\frac{\gamma}{\delta},\; \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)$$
Jacobian at Coexistence Point

Linearize around $(x^*, y^*)$ using Jacobian $J = \partial(\dot x, \dot y)/\partial(x,y)$:

$$J = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha - \beta y & -\beta x \\ \delta y & \delta x - \gamma \end{pmatrix}\bigg|_{(x^*,y^*)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\beta\gamma/\delta \\ \delta\alpha/\beta & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda = \pm i\sqrt{\alpha\gamma}$ — purely imaginary, giving neutrally stable center (closed orbits in phase space).

Conservation Law

Divide the two equations: $dy/dx = (\delta xy - \gamma y)/(\alpha x - \beta xy)$. Separating variables:

$$\frac{\delta x - \gamma}{x}\,dx = \frac{\beta y - \alpha}{y}\,dy$$
$$\delta x - \gamma\ln x + \beta y - \alpha\ln y = V_0 = \text{const}$$

This is the Lotka–Volterra conserved quantity. Each initial condition $(x_0, y_0)$ determines a unique value $V_0$, and the trajectory stays on the level curve $V(x,y) = V_0$ forever.

Approximate Oscillation Period

For small oscillations around $(x^*, y^*)$, the period is approximately:

$$T \approx \frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\alpha\gamma}}$$

For large-amplitude oscillations, the period increases. The period depends on $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ only (not $\beta$ or $\delta$) to leading order.

💧 Fluid Dynamics & the Bernoulli Equation

Bernoulli's principle states that in a steady, incompressible, inviscid flow, an increase in fluid speed occurs simultaneously with a decrease in pressure or potential energy. Applied between two points along a streamline, it yields one of the most powerful equations in classical fluid mechanics.

When applied to a draining tank (Torricelli's theorem), Bernoulli's equation produces a first-order nonlinear ODE governing the fluid height. Unlike the exponential decay of linear systems, the tank level follows a parabolic law — reaching zero in finite time. This subtle difference reveals the profound impact of nonlinearity.

∂ Derivation from Bernoulli's Principle
Bernoulli's Equation Along a Streamline

For steady, incompressible, inviscid flow, apply Bernoulli between the tank free surface (point 1, height $h$) and the orifice exit (point 2, height $0$):

$$P_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 + \rho g h \;=\; P_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2 + 0$$

Both surfaces are exposed to atmosphere: $P_1 = P_2 = P_{\text{atm}}$. Atmospheric pressure cancels from both sides.

Apply the Wide-Tank Approximation (Torricelli's Theorem)

By mass continuity: $A v_1 = a v_2$. For $A \gg a$, the surface speed $v_1 \approx 0$. The Bernoulli equation then reduces to:

$$\rho g h = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2 \implies v_{\text{exit}} = \sqrt{2gh}$$

This is Torricelli's theorem (1643): the exit speed equals the speed of a body in free fall from height $h$, regardless of the fluid's density.

Volume Conservation — Continuity Equation

The rate of volume decrease inside the tank must equal the volumetric flow rate leaving through the orifice:

$$-A\frac{dh}{dt} = Q_{\text{out}} = C_d \cdot a \cdot v_{\text{exit}} = C_d\,a\sqrt{2gh}$$

The discharge coefficient $C_d \leq 1$ corrects for the vena contracta — the actual jet contracts below the geometric orifice area due to fluid inertia.

The Governing ODE

Rearranging and introducing $k = \dfrac{C_d\,a}{A}\sqrt{2g}$:

$$\frac{dh}{dt} = -k\,h^{1/2} \qquad \text{(1st-order nonlinear, separable, autonomous)}$$

The $h^{1/2}$ nonlinearity is crucial — it means the restoring rate decreases as the tank empties, leading to a finite emptying time rather than asymptotic approach to zero.

Separate Variables and Integrate
$$h^{-1/2}\,dh = -k\,dt \implies \int_{h_0}^{h} u^{-1/2}\,du = -k\int_0^t dt'$$
$$\Bigl[2\sqrt{u}\Bigr]_{h_0}^{h} = -kt \implies 2\sqrt{h} - 2\sqrt{h_0} = -kt$$
$$\sqrt{h(t)} = \sqrt{h_0} - \frac{k}{2}t$$
Exact Solution for Height

Squaring both sides (valid while $\sqrt{h} \geq 0$):

$$\boxed{h(t) = h_0\!\left(1 - \frac{t}{t_e}\right)^{\!2}, \qquad t \in [0,\,t_e]}$$

The emptying time is the moment $\sqrt{h(t_e)} = 0$:

$$t_e = \frac{2\sqrt{h_0}}{k} = \frac{A}{C_d\,a}\sqrt{\frac{2h_0}{g}}$$

For $t > t_e$: $h = 0$ (tank empty). Unlike linear decay, the system reaches zero in finite time.

Exit Velocity and Flow Rate Over Time
$$v_{\text{exit}}(t) = \sqrt{2g\,h(t)} = \underbrace{\sqrt{2g\,h_0}}_{v_{\max}}\!\left(1 - \frac{t}{t_e}\right)$$
$$Q(t) = C_d\,a\,v_{\text{exit}}(t) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \int_0^{t_e} Q\,dt = A\,h_0 \;\checkmark$$

Both the exit velocity and flow rate decay linearly in time — despite the nonlinear ODE. The total volume discharged exactly equals the initial volume $A h_0$, confirming conservation of mass.

Physical Interpretation

Parabolic vs. Exponential Decay

A linear drain rate (e.g. pump at constant flow) would give exponential decay: $h(t) = h_0 e^{-t/\tau}$, never reaching zero. The Bernoulli nonlinearity $(-k\sqrt{h})$ instead drives the height to zero in finite time $t_e$. At low heights, both the pressure head and exit speed diminish together, causing the parabolic slowdown.

$$h_{\text{linear}}(t) = h_0\,e^{-t/\tau} \;\neq\; h_0\!\left(1-\tfrac{t}{t_e}\right)^2$$

Pressure at the Orifice

The gauge pressure driving the jet is $\Delta P = \rho g h(t)$, which converts to kinetic energy. The power delivered to the jet:

$$\dot{W}(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,Q(t)\,v_{\text{exit}}^2(t) = \rho g\,A\,h_0\!\left(1-\tfrac{t}{t_e}\right)^3\frac{2h_0}{t_e}$$

Power is maximum at $t=0$ and drops to zero cubically — the jet weakens rapidly near empty.

∫ Analytical Solution
$$h(t) = h_0\!\left(1 - \frac{t}{t_e}\right)^{\!2}, \qquad t_e = \frac{A}{C_d\,a}\sqrt{\frac{2h_0}{g}}, \quad h = 0 \text{ for } t > t_e$$

🌡 Blackbody Radiation & the Planck Law

A blackbody is an idealised object that absorbs all incident radiation and re-emits energy purely as a function of its temperature. Planck's law (1900) gives the spectral radiance — the power emitted per unit area, per unit solid angle, per unit wavelength — and was the first result to require quantisation of energy, marking the birth of quantum mechanics.

Integrating Planck's law over all wavelengths gives the Stefan–Boltzmann law ($P = \varepsilon\sigma A T^4$), which drives a first-order nonlinear ODE for radiative cooling. The $T^4$ dependence makes this system qualitatively different from Newton's linear cooling: hot objects lose heat far faster, and the dynamics are strongly temperature-dependent.

∂ Derivation: From Planck's Law to the Cooling ODE
Planck's Quantisation Hypothesis

Planck (1900) resolved the ultraviolet catastrophe by assuming electromagnetic energy is emitted in discrete quanta $E = h\nu = hc/\lambda$. Using Bose–Einstein statistics for photons, the mean energy per mode is $\langle E\rangle = hc/\lambda\cdot 1/(e^{hc/\lambda k_BT}-1)$, giving the spectral radiance:

$$B(\lambda,T) = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\cdot\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda k_BT}-1}$$

Units: W sr⁻¹ m⁻² m⁻¹. Two limits: Rayleigh–Jeans ($\lambda\to\infty$): $B\approx 2ck_BT/\lambda^4$; Wien ($\lambda\to0$): $B\approx 2hc^2\lambda^{-5}e^{-hc/\lambda k_BT}$.

Wien's Displacement Law (Peak Wavelength)

Differentiate $B(\lambda,T)$ with respect to $\lambda$ and set to zero. Let $x = hc/\lambda k_BT$:

$$\frac{\partial B}{\partial\lambda}=0 \;\implies\; 5(1-e^{-x}) = x \;\implies\; x \approx 4.9651$$
$$\lambda_{\max} = \frac{hc}{4.9651\,k_B}\cdot\frac{1}{T} = \frac{b}{T}, \quad b = 2.898\times10^{-3}\ \text{m·K}$$

The peak shifts to shorter wavelengths as temperature increases — objects progress from infrared glow to red, orange, yellow, and finally white/blue-hot.

Stefan–Boltzmann Law (Total Power)

Integrate $B(\lambda,T)$ over all wavelengths and solid angles to get total emitted power per unit area. Let $u = hc/\lambda k_BT$:

$$M(T) = \pi\!\int_0^\infty B(\lambda,T)\,d\lambda = \frac{2\pi^5 k_B^4}{15 h^3 c^2}T^4 \equiv \sigma T^4$$
$$P_{\text{emit}} = \varepsilon\sigma A T^4, \qquad \sigma = \frac{2\pi^5 k_B^4}{15h^3c^2} = 5.6704\times10^{-8}\ \frac{\text{W}}{\text{m}^2\text{K}^4}$$

The $T^4$ scaling emerges from integrating the Planck distribution — the Stefan–Boltzmann law is an exact consequence of quantum statistics.

Net Radiative Power and the Energy Balance

An object at $T$ in an environment at $T_\infty$ simultaneously emits and absorbs blackbody radiation. The net power lost is:

$$P_{\text{net}} = \varepsilon\sigma A\!\left(T^4 - T_\infty^4\right)$$

Applying the first law of thermodynamics ($P_{\text{net}} = -mc_p\,dT/dt$):

$$\frac{dT}{dt} = -\frac{\varepsilon\sigma A}{mc_p}\!\left(T^4 - T_\infty^4\right) = -\alpha\!\left(T^4 - T_\infty^4\right)$$
Separating Variables — Implicit Integral Solution

The ODE is separable but yields a transcendental implicit equation:

$$\int_{T_0}^{T}\frac{dT'}{T'^4 - T_\infty^4} = -\alpha\,t$$

Partial fractions: $T^4-T_\infty^4 = (T^2+T_\infty^2)(T+T_\infty)(T-T_\infty)$. After integration:

$$\alpha\,t = \frac{1}{4T_\infty^3}\!\left[\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{T_0+T_\infty}{T+T_\infty}\cdot\frac{T-T_\infty}{T_0-T_\infty} + \arctan\!\frac{T_\infty}{T_0}-\arctan\!\frac{T_\infty}{T}\right]$$

This gives $t$ as a function of $T$ — no closed-form $T(t)$ exists. Numerical methods (RK4) are required to obtain $T(t)$ directly.

Linearisation: Recovery of Newton's Cooling

For small deviations $\Delta T = T - T_\infty \ll T_\infty$, expand $T^4$:

$$T^4 - T_\infty^4 = (T_\infty+\Delta T)^4 - T_\infty^4 \approx 4T_\infty^3\,\Delta T + \mathcal{O}(\Delta T^2)$$
$$\frac{d\Delta T}{dt} \approx -4\alpha T_\infty^3\,\Delta T \;\implies\; T(t)\approx T_\infty + (T_0-T_\infty)\,e^{-t/\tau_\ell}$$

with $\tau_\ell = 1/(4\alpha T_\infty^3)$. This is exactly Newton's law of cooling. The linearisation is accurate when $T \lesssim 1.2\,T_\infty$; for higher $T_0$ the $T^4$ term dominates and cooling is significantly faster than exponential at first.

Power Spectrum Shift During Cooling

As $T$ decreases, two things happen simultaneously in the Planck spectrum: the peak shifts to longer wavelengths (Wien), and the total area under the curve drops as $T^4$ (Stefan–Boltzmann). A red-hot object cools to infrared-glowing and finally becomes invisible to the eye but still radiates thermally.

$$\frac{P(T)}{P(T_0)} = \left(\frac{T}{T_0}\right)^4, \qquad \frac{\lambda_{\max}(T)}{\lambda_{\max}(T_0)} = \frac{T_0}{T}$$
Physical Interpretation

Why $T^4$ Cooling is Faster at High $T$

At $T = 2T_\infty$, radiative power is $2^4 = 16\times$ the absorbed power — a huge net loss. At $T = 1.1T_\infty$, the net loss is only $1.1^4 - 1 \approx 0.46$ times the absorbed power. High-temperature objects shed energy dramatically faster, which is why a steel ingot at 1500 K cools rapidly at first, then slows dramatically near room temperature.

Connection to the CMB

The Cosmic Microwave Background is a near-perfect blackbody spectrum at $T = 2.725\ \text{K}$ ($\lambda_{\max} \approx 1.06\ \text{mm}$). It represents the thermal radiation from the early universe, redshifted by cosmic expansion — the most precise blackbody spectrum ever measured, confirming Planck's law to extraordinary accuracy.

🎱 Billiard Ball Collisions — Conservation Laws

Collisions between billiard balls obey two fundamental conservation laws. For a perfectly elastic collision (e = 1) both linear momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. For a partially inelastic collision (0 < e < 1) momentum is still conserved but kinetic energy is partially lost as heat and sound. The 2-D geometry is resolved by decomposing velocities along the line of centres $\hat{n}$ and the perpendicular tangent $\hat{t}$.

$$\vec{p}_{\rm total}=m_1\vec{v}_1+m_2\vec{v}_2=\text{const}\qquad\text{(always conserved)}$$

$\hat{n}$Unit normal along line of centres at moment of collision—
$\hat{t}$Tangent perpendicular to $\hat{n}$ — frictionless contact, unchanged—
∂ Derivation of Post-Collision Velocities (2-D)
Define unit normal and decompose both velocities; $\hat{n}=(\vec{r}_2-\vec{r}_1)/|\vec{r}_2-\vec{r}_1|$, $\hat{t}=\hat{n}^\perp$
$$v_{in}=\vec{v}_i\cdot\hat{n},\quad v_{it}=\vec{v}_i\cdot\hat{t},\qquad i=1,2$$
Tangential components unchanged (frictionless contact)
$$v_{1t}'=v_{1t},\qquad v_{2t}'=v_{2t}$$
Apply 1-D collision with restitution $e$ along $\hat{n}$ (momentum + COR)
$$v_{1n}'=\frac{(m_1-em_2)\,v_{1n}+(1+e)\,m_2\,v_{2n}}{m_1+m_2},\qquad v_{2n}'=\frac{(m_2-em_1)\,v_{2n}+(1+e)\,m_1\,v_{1n}}{m_1+m_2}$$
Reconstruct full 2-D post-collision velocity vectors
$$\vec{v}_1'=v_{1n}'\hat{n}+v_{1t}'\hat{t},\qquad\vec{v}_2'=v_{2n}'\hat{n}+v_{2t}'\hat{t}$$
Verify total momentum conserved for any $e$
$$m_1\vec{v}_1'+m_2\vec{v}_2'=m_1\vec{v}_1+m_2\vec{v}_2\quad\checkmark$$

① Equal-mass elastic head-on ($m_1=m_2,\;e=1,\;b=0$)

Why does torque depend on sin θ?

The cross product τ = μ×B yields |τ| = μB sin θ. At θ=0° the loop is in equilibrium; maximum torque occurs at θ=90° where the field exerts greatest leverage on both current-carrying sides.

Why do DC motors need commutators?

Without a commutator torque reverses every half-turn and the coil oscillates. The commutator switches current exactly at θ=0, π, keeping torque unidirectional and enabling continuous rotation.

Why do AC motors use rotating fields?

Three-phase currents (120° apart) superpose to produce a resultant B vector rotating at supply frequency. The rotor is dragged electromagnetically — no brushes, no commutator, no mechanical wear.

Energy conversion process

Electrical → magnetic field → mechanical torque → rotation. Losses: I²R copper loss, eddy-current and hysteresis iron loss, and viscous friction (β term). Efficiency η = P_mech / P_in = τω / VI.

🌀🔄 Van der Pol Oscillator — Self-Sustained Oscillation

The Van der Pol equation describes an oscillator with amplitude-dependent damping: near the origin the "damping" is actually negative (pumping energy in), while at large amplitude it becomes strongly positive (dissipating energy). The result is a system that spontaneously grows from rest into a stable, self-sustaining oscillation — and just as remarkably, decays back down to that same oscillation if started too large. Unlike the linear harmonic oscillator (System 2), there is no continuum of possible amplitudes: the system is drawn to one specific periodic orbit regardless of initial condition.

Introduced by Balthasar van der Pol in 1927 while studying vacuum-tube (triode) circuits, this equation is the canonical example of a limit cycle — a robust, isolated closed orbit in phase space that nearby trajectories spiral into (from inside and outside). It underlies models of neuron firing (FitzHugh–Nagumo), cardiac rhythm, and electronic relaxation oscillators.

∂ Derivation & Stability of the Limit Cycle
Origin: Triode (Vacuum-Tube) Circuit

Van der Pol modeled a triode oscillator circuit whose effective resistance depends on the current amplitude — small currents see negative resistance (amplification), large currents see positive resistance (loss). Writing the circuit's charge $q$ with amplitude-dependent damping coefficient $-\mu(1-q^2)$ gives:

$$\ddot{q} - \mu(1-q^2)\dot{q} + \omega_0^2 q = 0$$

Rescaling time by $\omega_0$ and relabeling $q \to x$ gives the standard dimensionless form used throughout this module ($\omega_0 = 1$):

$$\ddot{x} - \mu(1-x^2)\dot{x} + x = 0$$
Rewrite as a First-Order System

Let $v = \dot x$. The state vector $(x,v)$ obeys:

$$\dot{x} = v, \qquad \dot{v} = \mu(1-x^2)v - x$$

This nonlinear system has no elementary closed-form solution — RK4 integration is used throughout the Solution and Simulation tabs.

Liénard Transformation (Smoother Numerical Form)

Define $F(x) = x^3/3 - x$ and $y = v/\mu - F(x)$. Substituting converts the system to the Liénard plane, which removes the large $\mu\,v$ term from the $\dot v$ equation and is far better conditioned for large $\mu$:

$$\dot{x} = \mu\!\left(y - \left(\frac{x^3}{3}-x\right)\right), \qquad \dot{y} = -\frac{x}{\mu}$$
Local Stability Analysis at the Origin

Linearizing $\dot x = v,\; \dot v = \mu v - x$ near $(0,0)$ (using $1-x^2\approx1$) gives characteristic equation $r^2 - \mu r + 1 = 0$:

$$r = \frac{\mu \pm \sqrt{\mu^2-4}}{2}$$

For any $\mu>0$, $\text{Re}(r) > 0$ — the origin is an unstable spiral (or node for $\mu\geq2$). Trajectories starting near rest are pushed outward, not damped toward equilibrium.

Existence of a Stable Limit Cycle (Poincaré–Bendixson)

Since the origin repels nearby trajectories, but the $\mu(1-x^2)$ term becomes strongly dissipative for $|x|>1$ (bounding trajectories from escaping to infinity), the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem guarantees a closed periodic orbit must exist in the annular trapping region between these two behaviors — and Liénard's theorem confirms it is unique and stable. All trajectories, from inside or outside, converge onto this one closed curve.

Weakly Nonlinear Limit — Amplitude Equation ($\mu \ll 1$)

Using the method of multiple scales / averaging, write $x(t) \approx A(t)\cos(t+\phi)$ with slowly varying amplitude $A$. Averaging the energy balance over one fast cycle yields:

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{\mu}{2}A\!\left(1 - \frac{A^2}{4}\right)$$

This is exactly the logistic equation (System 9) for the amplitude $A$! It has an unstable fixed point at $A=0$ and a globally stable fixed point at $A^*=2$ — explaining why the limit-cycle amplitude approaches 2 regardless of starting amplitude, for small $\mu$.

Strongly Nonlinear Limit — Relaxation Oscillation ($\mu \gg 1$)

For large $\mu$, the Liénard-plane dynamics split into slow drift along the cubic nullcline $y = x^3/3 - x$ and fast horizontal jumps between its branches whenever a fold is reached. The period is dominated by the slow phase and grows asymptotically as:

$$T(\mu) \;\approx\; \mu\!\left(3 - 2\ln 2\right) \;\approx\; 1.614\,\mu \qquad (\mu \to \infty)$$

This produces the characteristic "slow build-up, sudden discharge" waveform seen in relaxation oscillators, and is structurally analogous to integrate-and-fire neuron models.

🎯💨 Projectile Motion with Quadratic (Newton) Drag

System 1 modeled a falling object with drag linear in velocity ($F_d \propto v$, Stokes' law) — valid at low Reynolds number and solvable in closed form. Real projectiles moving through air at ordinary speeds (baseballs, artillery shells, thrown rocks) sit instead in the high-Reynolds-number regime, where drag scales with $v^2$ (Newton drag). This single change — from linear to quadratic — destroys the closed-form solution and couples the horizontal and vertical equations of motion, since the drag on each component depends on the total speed $v=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}$, not on that component alone.

The result is a genuinely two-dimensional nonlinear system: trajectories become asymmetric (steeper on the way down than on the way up), maximum range no longer occurs at 45°, and no elementary formula gives the range, time of flight, or impact angle — RK4 integration is required for the general case.

∂ Derivation from Newton's Second Law
Newton (Quadratic) Drag Force

At high Reynolds number, the drag force magnitude is $F_d = \tfrac12 \rho C_D A\, v^2$, directed opposite to the velocity vector $\mathbf{v}=(v_x,v_y)$:

$$\mathbf{F}_d = -\tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_D A\, v\,\mathbf{v}, \qquad v = |\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}$$

Note the extra factor of $v$ compared to linear drag: the force is proportional to $v^2$ in magnitude, but as a vector it's $v\,\mathbf{v}$, which is what introduces the $\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}$ coupling into every component.

Newton's Second Law — Component Form

With gravity acting only in $y$, and defining $b=\tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_D A/m$:

$$m\dot v_x = -\tfrac12\rho C_D A\, v\,v_x \;\Rightarrow\; \dot v_x = -b\,v\,v_x$$
$$m\dot v_y = -mg - \tfrac12\rho C_D A\, v\,v_y \;\Rightarrow\; \dot v_y = -g - b\,v\,v_y$$
Contrast with Linear Drag (System 1)

For linear (Stokes) drag, $\dot v_x = -(b_{\rm lin}/m)v_x$ and $\dot v_y = -g-(b_{\rm lin}/m)v_y$ are decoupled — each component decays independently and both admit the closed-form exponential solution from System 1. Here, both equations share the same factor $v=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}$, so $v_x$ and $v_y$ cannot be solved independently — this coupling is the essential nonlinearity.

Special Case: Pure Vertical Fall (Dropped from Rest)

With $v_x\equiv 0$, $\dot v_y = -g + b v_y^2$ (falling, $v_y<0$; write $s=-v_y\geq0$): $\dot s = g - bs^2$. Separating variables and using $v_T=\sqrt{g/b}$:

$$s(t) = v_T\tanh\!\left(\frac{gt}{v_T}\right) \qquad\text{(closed form — falling only)}$$
Special Case: Pure Vertical Rise (Thrown Straight Up)

Going up, both gravity and drag decelerate the projectile: $\dot v_y = -g - bv_y^2$. Separating variables:

$$v_y(t) = v_T\tan\!\left(\arctan\!\frac{v_0}{v_T} - \frac{gt}{v_T}\right), \qquad t_{\rm apex}=\frac{v_T}{g}\arctan\!\frac{v_0}{v_T}$$

These two closed forms (rise, then fall from the apex) fully solve the 1-D vertical throw, but the moment a horizontal component is added, $v=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}$ mixes the equations and no such closed form survives.

General 2-D Case — First-Order System for RK4

State vector $\mathbf{q}=(x,y,v_x,v_y)$:

$$\dot x = v_x,\quad \dot y = v_y,\quad \dot v_x = -b\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}\,v_x,\quad \dot v_y = -g-b\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}\,v_y$$

Integrated numerically from launch until $y$ returns to $0$ (ground impact, located by linear interpolation between the last two RK4 steps for sub-step precision).

🪐☀️ Kepler / Two-Body Orbital Motion

The two-body problem — one mass orbiting another under mutual gravity — is one of the few genuinely nonlinear systems in physics that admits a complete closed-form solution. Reduced to the motion of one body relative to the other (or, equivalently, a test particle orbiting a fixed central mass), the governing equation is a central force $\mathbf{F}\propto -\hat{\mathbf{r}}/r^2$ — and remarkably, this specific power law is one of only two force laws (the other being the harmonic oscillator, $F\propto -r$) for which every bound orbit is a closed curve (Bertrand's theorem).

Conservation of angular momentum and energy — both automatic consequences of the central, conservative nature of gravity — are enough to fully classify and solve the motion, producing Kepler's three empirical laws of planetary motion as direct mathematical corollaries.

Kepler's Three Laws

📜 I. Law of Ellipses: Every bound orbit is an ellipse with the central mass at one focus, never at the center.
📜 II. Law of Equal Areas: A line from the central mass to the orbiting body sweeps out equal areas in equal times — a direct restatement of angular-momentum conservation ($dA/dt = L/2 = \text{const}$).
📜 III. Law of Harmonies: The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, $T^2 \propto a^3$, with the same constant of proportionality for every body orbiting the same central mass.
⚠️ Bertrand's theorem: Among all possible central-force laws, only $F\propto 1/r^2$ (gravity/Coulomb) and $F\propto r$ (harmonic oscillator, System 2/16) produce orbits that are always closed curves for any bound initial condition. Almost any other power-law force — even $F\propto 1/r^{2.01}$ — produces orbits that precess and never close, tracing out a rosette instead of a fixed ellipse.
∂ Derivation — Conservation Laws and the Orbit Equation
Newton's Law of Gravitation → Equation of Motion

The gravitational force on a mass $m$ at position $\mathbf{r}$ from a central mass $M$ is $\mathbf{F}=-\dfrac{GMm}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}$. Dividing by $m$ gives the (mass-independent) equation of motion:

$$\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = -\frac{GM}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}} = -\frac{GM}{r^3}\mathbf{r}$$
Angular Momentum is Conserved (Central Force ⇒ Zero Torque)

Since $\mathbf{F}$ is always parallel to $\mathbf{r}$, the torque $\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}$. Therefore $\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v}$ is exactly conserved — both in direction (motion stays in a fixed plane) and magnitude:

$$\frac{d\mathbf{L}}{dt} = \mathbf{r}\times\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{r}\times\left(-\frac{GM}{r^3}\mathbf{r}\right) = \mathbf{0} \;\Rightarrow\; L = r^2\dot\phi = \text{const}$$

This immediately gives Kepler's Second Law: the areal sweep rate $dA/dt = \tfrac12 r^2\dot\phi = L/2$ is constant.

Energy is Conserved (Conservative Force)

Gravity derives from a potential $U(r)=-GM/r$ (per unit mass), so the specific mechanical energy is conserved:

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{r}} - \frac{GM}{r} = \text{const} \qquad \text{(vis-viva relation once orbit shape is known)}$$
The Key Substitution — Binet's Equation

Working in polar coordinates $(r,\phi)$ in the orbital plane, define $u=1/r$. Using $\dot\phi = L u^2$ (from angular momentum conservation) to convert time-derivatives into $\phi$-derivatives via the chain rule $\dfrac{d}{dt}=Lu^2\dfrac{d}{d\phi}$, the radial equation of motion transforms into:

$$\frac{d^2u}{d\phi^2} + u = \frac{GM}{L^2}$$

This is the single most important simplification in orbital mechanics: a genuinely nonlinear ODE in time becomes a linear ODE in angle — structurally identical to a driven harmonic oscillator (System 2) with the "driving" being the constant $GM/L^2$.

Solve the Linear ODE

The general solution is the particular solution (constant) plus the homogeneous harmonic solution:

$$u(\phi) = \frac{GM}{L^2} + C\cos(\phi-\phi_0)$$

Converting back to $r=1/u$ and defining $p=L^2/GM$ (semi-latus rectum) and $e = CL^2/GM$ (eccentricity, from the initial conditions):

$$\boxed{r(\phi) = \frac{p}{1+e\cos(\phi-\phi_0)}}$$

This is exactly the polar equation of a conic section with one focus at the origin — an ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola depending on $e$.

Relating $e$ and $a$ to the Conserved Quantities $E,L$

Evaluating $r(\phi)$ at periapsis/apoapsis and matching to the vis-viva energy equation gives the closed-form eccentricity and semi-major axis directly in terms of the two conserved quantities:

$$a = -\frac{GM}{2E}\quad(E<0\text{ bound}), \qquad e = \sqrt{1+\frac{2EL^2}{(GM)^2}}$$
Kepler's Third Law from the Ellipse Area

An ellipse of semi-major axis $a$ and eccentricity $e$ has semi-minor axis $b=a\sqrt{1-e^2}$ and area $\pi a b$. Since the areal sweep rate is the constant $L/2$, the period is total-area divided by rate:

$$T = \frac{\pi a b}{L/2} = \frac{2\pi a b}{L} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}a^3$$

The proportionality constant $4\pi^2/GM$ depends only on the central mass — identical for every orbiting body, exactly as Kepler observed for the planets around the Sun.

🚀 The Rocket Equation — Newton's Second Law with Variable Mass

Every ODE system so far has treated mass as a fixed parameter. A rocket breaks that assumption: it continuously ejects propellant, so its own mass $m(t)$ is a dynamical quantity that shrinks as the engine burns. Newton's second law must be applied to the whole system — rocket plus the exhaust it has just expelled — because momentum, not force on a fixed body, is what is truly conserved.

The result, derived independently by Konstantin Tsiolkovsky (1903), Robert Goddard, and Hermann Oberth, is deceptively simple: thrust arises purely from the rate of mass ejection times the exhaust speed relative to the rocket, with no external medium "pushed off". It is the founding equation of astronautics.

∂ Derivation from Conservation of Momentum
System at Time $t$ and $t+dt$

At time $t$ the rocket has mass $m$ and velocity $v$. In time $dt$ it ejects mass $-dm>0$ (since $dm<0$) backward at velocity $v-v_e$ relative to the ground (speed $v_e$ relative to the rocket), while the rocket itself accelerates to $v+dv$ with new mass $m+dm$.

Conserve Total Momentum of Rocket + Ejected Gas
$$mv = (m+dm)(v+dv) + (-dm)(v-v_e) - mg\,dt$$

The $-mg\,dt$ term is the external gravitational impulse on the whole system during $dt$.

Expand and Drop Second-Order Terms ($dm\,dv\to0$)
$$mv = mv + m\,dv + v\,dm - v\,dm + v_e\,dm - mg\,dt$$
$$m\,dv = -v_e\,dm - mg\,dt$$
Divide by $dt$ — the Rocket Equation
$$m\frac{dv}{dt} = -v_e\frac{dm}{dt} - mg = -\dot m\,v_e - mg$$

Note $\dot m<0$ during burn, so $-\dot m\,v_e = \mu v_e = F$ is a positive thrust pushing the rocket forward — no external medium is needed, unlike a propeller or wheel.

Constant Burn Rate — Prescribe $m(t)$

For a constant mass-flow rate $\mu=(m_0-m_f)/t_{burn}$, the mass decreases linearly while burning:

$$m(t) = m_0-\mu t, \qquad 0\le t\le t_{burn}$$

This turns the rocket equation into a linear, time-varying-coefficient ODE in $v$ alone — genuinely solvable in closed form, unlike most of the coupled nonlinear systems elsewhere in this module.

Integrate for Velocity (Tsiolkovsky's Equation, with Gravity Loss)
$$\frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{\mu v_e}{m_0-\mu t} - g$$
$$\boxed{v(t) = v_e\ln\!\frac{m_0}{m(t)} - gt}$$

Setting $g=0$ recovers the classic vacuum form $\Delta v = v_e\ln(m_0/m_f)$ — independent of burn time, only the mass ratio matters.

Integrate Again for Altitude

Integrating $v(t)$ from $0$ to $t$ (by parts, using $\int\ln(m_0-\mu s)\,ds$) gives a closed form in terms of $m(t)$:

$$h(t) = v_e t - \frac{v_e\,m(t)}{\mu}\ln\!\frac{m_0}{m(t)} - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$$

After burnout ($t>t_{burn}$), $\mu=0$ and the rocket becomes an ordinary constant-mass projectile (System 1 with $b=0$): $v(t)=v_b-g(t-t_{burn})$, $h(t)=h_b+v_b(t-t_{burn})-\tfrac12g(t-t_{burn})^2$.

Staging & the Δv Budget

Why Multi-Stage Rockets Exist

Each stage's $\Delta v$ adds linearly even though each depends only logarithmically on its own mass ratio: dropping dead tanks between stages lets the next stage's $v_e\ln R$ apply to a smaller $m_0$, avoiding the exponential penalty of hauling empty structure all the way to orbit.

$$\Delta v_{\rm total}=\sum_{i=1}^{N} v_{e,i}\ln\!\frac{m_{0,i}}{m_{f,i}}$$

Thrust-to-Weight at Ignition

Liftoff requires thrust to exceed the initial weight — the hardest instant, since the rocket is at its heaviest and hasn't yet burned off any mass:

$$\frac{F}{m_0g}=\frac{\mu v_e}{m_0 g} > 1 \quad\text{(necessary for liftoff)}$$
∫ Analytical Solution
$$m(t)=m_0-\mu t,\qquad v(t)=v_e\ln\!\frac{m_0}{m(t)}-gt,\qquad h(t)=v_et-\frac{v_e\,m(t)}{\mu}\ln\!\frac{m_0}{m(t)}-\tfrac12gt^2$$

valid for $0\le t\le t_{burn}$, with $\mu=(m_0-m_f)/t_{burn}$. Beyond burnout the rocket coasts ballistically at constant mass $m_f$.

🌊📈 The 1-D Wave Equation — Vibrating String

Every system so far has been an ODE: a single quantity evolving in time. The wave equation is different — it is a partial differential equation (PDE) governing a field $y(x,t)$ that depends on both position and time simultaneously. A taut string, an organ pipe of air, a plucked guitar string, and a vibrating drumhead (in 2-D) all obey the same equation: the restoring force from tension is proportional to the local curvature of the string, not its displacement.

Unlike the diffusion-type PDEs (heat equation), the wave equation is time-reversible and non-dissipative: energy sloshes between kinetic and potential forms but is never lost. Its solutions are disturbances that propagate at a fixed speed $c$ without changing shape (d'Alembert's solution) — the mathematical embodiment of "a wave."

∂ Derivation from Newton's Second Law
Isolate a String Element

Consider a small element of string between $x$ and $x+dx$, mass $\mu\,dx$, under tension $T$ pulling tangentially at each end. For small transverse displacements, the slope $\partial y/\partial x$ is small, so $T$ itself stays essentially constant along the string.

Net Transverse Force

The vertical component of tension at each end is $T\sin\theta\approx T\,\partial y/\partial x$ (small-angle). The net upward force on the element is the difference between the right and left ends:

$$F_y = T\left[\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\bigg|_{x+dx} - \frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\bigg|_{x}\right] \approx T\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}\,dx$$
Newton's Second Law on the Element

Mass times acceleration equals net force:

$$(\mu\,dx)\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = T\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}\,dx$$
The Wave Equation

Cancel $dx$ and define $c^2 = T/\mu$:

$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = c^2\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2}$$
Separation of Variables — Standing Waves

Seeking solutions $y(x,t)=X(x)T(t)$ and substituting splits the PDE into two ODEs, each equal to a separation constant $-k^2$:

$$X''+k^2X=0, \qquad \ddot{T}+(ck)^2T=0$$

Applying the fixed-end BCs $X(0)=X(L)=0$ forces $k_n = n\pi/L$, giving the normal modes:

$$X_n(x)=\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), \qquad \omega_n = c k_n = \frac{n\pi c}{L}, \qquad n=1,2,3,\dots$$
General Solution — Fourier Superposition

The full solution is a sum over all normal modes, each oscillating at its own frequency:

$$y(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\Big[A_n\cos(\omega_n t)+B_n\sin(\omega_n t)\Big]$$

The coefficients $A_n,B_n$ are fixed by the initial shape $y(x,0)$ and initial velocity $\partial y/\partial t(x,0)$ via Fourier sine series.

D'Alembert's Traveling-Wave Solution

Equivalently, the general solution to the unbounded wave equation is any sum of a right- and left-moving profile:

$$y(x,t) = f(x-ct) + g(x+ct)$$

Standing waves on a finite string are exactly the superposition of a right-moving and left-moving copy of the same pulse, reflecting back and forth off the fixed ends.

Energy in the Vibrating String

Kinetic & Potential Energy Densities

$$\text{KE density} = \tfrac{1}{2}\mu\!\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right)^{\!2}, \quad \text{PE density} = \tfrac{1}{2}T\!\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right)^{\!2}$$

Total energy $E=\int_0^L(\text{KE}+\text{PE})\,dx$ is exactly conserved for the undamped string — the discrete analogue of the harmonic oscillator's constant $E_0=\tfrac12kx_0^2$ (System 17), now summed continuously along the string.

Harmonic Series

Because $f_n=nf_1$, the overtones are exact integer multiples of the fundamental — the physical basis of musical pitch and timbre. The relative amplitudes $A_n$ (set by pluck/strike position and shape) determine the tone color.

$$f_1=\frac{c}{2L}=\frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}$$
∫ Analytical Solution & Numerical Scheme
$$y(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\Big[A_n\cos(\omega_n t)+B_n\sin(\omega_n t)\Big], \qquad \omega_n=\frac{n\pi c}{L}$$

Fourier Coefficients from Initial Conditions

$$A_n=\frac{2}{L}\int_0^L y(x,0)\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx$$
$$B_n=\frac{2}{n\pi c}\int_0^L \dot{y}(x,0)\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx$$

Plucked String (Triangular IC)

For a pluck at fraction $p=x_0/L$ of the length, released from rest, the Fourier coefficients have a compact closed form:

$$A_n=\frac{2h}{n^2\pi^2\,p(1-p)}\sin(n\pi p), \qquad B_n=0$$

Odd harmonics dominate for a center pluck ($p=0.5$); plucking near an end excites many more harmonics — brighter, twangier tone.

Numerical Method: Finite-Difference (Leapfrog) Scheme

Discretizing $x$ into $N$ points ($\Delta x = L/N$) and using the standard second-difference approximation for both derivatives:

$$\frac{y_i^{n+1}-2y_i^n+y_i^{n-1}}{\Delta t^2} = c^2\,\frac{y_{i+1}^n-2y_i^n+y_{i-1}^n}{\Delta x^2}$$
$$y_i^{n+1} = 2y_i^n - y_i^{n-1} + r^2\left(y_{i+1}^n-2y_i^n+y_{i-1}^n\right), \qquad r=\frac{c\,\Delta t}{\Delta x}$$

This explicit leapfrog scheme is second-order accurate in both space and time and matches the Fourier-mode solution to numerical precision when the CFL condition below is satisfied.

CFL Stability Condition

$$r=\frac{c\,\Delta t}{\Delta x}\leq 1 \qquad \text{(Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition)}$$

If $r>1$, the numerical domain of dependence is smaller than the true physical domain of dependence, and the scheme blows up exponentially — a discretization instability, not a physical effect. This simulation clamps $\Delta t$ so that $r=1$ exactly (the "magic" CFL value at which the finite-difference scheme reproduces the exact solution up to round-off).

🔥📉 The 1-D Heat Equation — Conduction in a Rod

System 26 introduced the wave equation, a hyperbolic PDE that conserves energy and propagates disturbances without decay. The heat equation is its parabolic cousin: instead of curvature driving acceleration, curvature drives a direct rate of change. The result is qualitatively opposite behavior — no oscillation, no wave propagation at finite speed, and every initial profile smooths out monotonically toward thermal equilibrium.

A thin, laterally insulated rod of length $L$ conducts heat only along its axis. Temperature $u(x,t)$ obeys Fourier's law of heat conduction combined with local energy conservation, producing a single linear PDE that governs everything from a cooling metal bar to diffusion of a pollutant, to the smoothing of noisy signals (heat-equation blurring in image processing) and even the Black–Scholes PDE (System 3–6 of your econophysics/options work) after a change of variables.

∂ Derivation from Fourier's Law & Energy Conservation
Fourier's Law of Heat Conduction

Heat flux $q$ (energy per unit area per unit time) flows from hot to cold, proportional to the local temperature gradient:

$$q(x,t) = -k\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$$
Energy Balance on a Thin Slice $[x,x+dx]$

The rate of change of thermal energy in the slice equals the net flux in minus flux out:

$$\rho c_p A\,dx\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = A\big[q(x,t)-q(x+dx,t)\big] = -A\,\frac{\partial q}{\partial x}\,dx$$
Substitute Fourier's Law
$$\rho c_p\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = k\,\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$$
The Heat Equation

Dividing by $\rho c_p$ and defining $\alpha=k/(\rho c_p)$:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha\,\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$$
Separation of Variables — Normal Modes

Seeking $u(x,t)=X(x)T(t)$ and substituting splits the PDE into two ODEs equal to a separation constant $-\lambda$:

$$X'' + \lambda X = 0, \qquad \dot{T} + \alpha\lambda T = 0$$

Applying the fixed-end BCs $X(0)=X(L)=0$ forces $\lambda_n=(n\pi/L)^2$, giving the eigenfunctions:

$$X_n(x)=\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), \qquad T_n(t) = \exp\!\left(-\alpha\!\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^{\!2} t\right), \qquad n=1,2,3,\dots$$
General Solution — Fourier Sine Series
$$u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n\,\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\exp\!\left(-\alpha\!\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^{\!2}t\right)$$

The coefficients $B_n$ are fixed by the initial temperature profile $u(x,0)$ via the standard Fourier sine-series projection:

$$B_n=\frac{2}{L}\int_0^L u(x,0)\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx$$
Decay Time Constants

Each mode decays independently with its own time constant $\tau_n=L^2/(n^2\pi^2\alpha)=\tau_1/n^2$. The fundamental ($n=1$) decays slowest, so at long times the profile approaches a pure half-sine hump regardless of the initial shape's fine detail:

$$u(x,t) \;\xrightarrow{t\gg\tau_1}\; B_1\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right)e^{-t/\tau_1}$$

🌊Ψ The Time-Dependent Schrödinger Equation

Systems 26 and 27 covered the two classical PDE archetypes: the non-dissipative, non-dispersive wave equation and the dissipative, non-oscillatory heat equation. The Schrödinger equation is the third archetype — a complex-valued, linear, dispersive PDE. Unlike the wave equation, different wavenumbers travel at different phase speeds, so a localized wave packet doesn't just propagate — it spreads, interferes with itself, and (in a bound potential) periodically refocuses.

This module evolves a Gaussian wave packet $\psi(x,t)$ in two canonical 1-D potentials: the infinite square well (particle in a box) and the quantum harmonic oscillator (QHO) — the same two systems whose stationary states you've likely built standalone solvers for (Numerov / FD bound-state code). Here the focus is the dynamics: how a superposition of those stationary states evolves in time.

∂ Separation of Variables & Stationary States
Separate $\psi(x,t)=X(x)T(t)$

Substituting into the Schrödinger equation and dividing by $XT$ separates space and time, both equal to a constant $E$ (the energy eigenvalue):

$$-\frac{1}{2}\varphi_n''(x) + V(x)\varphi_n(x) = E_n\varphi_n(x), \qquad T_n(t)=e^{-iE_nt}$$

This is exactly the eigenvalue problem your standalone Numerov / finite-difference bound-state solvers already handle — the difference here is what's done with the eigenbasis afterward.

$$\varphi_n(x)=\left(\frac{\omega}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n n!}}H_n(\sqrt{\omega}\,x)\,e^{-\omega x^2/2}, \qquad E_n=\left(n+\tfrac12\right)\omega$$

$H_n$ are the Hermite polynomials from your existing Hermite/rovibrational reference work — the QHO wavefunctions here are the special case $v=0,1,2,\dots$ of the same family.

General Time Evolution

Any initial state expands in the eigenbasis, $\psi(x,0)=\sum_n c_n\varphi_n(x)$ with $c_n=\int\varphi_n^*(x)\psi(x,0)\,dx$. Because each stationary state simply acquires a phase, the full time-dependent solution is:

$$\psi(x,t)=\sum_n c_n\,\varphi_n(x)\,e^{-iE_n t}$$

Each term rotates in the complex plane at its own frequency $E_n$ — interference between terms of different $n$ is exactly what makes the packet move, spread, and (in the box) partially or fully revive at the quantum revival time $t_{\rm rev}=4L^2$.

Ehrenfest's Theorem (Exact for the QHO)

Why Not Explicit FTCS?

Unlike the heat equation (System 27), naive explicit time-stepping of the Schrödinger equation is unconditionally unstable — the $i$ in front of $\partial_t$ makes it behave like an undamped oscillator in every Fourier mode, not a decaying one. A stable explicit scheme needs the real and imaginary parts to be interleaved in time.

Visscher's Staggered Leapfrog Algorithm

Writing $\psi=\psi_R+i\psi_I$ splits the Schrödinger equation into two coupled real PDEs:

$$\frac{\partial \psi_R}{\partial t} = H\psi_I, \qquad \frac{\partial \psi_I}{\partial t} = -H\psi_R, \qquad H\psi \equiv -\tfrac12\psi''+V\psi$$

Visscher (1991) staggers $\psi_R$ at integer steps $t_n=n\Delta t$ and $\psi_I$ at half-steps $t_{n+1/2}$:

$$\psi_R^{n+1} = \psi_R^{n} + \Delta t\,H\psi_I^{\,n+1/2}$$ $$\psi_I^{\,n+3/2} = \psi_I^{\,n+1/2} - \Delta t\,H\psi_R^{n+1}$$

with $H$ discretized via the standard 3-point Laplacian $\;(H\psi)_i=-\tfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\psi_{i+1}-2\psi_i+\psi_{i-1}}{\Delta x^2}+V_i\psi_i$. The probability density at integer steps uses the products of the sandwiching half-steps, which conserves norm to high accuracy:

$$|\psi_i^{\,n}|^2 \approx \left(\psi_R^{n}\right)^2 + \psi_I^{\,n-1/2}\,\psi_I^{\,n+1/2}$$

Stability Condition

$$\Delta t\left(\frac{2}{\Delta x^2}+V_{\max}\right)\leq 2$$

This simulation automatically clamps $\Delta t$ to satisfy this bound for the chosen grid and potential, the direct analog of the CFL condition (System 26) and the $r\le\tfrac12$ diffusion limit (System 27) — every PDE in this collection turns out to have its own explicit-scheme stability ceiling.

🔄🔗 The Kuramoto Model — Spontaneous Synchronization

Coupled oscillators and N-mass Coupled oscillators chain systems coupled identical oscillators through springs — every oscillator has the same natural frequency, and the coupling only redistributes energy between fixed normal modes. The Kuramoto model asks a different question: what happens when a population of oscillators has randomly scattered natural frequencies and only interacts through the phase of its neighbors, not amplitude? Below a critical coupling strength, oscillators drift independently and stay incoherent forever. Above it, they suddenly lock into a common frequency and march in step — a genuine phase transition with no counterpart in the linear coupled-spring systems.

Introduced by Yoshiki Kuramoto in 1975, this is the canonical minimal model for synchronization phenomena: flashing fireflies, clapping audiences, pacemaker cells in the heart, power-grid generators, and coupled Josephson junctions all show the same onset behavior.

∂ Mean-Field Reduction & the Self-Consistency Equation
Define the Complex Order Parameter

Package the population's collective state into a single complex number — the centroid of all phases on the unit circle:

$$re^{i\psi} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N e^{i\theta_j}$$

$r$ measures phase coherence ($r=0$: uniformly spread; $r=1$: all identical), and $\psi$ is the mean phase.

Rewrite the Coupling Term Using $r,\psi$

Multiply the order-parameter definition by $e^{-i\theta_i}$ and take the imaginary part:

$$r\sin(\psi-\theta_i) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_j \sin(\theta_j-\theta_i)$$

Substituting back into the original $N$-body equation collapses the all-to-all sum into a single mean-field term — each oscillator only "feels" the collective $(r,\psi)$, not every other oscillator individually:

$$\dot\theta_i = \omega_i + Kr\sin(\psi-\theta_i)$$

This is the heart of the mean-field simplification: an $N$-body problem becomes $N$ independent oscillators, each driven by a common self-consistently determined field.

Locked vs. Drifting Oscillators (steady state, $\psi=0$ WLOG)

In the rotating frame at the mean frequency $\Omega=\langle\omega\rangle$, a steady locked state has $\dot\theta_i=0$:

$$\omega_i = Kr\sin\theta_i \implies \theta_i=\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\omega_i}{Kr}\right)\quad\text{if } |\omega_i|\le Kr$$

Oscillators with $|\omega_i|>Kr$ cannot satisfy this — they keep drifting, just non-uniformly (spending more time near where $\sin\theta_i\approx\omega_i/Kr$).

Self-Consistency Equation for $r$

The locked oscillators alone must reproduce the assumed order parameter $r$. Summing (integrating) their contribution against the frequency distribution $g(\omega)$:

$$r = Kr\int_{-Kr}^{Kr} \cos\theta(\omega)\,g(\omega)\,d\omega = Kr\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^2\phi\;g(Kr\sin\phi)\,d\phi$$

($\theta=\phi$ substitution, $\omega=Kr\sin\phi$.) This is a nonlinear equation for $r$ given $K$ — the drifting oscillators contribute nothing on average by symmetry, in the stationary limit.

Critical Coupling from Linearizing at $r\to0^+$

As $r\to0$, the self-consistency equation reduces to $1 = K\pi g(0)/2$, giving the sync threshold purely from the density of oscillators with near-zero natural frequency:

$$K_c = \frac{2}{\pi g(0)}$$
Exact Solution for a Lorentzian $g(\omega)$

This module draws $\omega_i$ from a Lorentzian (Cauchy) distribution $g(\omega)=\dfrac{\gamma/\pi}{\omega^2+\gamma^2}$, chosen because the self-consistency integral above has a rare closed form:

$$K_c = 2\gamma, \qquad r_\infty(K) = \sqrt{1-\frac{K_c}{K}}\quad\text{for } K>K_c$$

This square-root branching curve is the signature of the Kuramoto transition — steep just above $K_c$, flattening toward $r\to1$ as $K\to\infty$.

🌀🔗 The Elastic Pendulum — Resonance Between Bounce and Swing

The elastic pendulum is a mass hanging from a spring that is free to both stretch and swing — literally System 2 (harmonic oscillator) and System 3 (pendulum) sharing a single degree of freedom apiece, coupled through the mass's own inertia. Written in polar-like coordinates, it looks deceptively simple: a radial spring equation and an angular pendulum equation. But the coupling terms — centrifugal stretching $(L_0+r)\dot\theta^2$ and Coriolis-like $2\dot r\dot\theta$ — make the two motions exchange energy, especially when the spring's natural bounce frequency is close to twice the pendulum's swing frequency.

At this 2:1 internal resonance, a mass released from a pure vertical bounce is dynamically unstable: within a few cycles, the vertical oscillation collapses and the pendulum swings wildly, then swings back down and re-excites the bounce — a slow, hypnotic back-and-forth first analyzed by Vitt & Gorelik in 1933. Push the amplitude higher and the same 2:1-resonant system stops repeating altogether and becomes chaotic — one of the simplest mechanical systems that transitions from clean periodic energy-sharing to genuine chaos as a single parameter (amplitude) is turned up.

∂ Lagrangian Derivation
Coordinates and Kinetic Energy

Let $r$ be the spring's extension beyond its natural length $L_0$, so the instantaneous pendulum length is $L_0+r$, and $\theta$ the angle from vertical. The mass position is $x=(L_0+r)\sin\theta,\; y=(L_0+r)\cos\theta$ (downward positive). Differentiating and combining:

$$T=\tfrac{1}{2}m\!\left[\dot r^2+(L_0+r)^2\dot\theta^2\right]$$
Potential Energy — Spring Plus Gravity
$$V=\tfrac{1}{2}kr^2 - mg(L_0+r)\cos\theta$$
Lagrangian and Euler–Lagrange Equations

With $\mathcal{L}=T-V$, apply $\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot q}-\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q}=0$ for $q=r,\theta$:

$$m\ddot r - m(L_0+r)\dot\theta^2 + kr - mg\cos\theta = 0$$
$$m\!\left[(L_0+r)^2\ddot\theta + 2(L_0+r)\dot r\dot\theta\right] + mg(L_0+r)\sin\theta = 0$$
Simplify to the Governing System
$$\ddot r = (L_0+r)\dot\theta^2 - \frac{k}{m}r + g\cos\theta$$ $$\ddot\theta = -\frac{2\dot r\dot\theta + g\sin\theta}{L_0+r}$$

The first equation is System 2's spring equation plus a centrifugal-stretching term $(L_0+r)\dot\theta^2$. The second is System 3's pendulum equation, modified by a "Coriolis-like" damping term $2\dot r\dot\theta/(L_0+r)$ and a time-varying effective length. Setting the coupling terms to zero recovers both parent systems exactly.

Linearize About Equilibrium — Normal Modes Decouple

At equilibrium $r_{eq}=mg/k,\ \theta=0$. Writing $r=r_{eq}+\rho$ with $\rho,\theta$ small, all quadratic coupling terms ($\dot\theta^2$, $\dot r\dot\theta$) drop out at leading order:

$$\ddot\rho + \omega_s^2\rho \approx 0,\qquad \ddot\theta+\omega_p^2\theta\approx0,\qquad \omega_s=\sqrt{k/m},\ \ \omega_p=\sqrt{g/L_0}$$

For truly infinitesimal amplitude, the spring bounces and the pendulum swings completely independently — exactly Systems 2 and 3 side by side. Coupling only becomes dynamically important once amplitude grows enough that the quadratic terms compete with the linear restoring forces.

Resonant Energy Exchange — Multiple-Scales Sketch

Near $\omega_s=2\omega_p+\varepsilon$, writing $\rho(t)=A(t)\cos(\omega_st)$ and $\theta(t)=B(t)\cos(\omega_pt+\phi)$ with slowly varying envelopes $A,B$, the coupling terms resonantly drive an exchange described (to leading order) by:

$$\dot A \propto -AB\sin\Phi,\qquad \dot B\propto AB\sin\Phi$$

where $\Phi$ is a slow relative phase. This has the same structure as the two-mode energy-conservation seen in Lotka–Volterra (System 10) and Van der Pol's amplitude equation (System 22) — envelope $A^2+B^2$ (total energy in each linear mode) is exchanged back and forth rather than dissipated, exactly the "sloshing" behavior seen in the simulation.

∫ Conserved Energy & Numerical Solution
⚠️ No closed-form solution exists once the coupling terms matter — like the double pendulum (System 14) and Lorenz (System 5), this is fundamentally a numerical problem. RK4 integrates the 4-D state $(r,\theta,\dot r,\dot\theta)$ directly.

Conserved Total Mechanical Energy

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}m\!\left[\dot r^2+(L_0+r)^2\dot\theta^2\right] + \tfrac{1}{2}kr^2 - mg(L_0+r)\cos\theta$$

Exactly conserved by the true dynamics; RK4's drift in $E$ over the simulation is reported as an accuracy check, mirroring the double-pendulum energy-drift diagnostic in System 14.

First-Order System for RK4

State vector $\mathbf{q}=(r,\theta,\dot r,\dot\theta)$:

$$\dot r=v_r,\quad \dot\theta=\omega,$$ $$\dot v_r=(L_0+r)\omega^2-\frac{k}{m}r+g\cos\theta,$$ $$\dot\omega=-\frac{2v_r\omega+g\sin\theta}{L_0+r}$$

Diagnosing Order vs. Chaos

As with Lorenz (System 5) and the double pendulum (System 14), a shadow trajectory started with a $10^{-4}$ rad perturbation in $\theta_0$ separates exponentially in the chaotic regime but stays close (bounded, oscillating separation) in the regular, small-amplitude resonant regime — the clearest numerical signature of the order-to-chaos transition.
