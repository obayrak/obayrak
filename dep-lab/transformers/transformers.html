Integral Transform Theory

Transformers

Every named transform in physics, mathematics, and engineering is one instance of a single operation: multiply a function by a kernel and integrate. Change the kernel, change the lens.

The signal, field, or quantity living in the original domain \(x\) — time, position, a random variable.

Integration / Summation

Transform
\(F(s)\)
The output in the new domain \(s\) — frequency, complex frequency, or another dual variable — where the original problem is often trivial to solve.

Fourier Transform Visualizer

Kernel \(K(x,\omega)=e^{-i\omega x}=\cos(\omega x)-i\sin(\omega x)\). Correlates \(f(x)\) against pure oscillations at every frequency \(\omega\); the result \(F(\omega)\) tells you how much of each frequency is present.

Integral accumulation \(\int_{-L}^{x} f(x')K(x',\omega_0)\,dx'\) — the running correlation that becomes \(F(\omega_0)\)
Why this matters: the Fourier transform is the mathematical statement that any signal — sound, an electric field, a quantum wavefunction — can be decomposed into a sum of pure sinusoids. Diagonalizing derivatives (turning \(d/dx \to i\omega\)) is what makes PDEs, filters, and diffraction patterns tractable. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, the Nyquist sampling theorem, JPEG/MP3 compression, and antenna array patterns are all statements about \(f(x)\) and \(F(\omega)\) trading off width for width. A narrow pulse in time must be broad in frequency — this app lets you watch that trade-off happen live.

Quantum Momentum Representation

Kernel \(K(x,p)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\,e^{-ipx/\hbar}\). This is the Fourier transform with a physical rescaling: the position-space wavefunction \(\psi(x)\) and momentum-space wavefunction \(\tilde\psi(p)\) are dual descriptions of the same quantum state, related by \(\tilde\psi(p)=\int\psi(x)K(x,p)\,dx\). The kernel is the momentum eigenstate \(\langle x|p\rangle\).

ψ(x) — Re, Im, |ψ|²
K(x,p) at current p₀ = ħk₀
|ψ̃(p)|² — momentum-space probability density
Why this matters: position and momentum wavefunctions are Fourier duals, so squeezing \(\psi(x)\) (small \(\sigma\)) necessarily broadens \(\tilde\psi(p)\), and vice versa — this is the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, not a separate postulate bolted onto quantum mechanics. The boost wavenumber \(k_0\) shifts the momentum-space peak to \(p_0=\hbar k_0\) via the modulation/shift property of the transform, while leaving the position-space probability density \(|\psi(x)|^2\) untouched — a plane-wave phase carries momentum but no position information. The Gaussian wave packet is the unique minimum-uncertainty state, saturating \(\Delta x\,\Delta p=\hbar/2\); try the infinite square well to see a state with more uncertainty than the minimum.

Radon Transform — Tomographic Projections

Kernel \(K\big((x,y),(\theta,r)\big)=\delta(r-x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta)\). Where the previous tabs transformed a 1D function, the Radon transform projects a 2D density \(\mu(x,y)\) onto a 1D line integral \(p(\theta,r)=\iint\mu(x,y)\,\delta(r-x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta)\,dx\,dy\) — the total attenuation an X-ray beam sees crossing the object at angle \(\theta\), offset \(r\) from center. Sweeping \(\theta\) over \([0,\pi)\) builds a sinogram, the raw data a CT scanner actually measures.

μ(x,y) — phantom being scanned
p(θ,r) at current θ — single projection
Sinogram p(θ,r) — θ (vertical) vs r (horizontal)
Why this matters: a CT or PET scanner never sees \(\mu(x,y)\) directly — it only measures line-integral projections \(p(\theta,r)\) from every angle around the body. Reconstructing the image is the inverse Radon transform, and the key to doing it efficiently is the Fourier Slice Theorem: the 1D Fourier transform of a single projection \(p(\theta,r)\) equals a slice through the 2D Fourier transform of \(\mu(x,y)\) taken at angle \(\theta\). Collect enough angles, fill in enough slices of 2D frequency space, and an inverse 2D Fourier transform (filtered backprojection, in practice) recovers the image — the same master equation from the header, just applied twice, once per dimension.

Green's Function Builder

Kernel \(K(x,\xi)=G(x,\xi)\), the response at \(x\) to a unit point source (a Dirac delta \(\delta(x-\xi)\)) placed at \(\xi\). A linear differential equation \(L[y]=f(x)\) inverts to \(y(x)=\displaystyle\int G(x,\xi)\,f(\xi)\,d\xi\) — solve once for the point-source response, then superpose it over the whole source distribution \(f\) to get the full solution for free.

G(x,ξ) — the kernel, every point-source response at once
G(x,ξ₀) — response to a single unit point source at ξ₀
f(ξ) — the source distribution being applied
y(x) = ∫G(x,ξ)f(ξ)dξ — solution reconstructed by superposing point responses
Why this matters: Green's functions are the "impulse response" idea from engineering translated into the language of differential operators, and they're the reason linearity is worth exploiting — solve \(L[G]=\delta(x-\xi)\) exactly once, and every subsequent problem \(L[y]=f\) for any source \(f\) becomes a single integral. The Green function/propagator row in the reference table is this same object: the quantum propagator is the Green's function of the Schrödinger operator, the electrostatic potential of a point charge is the Green's function of the Laplacian, and a system's impulse response is the Green's function of its governing ODE. Watch the animation closely — each point source contributes its own copy of \(G(x,\xi_k)\), scaled by \(f(\xi_k)\,d\xi\), and the solution curve is nothing more than all of those responses added together.
