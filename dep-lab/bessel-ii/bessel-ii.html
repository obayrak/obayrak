Fluid Mechanics — Pulsatile Flow in Rigid Pipes

From the Navier–Stokes Equations to Bessel's Equation

For axisymmetric, fully-developed flow in a straight rigid pipe (no swirl, no radial velocity), the axial momentum equation reduces to:

$$\rho\,\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial z} + \mu\left(\frac{\partial^2 v_z}{\partial r^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial r}\right)$$

Driven by a periodic pressure gradient $-\partial p/\partial z = \sum_n A_n\, e^{i n\omega_0 t}$ (the Fourier decomposition of a physiological pulse), each harmonic obeys, with $v_z = \text{Re}\{V_n(r)e^{in\omega_0 t}\}$:

$$\frac{d^2 V_n}{dr^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{dV_n}{dr} - \frac{in\omega_0\rho}{\mu}V_n = -\frac{A_n}{\mu}$$

The homogeneous part is exactly Bessel's equation of order zero in the complex variable $\Lambda_n r$, with $\Lambda_n = \alpha_n\, i^{1/2}/R$ and Womersley number $\alpha_n = R\sqrt{n\omega_0/\nu}$. The bounded, no-slip solution is:

$$V_n(r) = \frac{A_n}{i n\omega_0\rho}\left[1 - \frac{J_0(\Lambda_n r)}{J_0(\Lambda_n R)}\right]$$

Summing all harmonics (plus a steady n=0 Poiseuille term) reconstructs the full pulsatile velocity field — the basis of Womersley's classical model of arterial blood flow.

Volumetric Flow Rate per Harmonic

$$Q_n(t) = \text{Re}\left\{\frac{A_n \pi R^2}{in\omega_0\rho}\left[1 - \frac{2J_1(\Lambda_n R)}{\Lambda_n R\, J_0(\Lambda_n R)}\right]e^{in\omega_0 t}\right\}$$

At low αn the bracket → parabolic (Poiseuille) response, in phase with pressure. At high αn the bracket → 1, and flow lags pressure by ~90°, with motion confined to a thin Stokes layer near the wall.

Per-Harmonic Womersley Number

$$\alpha_n = \alpha_1\sqrt{n}, \qquad \alpha_1 = R\sqrt{\frac{\omega_0}{\nu}}$$

Higher harmonics see a larger effective Womersley number, so high-frequency components of the pulse are damped and phase-lagged more strongly than the fundamental — the pipe acts as a low-pass filter on the velocity profile even though pressure is transmitted almost instantly.

Elasticity in Cylindrical Bodies — Pressure Vessels, Rotating Disks & Turbine Rotors

Pressure vessels Rotating disks Turbine rotors Campbell diagram

From Plate Bending to Bessel's Equation

Free axisymmetric vibration of a thin elastic disk (Kirchhoff plate theory) is governed by the biharmonic equation:

$$D\nabla^4 w - \rho h\,\omega^2 w = 0, \qquad D=\frac{Eh^3}{12(1-\nu^2)}$$

With $\lambda^4=\rho h\omega^2/D$, the operator factors as a product of two Helmholtz operators:

$$\left(\nabla^2+\lambda^2\right)\left(\nabla^2-\lambda^2\right)w = 0$$

Writing $w(r,\theta)=R(r)\cos(m\theta)\cos(\omega t)$, each factor separately reduces to an ordinary Bessel-type radial equation — the first gives the ordinary Bessel equation (solutions Jm, Ym), the second the modified Bessel equation (solutions Im, Km):

$$R(r) = A\,J_m(\lambda r) + B\,Y_m(\lambda r) + C\,I_m(\lambda r) + D\,K_m(\lambda r)$$

Regularity at r = 0 (solid disk, no central bore) eliminates Ym and Km, leaving $R(r)=A\,J_m(\lambda r)+C\,I_m(\lambda r)$ — exactly the same J/I pairing used for the drumhead and quantum-well panels, but now driven by 4th-order plate stiffness rather than the 2nd-order wave equation.

Pressure Vessels — Lamé Stresses & the Limits of the Static Solution

For an axially-uniform thick-walled cylinder (inner radius a, outer radius b) under internal/external pressure, the classic Lamé solution is purely algebraic — no Bessel functions needed:

$$\sigma_r(r)=\frac{p_i a^2-p_o b^2}{b^2-a^2}-\frac{a^2b^2(p_o-p_i)}{(b^2-a^2)\,r^2}$$
$$\sigma_\theta(r)=\frac{p_i a^2-p_o b^2}{b^2-a^2}+\frac{a^2b^2(p_o-p_i)}{(b^2-a^2)\,r^2}$$

Bessel functions re-enter once axial uniformity is broken — e.g. a local nozzle load or periodic axial pressure $p(z)=p_k\cos(kz)$. Love's axisymmetric biharmonic stress function $\chi(r,z)=f(r)\cos(kz)$ then has the Michell-type solution:

$$f(r) = C_1 I_0(kr) + C_2\, r\,I_1(kr) + C_3 K_0(kr) + C_4\, r\,K_1(kr)$$

— the same modified Bessel functions Iν, Kν from the heat-conduction and waveguide panels, here describing the Saint-Venant decay of stress concentrations near nozzles and support rings.

Rotating Disks & Turbine Rotors — Free-Edge Eigenvalue Equation

A turbine disk's natural bending frequencies are found by enforcing zero moment and zero effective (Kirchhoff) shear at the free rim r = a. For $R(\rho)=A\,J_m(x\rho)+C\,I_m(x\rho)$, $\rho=r/a,\ x=\lambda a$:

$$M_r{=}0:\;\; R''(1) + \nu\!\left[R'(1)-m^2R(1)\right]=0$$
$$V_r{=}0:\;\; R'''(1)+R''(1)-\big[1{+}m^2(2{-}\nu)\big]R'(1)+m^2(3{-}\nu)R(1)=0$$

A nontrivial (A,C) requires the 2×2 determinant of these conditions to vanish — a transcendental equation in x solved numerically below. Each root xmn is a mode with m nodal diameters and n nodal circles; these frequencies must be kept clear of blade-passing excitation across the engine's operating speed range — the basis of the Campbell diagram below.

Seismology — Bessel Functions in Earth's Wave Propagation

Seismic Wave (Helmholtz) Equation

$$\nabla^2 u + k^2 u = 0, \qquad k = \frac{\omega}{c}, \;\; c = \alpha \text{ (P-wave) or } \beta \text{ (S-wave)}$$

Assuming harmonic time dependence $u(\mathbf{r},t)=U(\mathbf{r})e^{-i\omega t}$ reduces the elastic wave equation to this Helmholtz form in a locally homogeneous medium. The coordinate geometry then dictates which Bessel-function family appears.

Cylindrical Spreading — Point Source on the Surface

$$\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r\frac{dR}{dr}\right) + k_r^2 R = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; R(r) = J_0(k_r r),\,Y_0(k_r r)$$
$$H_0^{(1)}(k_r r) = J_0(k_r r)+iY_0(k_r r)\;\xrightarrow[k_r r\to\infty]{}\;\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi k_r r}}\,e^{i(k_r r-\pi/4)}$$

The $1/\sqrt{r}$ envelope is the geometric-spreading law for cylindrically-spreading surface waves — much slower decay than body waves.

Spherical Solutions — Free Oscillations of the Earth

$$\frac{d^2(rR)}{dr^2} + \left[k^2-\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}\right](rR)=0$$
$$R(r) = j_l(kr) = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2kr}}\,J_{l+1/2}(kr)$$

Regularity at r=0 excludes yl(kr). Free-surface boundary conditions at r=a quantize k, giving the Earth's discrete normal modes (e.g. 0S2, period ≈53.1 min).

Body-Wave Travel Times & Locating an Epicenter

$$\Delta = (t_S-t_P)\,\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha-\beta} \;\approx\; 8\,(t_S-t_P)\;\text{km/s}\quad(\alpha{\approx}6.5,\beta{\approx}3.7)$$

Three or more stations, each giving Δ from the S–P interval, triangulate the epicenter. The simulator below builds the seismogram one station records.

Antenna Theory — Bessel Functions in Radiating Structures

Why Bessel Functions Appear in Antenna Problems

  • The exterior field of an infinite cylindrical conductor separates in cylindrical coordinates into Bessel's equation in the radial variable ρ, so the near/guided field of a thin-wire (cylindrical) antenna is built from Hankel functions $H_0^{(2)}(k_\rho\rho)$ — outgoing cylindrical waves.
  • Hallén's and Pocklington's integral equations for the surface current I(z) on a cylindrical dipole use a kernel derived from this cylindrical-wave Green's function, which is how the antenna radius a enters the current distribution through Bessel/Hankel terms.
  • Sleeve, biconical, and waveguide-fed cylindrical antennas expand the aperture/gap field as a Fourier–Bessel series in ρ; matching boundary conditions at the conductor surface fixes the Bessel-mode amplitudes — the same Jm/Ym machinery as the circular waveguide panel.
  • For circular-aperture antennas (parabolic reflectors, horn-fed dishes), the far field is the Hankel transform of the aperture illumination — identical mathematics to Fraunhofer diffraction — so the radiation pattern is expressed directly through J₁(u)/u and its generalizations Λₙ(u).

Cylindrical Conductor — Exterior Field

Outside an infinite cylindrical wire of radius a, harmonic fields satisfying the Helmholtz equation and the radiation condition take the outgoing-Hankel form:

$$E_z(\rho,z) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} A(k_z)\, H_0^{(2)}(k_\rho \rho)\, e^{-ik_z z}\, dk_z, \qquad k_\rho^2 = k^2 - k_z^2$$

A(kz) is fixed by matching to the assumed (or numerically solved) current I(z) at ρ = a — the Pocklington/Hallén kernel is exactly this Bessel/Hankel Green's function evaluated on the wire surface.

Circular-Aperture Pattern — The Λₙ Family

For an axisymmetric aperture illumination tapered as $g(\rho)=(1-\rho^2/a^2)^p$, the Hankel-transform far field is exactly:

$$F(u) = \Lambda_{p+1}(u), \qquad \Lambda_n(u) = n!\left(\frac{2}{u}\right)^n J_n(u), \qquad u = \frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\sin\theta$$

p = 0 (uniform) gives $\Lambda_1(u)=2J_1(u)/u$ — the same Airy/jinc pattern as the diffraction panel, now interpreted as an antenna beam instead of an optical one.

Application: Satellite Dish (Parabolic Reflector) Antenna

Aperture Taper, Gain, Beamwidth & Sidelobes

A satellite dish behaves as a circular aperture antenna: a feed horn illuminates the reflector with an amplitude taper that falls off toward the rim (to limit spillover and control sidelobes). Heavier taper trades gain and beamwidth for lower sidelobes — the same uniform/parabolic/parabolic² family above (p = 0, 1, 2).

$$G \approx \eta_{ap}\left(\frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\right)^2, \qquad \mathrm{HPBW}\,(^\circ) \approx k_p\,\frac{\lambda}{D}$$

Accelerator Physics — Bessel Functions in RF Cavities

Pillbox Cavity — the TM₀₁₀ Accelerating Mode

A cylindrical ("pillbox") RF cavity supports the same family of TM modes as the circular waveguide panel, but as a standing wave bounded by end walls. The lowest accelerating mode, TM₀₁₀, has a purely axial electric field with the familiar Bessel radial profile:

$$E_z(r,t) = E_0\, J_0(k_r r)\,\cos(\omega t), \qquad k_r R = \alpha_{01} = 2.405\;\;(\text{1st zero of }J_0)$$

Because the field has no z-dependence and no angular dependence (m=0), a particle riding the axis (r=0) sees the maximum field $E_0$; a particle displaced radially sees a reduced field set by $J_0(k_r r)$ — exactly the cylindrical waveguide mathematics from the EM Waveguide panel, now applied to particle acceleration rather than wave transmission.

Why Bessel Functions Matter Here — Key Points

  • Resonant frequency ↔ cavity radius: $f = \dfrac{c\,\alpha_{01}}{2\pi R}$ — the cavity radius is fixed once the RF frequency is chosen, exactly as the waveguide cutoff condition.
  • Radial acceptance / RF focusing: since $E_z \propto J_0(k_r r)$ falls off away from the axis, off-axis particles receive a smaller energy kick — this radial field curvature also produces weak transverse RF focusing/defocusing near the cavity gap.
  • Higher-order modes for steering: dipole (m=1) and quadrupole (m=2) TM/TE modes — J₁, J₂ profiles — are used in RF deflecting and "crab" cavities to tilt or kick the bunch transversely.
  • Beam-pipe HOMs: higher-order modes that leak into the beam pipe propagate as ordinary cylindrical waveguide Bessel modes (same Jm, Jm′ zero tables used earlier) — these drive wakefields and impedance budgets that can destabilize the beam.
  • From single kick to synchrotron motion: a particle crosses the cavity once per revolution; the resulting once-per-turn energy kick, combined with momentum-dependent revolution time, builds up the longitudinal "synchrotron oscillation" simulated below.

Application: Synchrotron RF Bucket & Ring Simulator

From Cavity Kick to Phase-Space Motion

Each turn, the RF cavity changes a particle's energy by $\Delta E \to \Delta E + eV[\sin\varphi-\sin\varphi_s]$, where φ is the particle's RF phase and φs the synchronous phase. A particle with the "wrong" energy then drifts to a different phase before the next kick:

$$\varphi \to \varphi + 2\pi h\,\eta\,\frac{\Delta E}{\beta^2 E_0}$$

Iterating this turn-by-turn map traces closed orbits in (φ, ΔE) space — small ones are near-sinusoidal synchrotron oscillations; the largest closed orbit is the separatrix, bounding the stable "RF bucket."

Magnetic Fields of Solenoids — Bessel Functions from Maxwell's Equations

From Maxwell's Equations to the Modified Bessel Equation

For an azimuthal surface current Kφ(z) wound on a cylinder of radius a (an idealized solenoid), the vector potential Aφ(r,z) in current-free regions satisfies $\nabla\times\mathbf{B}=0$. Fourier-decomposing along z, $A_\varphi=\sum_k A_k(r)e^{ikz}$, each mode obeys:

$$A_k''(r) + \frac{1}{r}A_k'(r) - \left(\frac{1}{r^2}+k^2\right)A_k(r) = 0$$

— the modified Bessel equation of order 1. Regularity on-axis selects I1(kr) inside the coil; decay at infinity selects K1(kr) outside — matched at r=a by the jump in current, exactly like the I/K pairing used for pressure vessels and optical-fiber cladding elsewhere on this page.

Why Bessel Functions Appear Here — Key Points

  • Source-free field reconstruction: once the on-axis field Bz(0,z) is known (Fourier series in z), the off-axis field everywhere current-free follows from $B_z(r,z)=\sum_n A_n\,I_0(k_n r)\cos(k_n z)$ — the standard cylindrical-harmonic technique for solenoidal magnet field mapping.
  • I₀ inside the bore: bounded at r=0, I0(knr) grows slowly with r — this radial growth is exactly what limits field homogeneity away from the axis, defining the usable imaging volume (DSV) of a magnet.
  • K₀ outside the coil: matched to I0 at r=a, K0(knr) decay sets the fringe (stray) field falloff used to draw the "5-Gauss line" safety perimeter around an MRI suite.
  • Higher harmonics ↔ sharper features: larger kn (higher Fourier harmonics of the coil's current distribution) decay fastest in I0/K0 away from the windings — the field smooths out farther from the coil, the same Bessel-driven low-pass behavior seen in the heat-conduction and pulsatile-flow panels.
  • Same mathematics, many magnets: this I₀/K₀ machinery applies equally to NMR magnets, gradient/shim coils, and superconducting MRI solenoids — only the coil geometry and current distribution change.

Application: MRI Magnet Field Mapper

On-Axis Field → Off-Axis Reconstruction

The exact on-axis field of a finite uniformly-wound solenoid is used directly (closed form, no Bessel functions needed on-axis). It is then Fourier-decomposed in z, and the off-axis field is reconstructed via the I₀/K₀ expansion above — inside the bore (r<a) using I₀, outside the coil (r>a) using K₀, matched at r=a.

Skin Effect in Conductors — Bessel Functions from the AC Current Diffusion Equation

From the Diffusion Equation to a Complex-Argument Bessel Equation

Inside a good conductor, combining Ampère's and Faraday's laws with Ohm's law $\mathbf{J}=\sigma\mathbf{E}$ gives a diffusion equation for the current density (or H-field), not a wave equation:

$$\nabla^2 J_z = j\omega\mu\sigma\, J_z$$

In a round wire of radius a, separating variables in cylindrical coordinates turns this into the ordinary Bessel equation of order 0, but with a complex argument:

$$J_z(r) = J_0(kr), \qquad k = \frac{1-j}{\delta}, \qquad \delta=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}}\;\;\text{(skin depth)}$$

δ is the classic skin depth; J0(kr) here is exactly the same first-kind Bessel function used throughout this page, just evaluated off the real axis — equivalently written via the real Kelvin functions $\mathrm{ber}(qr)+j\,\mathrm{bei}(qr)$.

Why Bessel Functions Appear Here — Key Points

  • Complex-argument J₀: the bounded, physically valid solution inside the wire is J₀(kr) with k=(1−j)/δ — the same Bessel ODE as the rest of this page, solved off the real axis.
  • Current crowds to the surface: as frequency increases, |k|a grows and J₀(kr)/J₀(ka) collapses toward the rim; deep inside the wire the field decays approximately as $e^{-(a-r)/\delta}$ — the familiar textbook skin-effect approximation.
  • AC resistance from a Bessel ratio: the internal impedance per unit length is proportional to J₀(ka)/J₁(ka), giving the closed-form $R_{ac}/R_{dc} = \tfrac12\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left[ka\,J_0(ka)/J_1(ka)\right]$ — the same J₀/J₁ pairing used for waveguide cutoffs and orthogonality elsewhere on this page.
  • Asymptotic limit: for a≫δ this reduces to the well-known engineering rule $R_{ac}/R_{dc}\approx a/(2\delta)$ — AC resistance rises roughly with √f.
  • Litz wire payoff: bundling many thin insulated strands keeps each strand's a/δ small (deep in the low-ka regime where the ratio ≈1), avoiding the resistance penalty a single solid conductor of the same total area would pay at high frequency.

Nuclear Reactor — Bessel Functions in Neutron Diffusion

From the Diffusion Equation to the Helmholtz/Bessel Form

The one-group neutron diffusion equation balances leakage, absorption, and fission production. At criticality (k=1, or more generally normalizing by k) it rearranges into the same Helmholtz equation seen throughout this page:

$$-D\nabla^2\phi + \Sigma_a\phi = \frac{\nu\Sigma_f}{k}\phi \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \nabla^2\phi + B^2\phi = 0, \qquad B^2=\frac{\nu\Sigma_f/k-\Sigma_a}{D}$$

For a finite bare cylindrical core (radius R, height H), separating $\phi(r,z)=R(r)Z(z)$ turns the radial part into the ordinary Bessel equation of order 0. Regularity on-axis selects J₀; the extrapolated-boundary condition φ(R)=0 fixes the radial wavenumber at the first zero of J₀:

$$\phi(r,z) = \phi_0\, J_0\!\left(\frac{2.405\,r}{R}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi z}{H}\right), \qquad B_g^2=\left(\frac{2.405}{R}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\pi}{H}\right)^2$$

Bg² is the geometric buckling — set purely by core shape and size, independent of materials.

Why Bessel Functions Appear Here — Key Points

  • J₀ radial flux shape: the only solution finite on the core axis and vanishing at the (extrapolated) edge is J₀(2.405 r/R) — same axis-regularity argument used for the drumhead and quantum-well panels.
  • Criticality = buckling match: the reactor is critical when the material buckling (set by fuel/moderator nuclear properties) equals the geometric buckling Bg² (set by core size) — a direct analogue of matching a Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue to a boundary condition.
  • Effective multiplication factor: $k_{eff}=k_\infty/(1+M^2B_g^2)$ links the infinite-medium multiplication k∞, the neutron migration area M², and the Bessel-derived buckling into a single design number.
  • Flux peaking: J₀(2.405 r/R) peaks sharply on-axis (radial peaking factor ≈2.32 for a bare, unreflected core) — this drives control-rod patterns, burnable poison loading, and reflector design used to flatten the power profile.
  • Same Bessel zero, many reactor types: the identical α₀₁=2.405 boundary condition reappears (with different D, Σ, M²) whether the moderator is light water, heavy water, or graphite — only the material buckling and migration area change.

Medical Ultrasound — Bessel-Type Beam Patterns from Circular Transducers

From the Acoustic Wave Equation to the Same Diffraction Mathematics

A circular piston transducer radiating into tissue obeys the same Helmholtz equation used for the optical diffraction and antenna-dish panels:

$$\nabla^2 p + k^2 p = 0, \qquad k = \frac{2\pi f}{c}$$

For a uniformly-driven circular aperture of radius a, the far-field directivity pattern is the familiar "jinc" function built from J₁:

$$D(\theta) = \frac{2J_1(ka\sin\theta)}{ka\sin\theta}$$

This is literally the same equation as the Fraunhofer Airy pattern and the satellite-dish beam pattern elsewhere on this page — only the wave type (acoustic vs. optical vs. RF) and the physical scale change.

Why Bessel Functions Appear Here — Key Points

  • Lateral resolution = Bessel zero: the first dark ring of the beam occurs at $\sin\theta_1=1.22\lambda/D$ — the same J₁ first zero (u=3.8317) as the Rayleigh criterion for telescopes, now setting how close two reflectors can be and still appear as separate dots in the image.
  • Near-field length: $N=a^2/\lambda$ marks where the beam transitions from a roughly collimated column to the diverging Bessel/jinc far-field pattern — directly analogous to the non-diffracting zone of the optical Bessel-beam panel.
  • Focusing reshapes, not removes, the Bessel structure: adding a focal lens applies a quadratic phase before the same aperture integral, so the diffraction-limited jinc pattern reappears at the focal depth instead of at infinity — the focal spot radius is still set by the J₁ zero, now via the f-number zf/D.
  • Apodization trims side lobes: tapering the aperture drive (vs. uniform) trades a wider main lobe (J₂-based pattern, first zero at u=5.136) for lower side lobes — the same uniform/tapered family used for the satellite-dish antenna panel, here reducing image clutter and off-axis artifacts instead of RF interference.
  • Axial resolution is a different story: depth-direction resolution is set mainly by pulse length / bandwidth, not by the aperture's Bessel pattern — lateral (Bessel-limited) and axial (pulse-limited) resolution are independent and usually quite different in practice.

Electromagnetic Scattering — Bessel & Hankel Functions in Cylinder Scattering

Plane Wave Scattering by an Infinite Cylinder

A plane wave incident on an infinite circular cylinder is expanded in cylindrical harmonics via the Jacobi–Anger expansion:

$$e^{ikr\cos\theta} = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} i^n J_n(kr)\, e^{in\theta}$$

Because the scattered field must radiate outward and satisfy the Sommerfeld radiation condition at infinity, it cannot be built from Jn or Yn alone — it requires the Hankel function, the specific J/Y combination that behaves as an outgoing cylindrical wave:

$$\psi_{sc}(r,\theta) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} i^n a_n\, H_n^{(1)}(kr)\, e^{in\theta}, \qquad H_n^{(1)}(kr) = J_n(kr) + iY_n(kr)$$

Matching the total field to the boundary condition at the cylinder surface (r=a) fixes the scattering coefficients an — this is the same Bessel/Hankel machinery used for the antenna panel's exterior wire fields, now applied to scattering rather than radiation from a source.

Why Bessel & Hankel Functions Appear Here — Key Points

  • Incident field → Jn: the regular (finite everywhere) Bessel function represents the known incoming plane wave, decomposed mode-by-mode in angle.
  • Scattered field → Hn(1): only the Hankel function has the correct outgoing-wave asymptotic behavior $H_n^{(1)}(kr)\sim\sqrt{2/\pi kr}\,e^{i(kr-n\pi/2-\pi/4)}$ — the same large-argument behavior used for the seismology and waveguide panels' traveling-wave solutions.
  • Boundary condition fixes an: for a perfectly conducting cylinder, soft (Dirichlet, TM/E-polarized) scattering gives $a_n=-J_n(ka)/H_n^{(1)}(ka)$; hard (Neumann, TE/H-polarized) scattering gives $a_n=-J_n'(ka)/H_n^{(1)\prime}(ka)$.
  • Resonances are Bessel/Hankel zeros and ripples: as ka sweeps through values where |Hn(1)(ka)| dips, the corresponding mode's contribution spikes — these "Mie-type" resonances and the residual ripples at large ka (creeping waves) are direct fingerprints of the Bessel/Hankel structure.
  • Three regimes set by ka: Rayleigh (ka≪1, strongly frequency-dependent), resonance/Mie (ka~1–5, oscillatory), and optical (ka≫1, the scattering cross-section flattens toward the geometric limit) — the same low/high-argument asymptotics of Jn/Yn seen throughout this page.

Application: Radar Cross Section of a Conducting Cylinder

From Scattering Coefficients to RCS

Summing the Hankel-function far-field asymptotics gives the scattering amplitude and, from it, the (2D) radar cross section as a function of observation angle:

$$f(\theta) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} a_n\, e^{in\theta}, \qquad \sigma(\theta)\;\propto\; |f(\theta)|^2$$
Infinite PEC cylinder, normal incidence, exact modal series — σ(θ) shown in normalized (illustrative) units to focus on angular/frequency shape rather than absolute calibration; a real radar link budget needs the full dimensional constant.

Photonic Crystals — Bessel Functions in Periodic Cylindrical Structures

Cylindrical inclusions Photonic band gaps Mie resonances Transformation-optics cloaking

Why Cylindrical Inclusions Lead to Bessel Functions

A 2D photonic crystal — a periodic lattice of dielectric or metallic rods embedded in a background medium — is built from unit cells with circular boundaries. Inside each rod the field satisfies the ordinary Helmholtz equation in cylindrical coordinates, so it separates exactly as in the EM-Scattering panel: regular Bessel functions inside, where the field must stay finite at the rod's axis,

$$\psi_{\text{in}}(r,\theta) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n\, J_n(n_1 k r)\, e^{in\theta}, \qquad r<a_{rod}$$

and outside the rod, a superposition of the known incident wave and an outgoing scattered wave built from Hankel functions — the same Jacobi–Anger decomposition used for the single-cylinder scattering problem:

$$\psi_{\text{out}}(r,\theta) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} i^n\Big[J_n(kr) + a_n H_n^{(1)}(kr)\Big] e^{in\theta}, \qquad r>a_{rod}$$

A photonic crystal is, in this sense, just many copies of the EM-Scattering panel's cylinder problem, arranged periodically and coupled to each other.

Why Bessel Functions Appear Here — Key Points

  • Single-rod scattering is the building block: every rod in the lattice scatters according to exactly the Jn/Hn(1) machinery from the EM-Scattering panel — the periodic structure's optical response starts from this single-cylinder Mie problem.
  • A natural basis for circular boundaries: band-structure calculations for rod lattices are often solved with a cylindrical-wave (multipole) expansion rather than a plane-wave Fourier basis — the field around each rod is expanded directly in Jn/Hn(1), with neighboring rods coupled through lattice sums of Hankel functions evaluated at the inter-rod separations. Plane waves converge slowly against circular interfaces; Bessel/Hankel harmonics are the natural fit.
  • Mie resonances set the gaps: the band-gap map of a rod lattice is closely tied to the Mie resonances of the isolated rod — the same Jn/Hn(1) ratio that fixes the scattering coefficient an in the EM-Scattering panel. Gaps tend to open most strongly near these single-rod resonances.
  • Transformation optics extends the same toolkit: a coordinate transformation that maps a hidden region down to a point produces a graded, anisotropic cylindrical shell. Matching fields across every interface of that shell is, layer by layer, the same cylindrical Bessel/Hankel boundary-matching used for ordinary cylinder scattering.
  • Cloaking = forcing every multipole to cancel: an ideal cylindrical cloak's design goal is simply the all-n generalization of the EM-Scattering panel's an — build the shell so that every scattering coefficient vanishes, leaving the exterior field identical to the undisturbed incident wave.

Application: Cylindrical Invisibility Cloak Simulator

From Scattering Coefficients to Invisibility

Take an object of radius a with its own ordinary Mie scattering coefficients an(0) (computed exactly as in the EM-Scattering panel). A single "cloak quality" knob η models what an ideal transformation-optics shell of outer radius b accomplishes — suppressing every multipole together:

$$a_n \;\longrightarrow\; (1-\eta)\,a_n^{(0)}, \qquad \eta\to 1 \;\Rightarrow\; a_n\to 0\ \ \forall n$$
Multipole-suppression model: scattering coefficients are scaled uniformly by (1−η) to mimic the exterior invisibility of an ideal cloak shell — this is not a full solve of the graded anisotropic medium's wave equation inside the shell itself. The interior r<a is always shown as protected/hidden, independent of η.

Astrophysics — Bessel Functions Across the Cosmos

Stellar oscillations Magnetized jets Accretion disks Pulsar magnetospheres

One Equation, Many Skies

$$\nabla^2\psi + k^2\psi = 0$$

Wherever an astrophysical system — a pulsating star, a confined plasma column, a viscously spreading disk, or a rotating magnetosphere — has enough spherical or cylindrical symmetry to separate variables, the same Helmholtz/diffusion mathematics used throughout this page reappears, now acting on scales of stars, jets, and entire galaxies instead of waveguides and drumheads.

Stellar Oscillations — Asteroseismology

$$\nabla^2\delta p + k^2\delta p = 0 \;\;\xrightarrow{\text{spherical}}\;\; \delta p(r,\theta,\varphi,t) = j_l(k_{nl}r)\,Y_{lm}(\theta,\varphi)\,\cos(\omega_{nl}t)$$
  • Global standing sound waves: in the idealized limit of a uniform-density, uniform sound-speed sphere, the linearized adiabatic wave equation separates exactly as in the Earth's free-oscillation panel — the radial part is the spherical Bessel equation, with regularity at the stellar core selecting jl over yl (the same axis-regularity argument used for the drumhead and quantum-well panels).
  • Quantized p-mode frequencies: a surface boundary condition fixes the radial wavenumber at a zero of jl, exactly as the circular-membrane and circular-well panels used zeros of Jm — giving the evenly-spaced overtone families (n=1,2,3…) seen in the Sun's five-minute oscillations.
  • Degree and order: the angular pattern Ylm(θ,φ) plays the same role as cos(mθ) in the drumhead panel — l sets the number of nodal lines on the stellar surface, m sets how they are arranged in azimuth.
  • Beyond the toy model: real stars are stratified (ρ and cs vary with r) and self-gravitating, so quantitative asteroseismology needs numerical codes (e.g. ADIPLS, GYRE); the homogeneous-sphere Bessel solution below is the textbook zeroth-order picture that explains why the overtone spacing is so regular.

Magnetized Plasma Jets

$$\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \alpha\mathbf{B} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; B_z(r) = B_0\,J_0(\alpha r), \qquad B_\varphi(r) = B_0\,J_1(\alpha r)$$
  • Force-free confinement: jets from young stars, X-ray binaries, and active galactic nuclei are often modeled as constant-α force-free, cylindrically symmetric magnetic equilibria — the classic Lundquist solution above is built directly from the same J₀/J₁ pairing used for the EM-waveguide and skin-effect panels, now confining a column of relativistic plasma instead of guiding a wave or carrying current.
  • Helical pitch sets stability: the field's twist, and hence the jet's susceptibility to the m=0 "sausage" and m=1 "kink" instabilities from the laboratory-plasma panel, is controlled by how far αr extends before crossing the first zeros of J₀ and J₁.
  • A natural truncation radius: Bz reverses sign at the first zero of J₀, so the simple constant-α core is typically pressure-matched to an external sheath — real jets add an ambient/disk-wind boundary beyond this radius.

Accretion Disks

$$\Sigma(x,\tau) \;\propto\; \tau^{-1} x^{-1/4} \exp\!\left(-\frac{1+x^2}{\tau}\right) I_{1/4}\!\left(\frac{2x}{\tau}\right), \qquad x=\frac{r}{r_0}$$
  • Viscous ring-spreading: the Lynden-Bell & Pringle (1974) solution for a thin ring of disk material released at radius r₀ writes the surface-density evolution directly in terms of a fractional-order modified Bessel function I1/4 — the same exponentially-growing I-family used for the optical-fiber and pressure-vessel panels, here driven by viscous diffusion instead of a wave equation.
  • Two-way transport: the I1/4 term governs how matter diffuses both inward (feeding accretion onto the central star or black hole) and outward (carrying angular momentum away) — the basis of the self-similar late-time spreading law fit to protoplanetary and X-ray-binary disks.
  • Disk normal modes: when a disk is treated locally as a thin elastic/pressure-supported sheet, its vertical and warp oscillation modes reduce to a cylindrical Bessel eigenvalue problem directly analogous to the drumhead panel.

Pulsar Magnetospheres

$$h_l^{(1)}(kr) = j_l(kr) + i\,y_l(kr) \;\xrightarrow[kr\to\infty]{}\; \frac{1}{kr}\,e^{i\left(kr-\frac{(l+1)\pi}{2}\right)}$$
  • Radiating exterior: beyond the light cylinder, the time-varying multipole fields of a rotating neutron star radiate as outgoing spherical waves — exactly as the EM-scattering panel needed the cylindrical Hankel function Hn(1) for outgoing waves, the spherical problem needs hl(1), built from the same spherical Bessel functions as the stellar-oscillation panel.
  • Multipole hierarchy: each magnetic multipole order l radiates its own hl(1)(kr) pattern; the dominant dipole term (l=1) sets the canonical pulsar spin-down luminosity, while higher multipoles (l≥2) fall off faster and matter only very close to the star.
  • Quasi-static interior: inside the light cylinder, the corotating charge/current structure (the Goldreich–Julian density and "dead zone" boundary) is often analyzed with the same cylindrical modified-Bessel I/K machinery used in the solenoid and skin-effect panels — I/K for the quasi-static interior, hl(1) for the radiating exterior.

Application: Stellar p-mode Oscillations (Homogeneous-Sphere Model)

From the Wave Equation to an Oscillating Star

For a uniform-density, uniform sound-speed sphere of radius R, the acoustic pressure perturbation separates into a radial spherical-Bessel profile and an angular pattern. To visualize a mode on a 2D equatorial slice through the star, the angular part is shown as $\cos(m\varphi)$ (an azimuthal cut, the same simplification used for the drumhead and EM-waveguide panels) combined with the radial profile:

$$\delta p(r,\varphi,t) = j_l\!\left(\beta_{nl}\frac{r}{R}\right)\cos(m\varphi)\,\cos(\omega_{nl}t), \qquad j_l(\beta_{nl})=0,\;\; \omega_{nl}=\frac{c_s\,\beta_{nl}}{R}$$
Homogeneous, non-self-gravitating sphere — the textbook zeroth-order asteroseismology model. Real stars are centrally condensed and self-gravitating; this illustrates the overtone structure, not a precision solar model.

Gravitational Waves — Bessel Functions in Curved-Spacetime Wave Propagation

From the Linearized Einstein Equations to the Same Wave Equation

In the weak-field limit, writing the spacetime metric as $g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}$ and choosing harmonic (Lorenz) gauge, the vacuum Einstein equations linearize into a flat-space wave equation for the trace-reversed perturbation:

$$\Box\,\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0, \qquad \Box = -\partial_t^2+\nabla^2$$

In transverse-traceless (TT) gauge this splits into two independent scalar wave equations, one per polarization:

$$\Box\,h_+ = 0, \qquad \Box\,h_\times = 0$$

This is the pinpoint: mathematically, h+ and h× obey exactly the same flat-space scalar wave equation used for sound, light, and every other field on this page. Wherever the source or boundary geometry has cylindrical or spherical symmetry, the separation-of-variables argument used throughout this page applies unchanged — and Bessel functions appear again, now describing ripples in spacetime itself.

Cylindrical Gravitational Waves — the Einstein–Rosen Solution

Remarkably, cylindrically symmetric vacuum solutions don't need to be linearized at all. The exact Einstein–Rosen metric,

$$ds^2 = e^{2(\gamma-\psi)}(-dt^2+d\rho^2) + \rho^2 e^{-2\psi}d\varphi^2 + e^{2\psi}dz^2$$

reduces the full nonlinear vacuum field equations to a single linear PDE for ψ(ρ,t) — the ordinary flat-space cylindrical wave equation seen in the EM-waveguide and acoustics panels:

$$\psi_{tt} = \psi_{\rho\rho} + \frac{1}{\rho}\psi_\rho + \psi_{zz}$$

Separating $\psi=R(\rho)Z(z)T(t)$ with $Z\propto e^{ik_zz}$, $T\propto e^{-i\omega t}$ turns the radial part into the ordinary Bessel equation of order zero, with $\kappa^2=\omega^2-k_z^2$:

$$R''+\frac{1}{\rho}R'+\kappa^2 R = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; R(\rho)=J_0(\kappa\rho),\,Y_0(\kappa\rho)$$

Regularity on the symmetry axis (ρ=0) selects J0 — the same axis-regularity argument used for the drumhead, nuclear-reactor, and quantum-well panels. An outgoing radiating disturbance instead requires the Hankel combination $H_0^{(1)}=J_0+iY_0$, exactly as in the cylindrical point-source seismic-waves panel.

The γ-Function — Where GR Departs from Electromagnetism

Once ψ is known, the companion metric function γ — which sets the actual curvature and the wave's gravitational self-energy (the Weber–Wheeler–Thorne "C-energy") — follows by quadrature, not by solving another wave equation:

$$d\gamma = \rho\!\left[(\psi_\rho^2+\psi_t^2)\,d\rho + 2\,\psi_\rho\psi_t\,dt\right]$$

Because γ depends quadratically on gradients of ψ, two superposed Bessel-wave solutions for ψ do not produce a superposed γ — the wave's own energy content does not add linearly, unlike an electromagnetic field. This nonlinear self-coupling, entirely absent from the EM-waveguide and acoustics analogues, is the genuinely general-relativistic feature hiding inside an otherwise completely familiar Bessel-equation story.

In short: the wave equation is borrowed unchanged from electromagnetism and acoustics; the bookkeeping of the energy it carries is not.

Why Bessel & Hankel Functions Appear Here — Key Points

  • Axis regularity → J0: any cylindrically symmetric GW solution finite on the symmetry axis is built from J0(κρ), the identical regularity argument used for the drumhead, EM-waveguide, and nuclear-reactor panels — now applied to spacetime curvature instead of a membrane or a neutron flux.
  • Outgoing radiation → H0(1): a genuinely radiating (as opposed to standing-wave) cylindrical disturbance needs the same outgoing-wave Hankel combination used for cylindrically-spreading seismic waves and EM/acoustic scattering — with the same $1/\sqrt{\kappa\rho}$ amplitude falloff law.
  • Multipole radiation from isolated sources: far from a bounded source such as an inspiraling binary, the radiative field is expanded in spherical harmonics and spherical Hankel functions $h_l^{(1)}(kr)$ — the identical multipole machinery used for antenna radiation and pulsar-magnetosphere fields elsewhere on this page, with the mass quadrupole moment playing the role the electric dipole plays in EM radiation.
  • Ringdown — a Bessel-flavored limit, not an exact one: perturbations of a black hole (Regge–Wheeler/Zerilli equations) generally require Confluent Heun functions, not Bessel functions, to solve exactly. But in the high-frequency (eikonal) limit near the photon sphere, the effective radial potential locally resembles the centrifugal term of the Bessel ODE, and WKB tunneling through that barrier gives a useful approximate picture of how ringdown frequencies are set.
  • 1/√ρ vs 1/r falloff: the cylindrical Bessel/Hankel envelope falls as 1/√ρ (energy spreading over an expanding cylinder), while the spherical multipole envelope falls as 1/r (energy spreading over an expanding sphere) — both are direct consequences of the large-argument asymptotics of Jn/Yn and jl/yl used throughout this page, and both underlie why GW detectors must be sensitive to strains as small as ~10⁻²¹.

Density Perturbations — Acoustic Oscillations in the Primordial Plasma

In conformal time τ, the Fourier-space density contrast δk of a single perfect fluid with equation of state p=wρ obeys a damped-oscillator equation. The expansion-driven "friction" term $2a'/a$ plays exactly the role of the $2/x$ term that appears throughout this page's spherical Bessel equations:

$$\delta_k'' + \frac{2a'}{a}\delta_k' + c_s^2k^2\delta_k = 0, \qquad a(\tau)\propto\tau^{2/(1+3w)},\;\; c_s^2=w$$

During radiation domination (w=1/3, the era relevant to the CMB), $a\propto\tau$ and the equation reduces exactly to the l=0 spherical Bessel equation used elsewhere on this page:

$$\delta_k'' + \frac{2}{\tau}\delta_k' + \frac{k^2}{3}\delta_k = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \delta_k(\tau) \propto j_0\!\left(\frac{k\tau}{\sqrt3}\right) = \frac{\sin(k\tau/\sqrt3)}{k\tau/\sqrt3}$$

This is the textbook acoustic-oscillation solution for the tightly-coupled photon–baryon fluid — directly responsible for the CMB's acoustic peaks. For a general fluid the same equation gives a generalized Bessel order:

$$\delta_k(\tau) \propto (c_sk\tau)^{-\nu}J_\nu(c_sk\tau), \qquad \nu = \frac{3(1-w)}{2(1+3w)}$$

Superhorizon modes (kτ≪1) are frozen — the same small-argument regularity used for every "finite at the origin" solution on this page — while subhorizon modes (kτ≫1) oscillate and decay, just like the large-argument Bessel asymptotics used in the diffraction and waveguide panels.

Primordial Gravitational Waves — Tensor Perturbations

Tensor metric perturbations (gravitational waves) hk obey the same friction structure as density perturbations, but with cs=1 (they always travel at the speed of light, regardless of the fluid's equation of state):

$$h_k'' + \frac{2a'}{a}h_k' + k^2h_k = 0$$

During radiation domination this is again exactly a spherical Bessel equation — this time order l=1:

$$h_k'' + \frac{2}{\tau}h_k' + k^2h_k = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; h_k(\tau) = h_k^{\rm prim}\cdot 3\,\frac{j_1(k\tau)}{k\tau}$$

Why Bessel functions appear here — key points:

  • Universal order, no fluid dependence: because GWs feel no pressure, ν=1/2 (giving j1) in radiation domination regardless of what's sourcing the expansion — unlike density perturbations, whose order ν depends on w.
  • Freeze, then redshift: for kτ≪1, j1(x)/x→1/3 (constant) — the wave is frozen outside the horizon. For kτ≫1 it oscillates with envelope ∝1/τ — ordinary radiation redshifting once inside the horizon.
  • Zeros pinpoint exact nulls: the zeros of j1 (x≈4.493, 7.725, 10.904,…) mark the precise instants the GW strain crosses through zero as it propagates — literal pinpoints in the oscillation, set by the same Bessel zero tables used for drumhead and waveguide modes elsewhere on this page.
  • Same machinery, different era: swapping the radiation-era a(τ)∝τ for a matter-era a(τ)∝τ² shifts the effective order, changing the transfer function shape — this is exactly the inflationary stochastic gravitational-wave-background calculation used for LISA/PTA/CMB B-mode forecasts.

From 3D Perturbations to the 2D Sky — The Line-of-Sight Projection

A 3D plane-wave perturbation of wavenumber k is observed today as power spread across multipoles l on the 2D sky. The projection kernel that performs this 3D→2D conversion is, again, a spherical Bessel function — evaluated at the comoving distance to the last-scattering surface Dls=τ0−τrec:

$$\Theta_l(k) \;\approx\; S(k)\, j_l\!\big(k\,D_{\rm ls}\big), \qquad C_l \;\propto\; \int \frac{dk}{k}\,\Delta^2(k)\,\big[\Theta_l(k)\big]^2$$

Because jl(x) is sharply peaked near x≈l (the same "peak tracks the order" behavior visualized in the Application below), the projection roughly maps k↔l/Dls — turning the k-space acoustic oscillations from the card above directly into the l-space acoustic peaks of the CMB.
