Foundations of Theoretical Physics

Conservation Laws

The invariant quantities that underlie every physical process — from subatomic collisions to the large-scale structure of the cosmos.

Every differentiable symmetry of the action of a physical system with conservative forces has a corresponding conservation law.
— Emmy Noether, 1915 · Invariante Variationsprobleme
Noether's Theorem — the Master Principle
The profound unifying insight behind all conservation laws is Noether's theorem. It establishes a deep correspondence between continuous symmetries of the action functional $S[\mathbf{q}] = \int \mathcal{L}\,dt$ and conserved quantities called Noether charges. If the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}$ is invariant under a one-parameter group of transformations, then there exists a quantity that remains constant throughout the motion. Each conservation law listed below can be traced directly to a specific symmetry:

I

Conservation of Energy

$E = K + V = \text{const}$  |  First Law of Thermodynamics
Symmetry: Invariance under time translation
Statement & Origin

The total energy of an isolated system is constant. Energy can be transformed between forms — kinetic, potential, thermal, chemical, electromagnetic, nuclear — but it is never created or destroyed. This principle follows from the invariance of the laws of physics under time translation: if an experiment performed today gives the same result as one performed tomorrow, then by Noether's theorem, there exists a conserved quantity that we call energy.

Hamiltonian Conservation
$$\frac{dH}{dt} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \mathcal{L} \text{ has no explicit time dependence}$$
When $\mathcal{L}$ does not depend explicitly on $t$, the Hamiltonian $H = \sum_i p_i\dot{q}_i - \mathcal{L}$ is conserved.
Classical Mechanics

Work–Energy Theorem

For a conservative force $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla V$, the total mechanical energy is:

$$E = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}mv^2}_{K} + \underbrace{V(\mathbf{r})}_{U} = \text{const}$$

The work done on a system equals the change in its kinetic energy. For conservative forces, the potential energy stores work done against the field, and the sum $K+U$ is invariant along any trajectory.

Conservative force Path-independent Lagrangian mechanics
Thermodynamics

First Law

In thermodynamics, energy conservation takes the form of the First Law, connecting internal energy $U$, heat $Q$, and work $W$:

$$\Delta U = Q - W$$

Heat is energy in transit due to a temperature difference; work is energy transferred by a mechanical force. Neither creates energy — they simply redirect it. The internal energy $U$ of a system accounts for all microscopic kinetic and potential energies.

Electromagnetism

Poynting's Theorem

Energy density $u$ in an electromagnetic field and its flow via the Poynting vector $\mathbf{S}$:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{S} = -\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$$ $$u = \frac{\epsilon_0 E^2}{2} + \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}, \quad \mathbf{S} = \frac{\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}}{\mu_0}$$

The right-hand side $-\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ is the power delivered by the field to currents (Ohmic loss). This is precisely the local form of energy conservation for the electromagnetic field — a continuity equation for energy density.

Special Relativity

Mass–Energy Equivalence

Einstein unified mass and energy into a single relativistic conservation law:

$$E^2 = (pc)^2 + (m_0c^2)^2$$ $$E = \gamma m_0 c^2, \quad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}$$

Rest mass itself carries energy $E_0 = m_0c^2$. In nuclear reactions, a tiny mass deficit $\Delta m$ corresponds to an enormous energy release $\Delta E = \Delta m\,c^2$. In relativistic mechanics, it is the 4-momentum $p^\mu = (E/c, \mathbf{p})$ whose norm is conserved.

Quantum Mechanics

Ehrenfest & Energy Eigenstates

In quantum mechanics, if $\hat{H}$ has no explicit time dependence:

$$\frac{d\langle \hat{A}\rangle}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[\hat{H},\hat{A}]\rangle + \left\langle\frac{\partial \hat{A}}{\partial t}\right\rangle$$

For an energy eigenstate $\hat{H}|\psi\rangle = E|\psi\rangle$, energy is sharp (zero variance). The time evolution $|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-iEt/\hbar}|\psi_0\rangle$ is a pure phase rotation — the probability distribution does not change. Energy conservation is encoded in the commutativity $[\hat{H},\hat{H}]=0$.

Violation & Apparent Non-Conservation
⚠

On cosmological scales, the expansion of the universe causes photons to redshift, apparently losing energy. General relativity does not have a globally conserved energy in the same sense because spacetime itself is dynamic — there is no global timelike Killing vector in a general curved spacetime. The local conservation law $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ (covariant divergence of the stress-energy tensor) holds everywhere, but global energy conservation requires asymptotic flatness (as in the ADM formalism). This is not a violation of Noether's theorem — the symmetry simply does not exist globally in an expanding universe.

II

Conservation of Linear Momentum

$\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v} = \text{const}$  |  Newton's 3rd Law
Symmetry: Invariance under spatial translation
Statement & Physical Meaning

The total linear momentum of a closed system is conserved. No net external force means no change in total momentum. By Noether's theorem, this follows from the homogeneity of space: the laws of physics are the same at every point in space, meaning no location is physically special. This isotropy of the vacuum guarantees that the total momentum vector $\mathbf{P} = \sum_i m_i\mathbf{v}_i$ is constant.

$$\mathbf{P} = \sum_{i=1}^N m_i \mathbf{v}_i = \text{const}, \quad \text{when} \quad \mathbf{F}_\text{ext} = \frac{d\mathbf{P}}{dt} = 0$$
Newton's 3rd Law Connection

Action–Reaction Pairs

For two interacting bodies, Newton's 3rd law states $\mathbf{F}_{12} = -\mathbf{F}_{21}$. Summing over the system:

$$\frac{d\mathbf{p}_1}{dt} + \frac{d\mathbf{p}_2}{dt} = \mathbf{F}_{12} + \mathbf{F}_{21} = 0$$ $$\Rightarrow \mathbf{p}_1 + \mathbf{p}_2 = \text{const}$$

Internal forces — however complex — cancel in pairs. Only external forces can change the total momentum. This makes momentum conservation a powerful tool even when internal forces are entirely unknown.

Center of Mass

COM Frame

The center of mass moves with constant velocity when $\mathbf{F}_\text{ext}=0$:

$$\mathbf{R}_\text{cm} = \frac{\sum m_i \mathbf{r}_i}{M_\text{tot}}, \quad \ddot{\mathbf{R}}_\text{cm} = \frac{\mathbf{F}_\text{ext}}{M}$$

In the center-of-mass frame $\mathbf{P}_\text{cm}=0$ by definition. This frame decouples the center-of-mass motion from relative motion, simplifying the analysis of all two-body problems (scattering, orbits, collisions).

Elastic Collisions

Both $\mathbf{P}$ and $E$ Conserved

In a 1D elastic collision between masses $m_1$ (moving at $v_0$) and $m_2$ (at rest):

$$v_1' = \frac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}v_0, \quad v_2' = \frac{2m_1}{m_1+m_2}v_0$$

When $m_1=m_2$: $v_1'=0$, $v_2'=v_0$ — the classic billiard-ball exchange. When $m_1 \gg m_2$: the heavy mass barely slows, and the light mass bounces back at nearly $2v_0$. These results follow purely from the two conservation laws.

Inelastic Collisions

Only $\mathbf{P}$ Conserved

In perfectly inelastic collisions (objects stick together), kinetic energy is lost to deformation/heat, but momentum is still conserved:

$$\mathbf{p}_\text{before} = \mathbf{p}_\text{after}$$ $$m_1\mathbf{v}_1 + m_2\mathbf{v}_2 = (m_1+m_2)\mathbf{V}$$ $$\Delta K = -\frac{m_1m_2}{2(m_1+m_2)}|\mathbf{v}_1-\mathbf{v}_2|^2$$

The lost kinetic energy $\Delta K$ is negative (energy goes to internal degrees of freedom), but total energy including thermal is still conserved.

Rocket Propulsion

Tsiolkovsky Equation

A rocket expels mass to propel itself — the ejected gas carries away momentum:

$$M\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = -\mathbf{v}_\text{ex}\frac{dM}{dt}$$ $$\Delta v = v_\text{ex}\ln\frac{M_0}{M_f}$$

No external forces act in deep space. The rocket + exhaust system has constant total momentum. As the exhaust gains backward momentum, the rocket gains forward momentum. This is conservation of momentum in its purest engineering form.

Relativistic & Field Momentum

In special relativity, momentum is modified to $\mathbf{p} = \gamma m_0 \mathbf{v}$, which preserves conservation under Lorentz transformations. Crucially, the electromagnetic field itself carries momentum density $\mathbf{g} = \epsilon_0(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}) = \mathbf{S}/c^2$. This field momentum must be included to make the total momentum of charged particles plus radiation a conserved quantity. This was famously demonstrated by the Abraham–Minkowski debate and the pressure of light.

4-Momentum Conservation
$$p^\mu = \left(\frac{E}{c},\, \mathbf{p}\right), \quad p_\mu p^\mu = m_0^2 c^2 = \text{invariant}$$ $$p^\mu_\text{total} = \sum_i p^\mu_i = \text{const in isolated system}$$
III

Conservation of Angular Momentum

$\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p} = \text{const}$  |  Kepler's 2nd Law
Symmetry: Invariance under rotation
Physical Origin

Angular momentum is conserved whenever no net external torque acts on a system. The deeper reason is the isotropy of space — physical laws look the same regardless of the orientation of the coordinate system (no direction in space is preferred). Noether's theorem then guarantees that the generator of rotations — angular momentum — is conserved.

$$\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p} = I\boldsymbol{\omega}, \qquad \frac{d\mathbf{L}}{dt} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_\text{ext}$$ $$\boldsymbol{\tau}_\text{ext} = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \mathbf{L} = \text{const}$$
Kepler's Second Law

Equal Areas in Equal Times

Gravity is a central force ($\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F} = 0$ because $\mathbf{F} \parallel \mathbf{r}$), so $\mathbf{L}$ is conserved. The rate of area swept by the radius vector is:

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{|\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v}|}{2} = \frac{L}{2m} = \text{const}$$

Planets move faster at perihelion (closest approach) and slower at aphelion (farthest point). This is a direct consequence of angular momentum conservation — not a separate empirical law but a theorem.

Moment of Inertia

Changing Distribution

For a rigid body, $L = I\omega$. When $L$ is conserved and $I$ changes:

$$I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2$$

A figure skater pulling in their arms reduces $I$, which increases $\omega$ proportionally. A collapsing stellar core (neutron star formation) drops $I$ by a factor of $\sim10^{10}$, spinning up from days to milliseconds. Conservation of $L$ is the sole explanation.

Spin & Orbital Angular Momentum

Quantum Mechanical Total $J$

In quantum mechanics, angular momentum is quantized. Total angular momentum:

$$\hat{\mathbf{J}} = \hat{\mathbf{L}} + \hat{\mathbf{S}}$$ $$|\mathbf{L}| = \hbar\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}, \quad L_z = m_\ell\hbar$$ $$|\mathbf{S}| = \hbar\sqrt{s(s+1)}, \quad S_z = m_s\hbar$$

In the absence of external torques, $\hat{J}^2$ and $\hat{J}_z$ commute with $\hat{H}$ and are conserved. Selection rules in atomic transitions ($\Delta\ell = \pm1$, $\Delta m_\ell = 0,\pm1$) enforce angular momentum conservation via the emitted photon carrying one unit $\hbar$ of spin.

Precession

Gyroscopic Motion

When a torque $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ acts perpendicular to $\mathbf{L}$, the direction of $\mathbf{L}$ changes — it precesses:

$$\boldsymbol{\Omega}_\text{prec} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\tau}}{L\sin\theta} = \frac{mgr}{I\omega}$$

Earth's rotational axis precesses with a period of ~26,000 years (the precession of the equinoxes) due to gravitational torques from the Sun and Moon acting on the equatorial bulge. The magnitude of $\mathbf{L}$ is conserved; only its direction changes.

Galaxy Formation

Cosmological Scale

Spiral galaxies acquire their rotation from the angular momentum of the initial gas cloud. As the cloud collapses under gravity, $|\mathbf{L}|$ is conserved but the moment of inertia shrinks dramatically, spinning up the disk. Galaxy morphology — spiral vs. elliptical — is partly determined by angular momentum content of the original collapse. Dark matter halos also carry angular momentum, characterized by the spin parameter $\lambda = L|E|^{1/2}/(GM^{5/2})$.

Rotational Parity & CPT Symmetry
🔬

Angular momentum conservation is connected to rotational symmetry (SO(3) in 3D, or SU(2) for spinors). In particle physics, angular momentum must be accounted for in all reactions. When a pion $\pi^0$ (spin 0) decays to two photons, each photon (spin 1) must have their spins anti-aligned along the decay axis to conserve $J=0$. This constraint, combined with Bose symmetry of the two-photon state, leads to measurable correlations in the polarization of the decay photons — experimentally verified with high precision.

Conservation of Parity

$P:\psi(t,\mathbf r)\rightarrow \hat P\psi(t,\mathbf r)=\eta_P\psi(t,-\mathbf r)$  |  Spatial Inversion
Symmetry: Invariance under spatial inversion — exact for strong and electromagnetic interactions, violated by the weak interaction
Statement & Physical Meaning

Parity is the symmetry associated with reversing all spatial coordinates. Under parity, a point $(x,y,z)$ is mapped to $(-x,-y,-z)$ while time is unchanged. A physical interaction conserves parity when the laws of physics are unchanged by this spatial inversion. For a parity eigenstate, the parity quantum number is $\eta_P=+1$ (even) or $\eta_P=-1$ (odd).

Parity Transformation
$$\mathbf r\rightarrow-\mathbf r,\qquad t\rightarrow t$$ $$\hat P\,\psi(t,\mathbf r)=\eta_P\,\psi(t,-\mathbf r),\qquad \eta_P=\pm1$$
Applying parity twice returns the original state: $\hat P^2=1$. Therefore parity eigenvalues are $\pm1$ for states that are eigenstates of parity.
Quantum Mechanics

Parity Operator & Eigenstates

In quantum mechanics the parity operator acts on a wavefunction by reversing its spatial argument. If the Hamiltonian is invariant under parity, it commutes with the parity operator and energy eigenstates can be chosen to have definite parity.

$$\hat P\psi(\mathbf r)=\psi(-\mathbf r)$$ $$[\hat H,\hat P]=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat P\psi=\eta_P\psi$$

The commutator condition is the quantum-mechanical statement that parity is a symmetry of the Hamiltonian.

Orbital Parity

Angular Momentum Rule

Spherical harmonics acquire a simple factor under spatial inversion. Consequently, an orbital state with orbital angular momentum $\ell$ has parity $(-1)^\ell$.

$$Y_{\ell m}(\pi-\theta,\phi+\pi)=(-1)^\ell Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)$$ $$P_{\rm orbital}=(-1)^\ell$$

Thus $s,p,d,f,\ldots$ orbitals have parity $+,-,+,-,\ldots$, respectively.

Parity Conservation in Reactions

Selection Rule

If an interaction conserves parity, the total parity of the initial state must equal the total parity of the final state. For a reaction or transition, orbital angular momentum contributes a factor $(-1)^\ell$, while intrinsic particle parities contribute multiplicatively.

$$P_{\rm initial}=P_{\rm final}$$ $$P_{\rm total}=\left(\prod_i\eta_i\right)(-1)^\ell$$ $$\boxed{\Delta P=0\qquad\text{for a parity-conserving interaction}}$$
✓

Strong and electromagnetic interactions obey this selection rule. A transition forbidden by parity conservation in these interactions cannot occur unless another mechanism supplies the required parity change.

Nuclear Physics

Parity of Nuclear States

Nuclear levels are commonly labeled by total angular momentum and parity, $J^\pi$. For a shell-model configuration, the parity is the product of the single-particle parities.

$$J^\pi\;=\;J^{\,\pm}$$ $$\pi_{\rm configuration}=\prod_i(-1)^{\ell_i}=(-1)^{\sum_i\ell_i}$$

Nuclear spectroscopy therefore uses parity together with angular momentum to classify excited states and transitions.

Atomic & Electromagnetic Transitions

Electric and Magnetic Multipoles

Electromagnetic transitions provide a direct example of parity selection rules. The electric multipole operator $E L$ changes parity according to $(-1)^L$, whereas the magnetic multipole operator $M L$ changes it according to $(-1)^{L+1}$.

$$E L:\quad \frac{\pi_f}{\pi_i}=(-1)^L$$ $$M L:\quad \frac{\pi_f}{\pi_i}=(-1)^{L+1}$$

In particular, electric dipole ($E1$) transitions change parity, while magnetic dipole ($M1$) transitions preserve it.

The Historical Breakthrough

Wu Experiment — Parity Violation in Beta Decay

In 1956–57, the cobalt-60 beta-decay experiment led by Chien-Shiung Wu demonstrated that the weak interaction does not conserve parity. Polarized nuclei were cooled in a magnetic field and the emitted electrons were found to have a preferred direction relative to the nuclear spin. The mirror-reflected process would reverse this correlation, showing that the weak interaction distinguishes left from right.

$$^{60}{\rm Co}\rightarrow{}^{60}{\rm Ni}+e^-+\bar\nu_e$$ $$\text{Parity transformation:}\quad \mathbf r\rightarrow-\mathbf r$$ $$\text{Observed weak decay:}\quad W(P\text{-transformed process})\neq W(\text{original process})$$
⚠

This is not a small correction to a parity-conserving law: parity is fundamentally violated by the weak interaction. The weak charged-current interaction is chiral and couples to left-handed fermions and right-handed antifermions.

Weak Interaction

Left–Right Asymmetry

For a relativistic fermion, chirality distinguishes left- and right-chiral components. The charged-current weak interaction selects the left-chiral fermion component, which is the origin of its maximal parity violation.

$$\psi_L=\frac{1-\gamma^5}{2}\psi,\qquad \psi_R=\frac{1+\gamma^5}{2}\psi$$ $$\mathcal L_{\rm CC}\propto \bar\psi\,\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)\psi\,W_\mu$$

The $(1-\gamma^5)$ structure is the characteristic V−A form of the charged weak current.

Meson Physics

Parity in Weak Decays

Weak decays can connect states of opposite parity without violating any fundamental conservation law of the complete theory, because parity itself is not a symmetry of the weak interaction.

$$K^0\rightarrow\pi^+ + \pi^-$$ $$\pi^\pm:\;J^P=0^-$$

Weak amplitudes therefore need not obey the parity selection rules that apply to strong or electromagnetic amplitudes.

P, C, CP and CPT

What Remains Conserved?

Parity violation does not mean that every discrete symmetry is violated. Charge conjugation $C$ exchanges particles with antiparticles, parity $P$ reverses spatial coordinates, and time reversal $T$ reverses the direction of time. The weak interaction violates $P$ and can also violate $C$ and $CP$, while the combined $CPT$ symmetry is a fundamental theorem of local, Lorentz-invariant quantum field theory under its standard assumptions.

$$C:\;\text{particle}\leftrightarrow\text{antiparticle}$$ $$P:\;\mathbf r\rightarrow-\mathbf r$$ $$T:\;t\rightarrow-t$$ $$\boxed{CPT\;\text{is conserved}}$$
🔬

Thus the correct modern statement is not simply “parity is conserved.” Parity is conserved by the strong and electromagnetic interactions, but is violated by the weak interaction. CPT provides a deeper symmetry constraint on relativistic quantum field theories.

Intrinsic Parity

Particles and Antiparticles

A particle at rest can be assigned an intrinsic parity. For fermions, the intrinsic parity convention of the antiparticle differs by a relative minus sign from that of the corresponding particle.

$$\hat P|\mathbf p=0,s\rangle=\eta_P|\mathbf p=0,s\rangle$$ $$\eta_{\bar f}=-\eta_f\qquad\text{(fermion convention)}$$

Boson particle–antiparticle intrinsic-parity relations depend on the field and convention; physical reaction amplitudes, rather than an isolated convention, determine observable selection rules.

Conservation of Electric Charge

$Q_\text{total} = \text{const}$  |  U(1) Gauge Symmetry
Symmetry: Global U(1) phase invariance
Gauge Origin

Electric charge conservation is the consequence of the global U(1) gauge symmetry of the Lagrangian of quantum electrodynamics. Under the transformation $\psi \to e^{i\alpha}\psi$ (a global phase rotation of all charged fields), the action is invariant. By Noether's theorem, this invariance produces a conserved Noether current $j^\mu$ and hence a conserved charge $Q = \int j^0\,d^3x$. When this symmetry is promoted to a local symmetry $\alpha = \alpha(x)$, it requires the introduction of a gauge boson — the photon — and yields the full structure of QED.

Noether Current
$$j^\mu = -e\bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu\psi, \qquad \partial_\mu j^\mu = 0$$ $$Q = \int j^0\,d^3x = -e\int\psi^\dagger\psi\,d^3x = \text{const}$$

Charge Quantization

Dirac's Monopole Argument

All observed charges are integer multiples of the elementary charge $e$. Dirac showed in 1931 that the mere existence of a single magnetic monopole anywhere in the universe would force charge quantization:

$$\frac{eg}{2\pi\hbar c} = n \in \mathbb{Z}$$ $$e = 1.602176634 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$$

In the Standard Model, charge quantization follows from the embedding of U(1) into non-Abelian gauge groups (anomaly cancellation conditions in each generation of quarks and leptons enforce this).

Charge Continuity

Charge conservation is local — not just global. The differential form is the continuity equation:

$$\frac{\partial \rho_e}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = 0$$

Here $\rho_e$ is the charge density (C/m³) and $\mathbf{J}$ is the current density (A/m²). This equation says that charge cannot spontaneously appear or disappear — it can only flow from place to place. A current flowing into a region must be accompanied by charge buildup there. This local form is far stronger than global conservation and is automatically satisfied by Maxwell's equations as an identity: $\partial_\mu j^\mu = \partial_\mu\partial_\nu F^{\nu\mu} = 0$ (antisymmetry of $F^{\mu\nu}$).

Antimatter

Pair Production & Annihilation

Creating or destroying charge always requires pair production. A photon can materialize an electron-positron pair:

$$\gamma \to e^- + e^+, \quad Q: \; 0 \to -1 + 1 = 0 \checkmark$$ $$e^- + e^+ \to \gamma + \gamma, \quad Q: \; -1+1 \to 0+0 = 0 \checkmark$$

The antiparticle carries the opposite charge. Charge conservation is why matter and antimatter are always created together, and why the universe's matter–antimatter asymmetry (baryon asymmetry) is one of the deepest unsolved problems in physics.

VI

Conservation of Matter

$B = \sum_i B_i = \text{const}$  |  Baryon Number & Lepton Number
Symmetry: Approximate — U(1)B global baryon symmetry
Historical Development

The conservation of matter — the idea that the total amount of substance is unchanged in chemical reactions — was first placed on a quantitative footing by Antoine Lavoisier in 1789 (loi de conservation de la masse). His careful experiments showed that the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products. In classical chemistry, this is absolute. However, at the level of fundamental particle physics, "conservation of matter" splits into more precise laws: conservation of baryon number, lepton number, and related quantum numbers — and these are only approximate symmetries.

Baryon Number

What Counts

Baryons (protons, neutrons, and their excited states) each carry $B = +1$. Antibaryons carry $B = -1$. Mesons and leptons carry $B = 0$.

$$B = N_\text{quarks}/3 - N_\text{antiquarks}/3$$ $$p,\,n: B=+1; \quad \bar p,\,\bar n: B=-1$$

In all observed nuclear and particle reactions, the total baryon number is conserved. The proton is stable (no lighter baryon to decay into) with a measured lifetime $\tau_p > 1.67\times10^{34}$ years — the longest known lower bound on any lifetime.

Lepton Number

Separate Conservation

Each lepton family has its own (approximately) conserved number:

$$L_e = N(e^-) + N(\nu_e) - N(e^+) - N(\bar\nu_e)$$ $$L_\mu,\; L_\tau \text{ similarly defined}$$

Beta decay: $n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu_e$. Here $L_e: 0 \to 0 + 1 + (-1) = 0$ ✓. Neutrino oscillations violate individual $L_e$, $L_\mu$, $L_\tau$ but preserve the total $L = L_e + L_\mu + L_\tau$. Discovering $L$ violation would be monumental.

Mass Conservation in Chemistry

Lavoisier's Law

In chemical reactions at ordinary energies, the binding energy changes $\Delta E$ are negligible compared to rest mass energies $mc^2$. The mass deficit is:

$$\Delta m = \frac{\Delta E_\text{chem}}{c^2} \sim \frac{1\text{ eV}}{(3\times10^8)^2\text{ J}} \approx 10^{-36}\ \text{kg}$$

This is utterly undetectable by any chemical balance. Hence, within the precision of all chemical measurement, mass is conserved. The statement "matter is conserved in chemical reactions" is accurate to at least 9 significant figures.

Nuclear Reactions

Where Mass Changes Matter

Nuclear binding energies are of order MeV, so the mass defect is measurable:

$$\Delta m = \frac{B.E.}{c^2}, \quad B.E. \sim 8\text{ MeV/nucleon}$$ $${}^4\text{He}: \Delta m \approx 0.030377\text{ u} \approx 28.3\text{ MeV}/c^2$$

In nuclear fission/fusion, mass is not conserved — it is converted to kinetic energy and photons. But baryon number IS conserved. The nucleon count (protons + neutrons) before equals the count after in any nuclear reaction.

Violations & Beyond Standard Model

Baryogenesis Problem

The observable universe contains matter with virtually no antimatter. If $B$ were exactly conserved from a symmetric Big Bang, we'd have equal amounts. Therefore, baryon number must have been violated in the early universe — one of Sakharov's three conditions for baryogenesis:

  • Baryon number violation
  • C and CP symmetry violation
  • Departure from thermal equilibrium

Grand Unified Theories (GUTs) predict $B$ violation via heavy $X$ bosons and sphaleron processes at $T \sim 10^{15}$ K. No proton decay has been observed yet.

Strangeness, Charm, and Other Flavour Numbers
⚛

Beyond baryon and lepton number, there are additional conserved quantum numbers in the Standard Model: strangeness $S$, charm $C$, bottomness $B'$, and topness $T$ — each associated with the corresponding quark flavor. These are conserved in strong and electromagnetic interactions (governed by QCD and QED respectively) but are violated in weak interactions mediated by the $W^\pm$ boson. The CKM (Cabibbo–Kobayashi–Maskawa) matrix governs the mixing of quark generations and hence the rate of flavor-changing weak processes. This is why strange particles have relatively long lifetimes — they can only decay weakly.

VII

The Continuity Equation

$\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$  |  Local Conservation
The universal form of all local conservation laws
The Unifying Framework

The continuity equation is the local expression of any conservation law. While a global conservation law says "the total amount of $X$ in the universe doesn't change," the continuity equation says something stronger and more physical: "$X$ cannot teleport — it can only flow continuously through space." This is the mathematical embodiment of the idea that nature is local: cause and effect propagate through fields, not action at a distance.

Master Continuity Equation
$$\boxed{\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = 0}$$
$\rho$ = density of conserved quantity  |  $\mathbf{J}$ = flux (current density) of that quantity

The physical interpretation emerges from integrating over a fixed volume $V$ bounded by surface $\partial V$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int_V \rho\,dV = -\oint_{\partial V} \mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{A}$$
Rate of change of total quantity inside $V$ = negative of net flux out through $\partial V$

This is Gauss's theorem applied to the conservation law. The quantity inside a volume only changes because it flows across the boundary — no creation or destruction anywhere.

Integral Form & Physical Derivation
At $t$

Mass Continuity

Fluid Dynamics

For a fluid of density $\rho$ with velocity field $\mathbf{v}$:

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v}) = 0$$

For an incompressible fluid ($\rho = \text{const}$): $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$. This constraint eliminates sound waves from incompressible fluid models. In a pipe of varying cross-section $A$, this reduces to $A_1v_1 = A_2v_2$ — the familiar result that flow speeds up where the pipe narrows (Bernoulli's continuity).

Charge Continuity

Electromagnetism

For charge density $\rho_e$ and current density $\mathbf{J}$:

$$\frac{\partial \rho_e}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$$ $$\text{or covariantly:} \quad \partial_\mu j^\mu = 0$$

This is automatically satisfied by Maxwell's equations: taking the divergence of Ampère's law ($\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J} + \mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf{E}$) and using Gauss's law ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \rho_e/\epsilon_0$) yields the continuity equation as an identity. Maxwell introduced the displacement current $\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf{E}$ precisely to ensure this consistency.

Probability Continuity

Quantum Mechanics

For wavefunction $\psi(\mathbf{r},t)$ obeying the Schrödinger equation:

$$\rho = |\psi|^2, \quad \mathbf{J} = \frac{\hbar}{2mi}\left(\psi^*\nabla\psi - \psi\nabla\psi^*\right)$$ $$\frac{\partial|\psi|^2}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$$

Probability is locally conserved — the quantum particle cannot teleport. If the probability of finding it in one region decreases, there must be a probability current flowing away from that region. This is a necessary condition for a consistent probabilistic interpretation of QM.

Energy Continuity

Poynting's Theorem Revisited

$$\frac{\partial u_{EM}}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{S} = -\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$$

Including mechanical energy density $u_\text{mech}$ and defining total energy density $u = u_{EM} + u_\text{mech}$, the source term vanishes and we obtain a pure continuity equation for total energy. The Poynting vector $\mathbf{S}$ is the energy flux — the power crossing a unit area — carried by the electromagnetic field.

Relativistic Form

4-Current & Covariant Expression

In special relativity, the continuity equation takes its most compact form:

$$j^\mu = (\rho c,\, \mathbf{J}), \qquad \partial_\mu j^\mu = 0$$ $$\frac{\partial(c\rho)}{\partial(ct)} + \frac{\partial J_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial J_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial J_z}{\partial z} = 0$$

The 4-divergence $\partial_\mu j^\mu=0$ is Lorentz-covariant: if it holds in one inertial frame, it holds in all. This is the relativistic guarantee of charge conservation — time and space components mix under boosts, but the divergence remains zero.

Source Terms & Non-Conservation

When there is creation or destruction of the conserved quantity (e.g., by sources or sinks), the continuity equation is modified:

$$\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = \sigma(\mathbf{r},t)$$
$\sigma > 0$: source (creation)  |  $\sigma < 0$: sink (destruction)  |  $\sigma = 0$: true conservation

Examples: the heat equation with internal heat generation $\sigma = \dot{q}/(\rho c_p)$; the Schrödinger equation with a complex potential $V = V_R - iV_I$ (optical model in nuclear physics) where $V_I > 0$ represents absorption; the diffusion-reaction equation in chemistry $\partial_t C = D\nabla^2 C + R(C)$ where $R$ is a reaction rate. In each case, the non-zero $\sigma$ reflects the exchange of the conserved quantity with another degree of freedom not explicitly tracked.

🔭

The transition from a conservation law with $\sigma=0$ to one with $\sigma \neq 0$ is not truly a violation of conservation — it reflects an incomplete accounting. When you include all degrees of freedom of the universe, $\sigma$ always vanishes. The optical model absorption of flux, for instance, represents the flux being scattered to unobserved channels, not genuinely disappearing. True conservation is always recovered in the complete, closed system.
