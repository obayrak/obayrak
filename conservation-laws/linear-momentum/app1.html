01 — Definition

What is Linear Momentum?

Linear momentum is the product of an object's mass and its velocity — a vector quantity pointing in the same direction as the motion. It encodes both how much matter is moving and how fast it moves.

Momentum
$$p = m · v$$

p is momentum (kg·m/s), m is mass (kg), v is velocity (m/s). A vector quantity.

Newton's 2nd Law
$$F = dp/dt$$

Force equals the rate of change of momentum — the deeper form of $$F = ma$$.

Impulse–Momentum
$$J = ΔpΔt$$

Impulse (force × time) equals the change in momentum of the object.

The total linear momentum of an isolated system — one subject to no net external force — remains constant in both magnitude and direction, regardless of what happens internally.

This law is not merely a convenient rule; it is a consequence of the spatial homogeneity of the universe — the fact that the laws of physics are the same everywhere, as shown by Noether's theorem. Every conservation law traces back to a symmetry of nature.

02 — The Conservation Law

When Momentum is Conserved

Momentum is conserved whenever the net external force on a system is zero. Internal forces (the forces objects in the system exert on each other) always come in equal and opposite pairs — Newton's third law guarantees that they cancel, contributing nothing to the total.

Two-Body Collision
$$m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁' + m₂v₂'$$

Before and after a collision, the vector sum of momenta is identical.

Multi-Body System
$$Σ mᵢvᵢ = constant$$

Extends to any number of particles; each component (x, y, z) is independently conserved.

Component Form
$$pₓ, pᵧ, p_z$$ each conserved

Momentum conserved in directions where external force is zero — even partially.

Note that momentum can be conserved in one direction even when it isn't in another. A ball rolling off a table conserves horizontal momentum (no horizontal force) while gravity changes the vertical component.

03 — Types of Collisions

Momentum in Collisions

Collisions are the most vivid arena for momentum conservation. All collision types conserve momentum, but they differ in whether kinetic energy is preserved.

🎱
Elastic

Perfectly Elastic

Both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. Ideal billiard balls, atomic collisions. KEbefore = KEafter.

🚗
Inelastic

Inelastic

Momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is partially lost to heat, sound, or deformation. Real-world collisions are generally inelastic.

🫨
Perfectly Inelastic

Perfectly Inelastic

Objects stick together after colliding — maximum kinetic energy loss while still conserving total momentum. vf = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/(m₁+m₂).

04 — Real-World Examples

Momentum in the World

The conservation of momentum governs phenomena from the microscopic to the cosmic.

01

Rocket Propulsion

A rocket expels exhaust gases backward. By momentum conservation, the rocket itself is pushed forward. No external surface to "push against" is needed — momentum of the whole system remains zero.

02

Gun Recoil

A gun and bullet begin at rest (total p = 0). When fired, the bullet gains momentum forward; the gun recoils backward with equal and opposite momentum — the classic action-reaction pair.

03

Billiard Ball Collisions

Near-elastic collisions where the cue ball can nearly stop and transfer almost all its momentum to the target ball — a direct demonstration of the conservation law.

04

Particle Physics & Detectors

When particles collide in accelerators, physicists reconstruct invisible neutral particles by measuring the momenta of visible products and applying conservation to find the "missing" momentum.

05

Astronaut in Space

With no external forces, an astronaut who throws an object away from the spacecraft will drift in the opposite direction — conserving the system's total momentum of zero.

06

Stellar Formation & Supernova

When a massive star explodes symmetrically, ejected material carries momentum in all directions that sums to zero — matching the initial momentum of the star at rest.
