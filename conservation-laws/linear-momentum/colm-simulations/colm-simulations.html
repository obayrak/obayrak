Conservation of Linear Momentum

🎱 Billiard Ball Collisions — Conservation Laws

When two billiard balls collide, linear momentum is always conserved regardless of how elastic the collision is. The coefficient of restitution $e$ determines how much kinetic energy is retained. At $e=1$ the collision is perfectly elastic (KE conserved); at $e=0$ it is perfectly inelastic (maximum KE lost). The 2-D geometry is resolved by decomposing velocities along the line of centres $\hat{n}$ and the perpendicular tangent $\hat{t}$.

$$\vec{p}_{\rm total}=m_1\vec{v}_1+m_2\vec{v}_2=\text{const}\qquad\text{(always conserved, any }e\text{)}$$
$$KE=\tfrac{1}{2}m_1v_1^2+\tfrac{1}{2}m_2v_2^2=\text{const}\qquad\text{(elastic only,}\;e=1\text{)}$$

Key Parameters

$e$Coefficient of restitution: $e = \dfrac{(\vec v_2'-\vec v_1')\cdot\hat n}{(\vec v_1-\vec v_2)\cdot\hat n}$dimensionless
$b$Impact parameter — perpendicular offset of ball centres at moment of contactm
$\hat{n}$Unit normal along the line of centres at moment of collision—
$\hat{t}$Tangent $\perp\hat{n}$ — frictionless contact leaves this component unchanged—
Why is momentum always conserved?
During the collision the only forces acting between the two balls are the internal contact forces, which by Newton's Third Law are equal and opposite. They cancel in the vector sum, so $d\vec{p}_{\rm total}/dt = 0$ regardless of $e$.
When is KE not conserved?
Any $e < 1$ means some kinetic energy is irreversibly converted to heat, sound, and deformation. Only in the idealised elastic limit $e=1$ does KE survive the collision intact.
∂ Derivation of Post-Collision Velocities (2-D, any $e$)
Compute the unit normal and decompose both velocity vectors
$$\hat{n}=\frac{\vec r_2-\vec r_1}{|\vec r_2-\vec r_1|},\quad\hat{t}=\hat{n}^\perp\qquad v_{in}=\vec v_i\cdot\hat n,\quad v_{it}=\vec v_i\cdot\hat t\quad(i=1,2)$$
Frictionless contact — tangential components are unchanged
$$v_{1t}'=v_{1t},\qquad v_{2t}'=v_{2t}$$

No friction means no impulse acts along $\hat t$; only the normal components are modified.

Apply 1-D momentum conservation + coefficient of restitution along $\hat{n}$
$$m_1 v_{1n}+m_2 v_{2n}=m_1 v_{1n}'+m_2 v_{2n}'\qquad\text{(momentum)}$$
$$v_{2n}'-v_{1n}'=e\,(v_{1n}-v_{2n})\qquad\text{(restitution)}$$
Solve the 2×2 system for post-collision normal components
$$v_{1n}'=\frac{(m_1-e\,m_2)\,v_{1n}+(1+e)\,m_2\,v_{2n}}{m_1+m_2},\qquad v_{2n}'=\frac{(m_2-e\,m_1)\,v_{2n}+(1+e)\,m_1\,v_{1n}}{m_1+m_2}$$
Reconstruct full 2-D post-collision velocity vectors
$$\vec v_1'=v_{1n}'\hat n+v_{1t}'\hat t,\qquad\vec v_2'=v_{2n}'\hat n+v_{2t}'\hat t$$
Verify momentum conservation for any $e$
$$m_1\vec v_1'+m_2\vec v_2'=m_1\vec v_1+m_2\vec v_2\quad\checkmark\qquad\text{(substitution confirms for all }e\in[0,1])$$
∫ Key Physics Cases

① Equal-mass elastic head-on  $m_1=m_2,\;e=1,\;b=0$

$$v_1'=v_2,\quad v_2'=v_1\;\Longrightarrow\text{velocities exchange completely — 100\% KE transferred}$$

The striking ball stops dead; all momentum is handed to the stationary ball. This is why Newton's cradle works.

② Perfectly inelastic  $e=0$

$$v'=\frac{m_1 v_1+m_2 v_2}{m_1+m_2},\qquad\Delta KE_{\rm lost}=\frac{m_1 m_2(v_1-v_2)^2}{2(m_1+m_2)}>0$$

③ Heavy strikes light at rest  $m_1\gg m_2,\;e=1,\;b=0$

$$v_1'\approx v_1,\quad v_2'\approx 2v_1\;\Longrightarrow\text{light ball ejected at nearly twice the incident speed}$$

④ Glancing collision  $b\to r_1+r_2$

$$\hat n\perp\vec v_1\;\Longrightarrow v_{1n}=0\;\Longrightarrow\text{no normal-component exchange; balls barely deflect}$$

⑤ Off-centre elastic equal-mass  $m_1=m_2,\;e=1,\;0<b<2r$

$$\vec v_1'\perp\vec v_2'\;\text{always}\;\Longrightarrow\text{post-collision angle between velocity vectors is exactly }90°$$
This $90°$ rule is a beautiful geometric consequence of both momentum and energy conservation for equal masses.

🚀 Rocket Propulsion — Variable-Mass System

A rocket conserves total system momentum (rocket + all expelled exhaust). In free space, if the rocket starts from rest, the system's total momentum is zero at all times: the rocket's forward momentum exactly equals the backward momentum of the cumulative exhaust cloud.

$$\vec p_{\rm total}=m(t)\,\vec v(t)+\vec p_{\rm exhaust}(t)=m_0\,v_0=\text{const}$$
$$\boxed{\Delta v = v_e\ln\!\left(\frac{m_0}{m_f}\right)}\quad\text{Tsiolkovsky Rocket Equation}$$

$\Delta v$ is the velocity change achieved, $v_e$ the effective exhaust speed, $m_0$ initial mass, and $m_f$ final (dry) mass after all propellant is spent.

Key Parameters

$v_e$Effective exhaust velocity (specific impulse × g)m/s
$m_0$Initial total mass (structure + propellant + payload)kg
$m_f$Final dry mass (structure + payload only)kg
$\dot m$Propellant mass flow rate (kg/s burned)kg/s
$F_T$Thrust $= v_e\,|\dot m|$N
No external surface needed
Unlike a car pushing on the road, a rocket creates thrust entirely from internal momentum exchange. This is why rockets work in the vacuum of space — no medium is required.
Logarithmic diminishing returns
The rocket equation is logarithmic. Doubling $\Delta v$ requires squaring the mass ratio, making high-$\Delta v$ missions extremely propellant-expensive without staging.
∂ Derivation of the Tsiolkovsky Rocket Equation
Set up the variable-mass system at time $t$

Rocket has mass $m$, velocity $v$. In time $dt$: mass $dm < 0$ is ejected at velocity $v - v_e$ (exhaust speed relative to rocket). Remaining rocket has mass $m + dm$, velocity $v + dv$.

Apply momentum conservation over interval $dt$
$$m\,v = (m+dm)(v+dv) + (-dm)(v-v_e)$$

Note: the ejected mass is $-dm > 0$ and moves at $v - v_e$ in the lab frame.

Expand and cancel the $mv$ terms
$$0 = m\,dv + dm\,dv + dm\,v - dm\,v + dm\,v_e$$
$$0 = m\,dv + v_e\,dm\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m\,dv = -v_e\,dm$$

The second-order term $dm\,dv$ is negligible.

Separate variables and integrate
$$\int_{v_0}^{v_f} dv = -v_e\int_{m_0}^{m_f}\frac{dm}{m} \;\Longrightarrow\; \Delta v = v_e\ln\!\left(\frac{m_0}{m_f}\right)$$
Verify system momentum is conserved (no external forces)
$$p_{\rm rocket}(t)+p_{\rm exhaust}(t)=m(t)\,v(t)+(m_0 v_0 - m(t)\,v(t))=m_0 v_0\quad\checkmark$$
∫ Solution Profiles & Real-World Numbers

Velocity as a function of remaining mass

$$v(m) = v_0 + v_e\ln\!\left(\frac{m_0}{m}\right)$$

Thrust and mass flow

$$F_T = v_e\,|\dot m|,\qquad a(t)=\frac{F_T}{m(t)}=\frac{v_e|\dot m|}{m_0 - |\dot m|\,t}$$

Acceleration increases as propellant burns off and the rocket becomes lighter.

💥 Explosion & Recoil — Internal Energy Release

An explosion is the reverse of a perfectly inelastic collision. Internal energy stored as chemical, mechanical, or electrical potential is converted to kinetic energy of the fragments. Since no external forces act during the brief event, total momentum is conserved exactly.

$$M\,v_{\rm cm} = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2' = \text{const}\qquad\text{(conserved)}$$
$$\text{Energy released: }\;E_{\rm exp}=\tfrac{1}{2}m_1(v_1')^2+\tfrac{1}{2}m_2(v_2')^2-\tfrac{1}{2}M\,v_{\rm cm}^2>0$$

In the centre-of-mass frame the fragments always fly apart with equal and opposite momenta, regardless of the mass ratio. The fragment velocities in the lab frame depend on both the mass ratio and $v_{\rm cm}$.

Key Parameters

$v_{\rm cm}$Centre-of-mass velocity before and after: $(m_1 v_{1,i}+m_2 v_{2,i})/M$m/s
$E$Total kinetic energy released by the explosionJ
$\mu$Reduced mass $\mu=m_1 m_2/(m_1+m_2)$kg
Gun recoil
A gun and bullet start at rest ($p=0$). The bullet of mass $m$ leaves at speed $v$; the gun of mass $M$ recoils at $-mv/M$. Total momentum remains zero.
Rocket staging
An spent stage is "exploded" away from the payload. The stage flies back as the payload accelerates forward — momentum conserved, payload gains $\Delta v$.
∂ Derivation of Post-Explosion Fragment Velocities
Compute the CM velocity — conserved throughout
$$v_{\rm cm}=\frac{m_1 v_{1,i}+m_2 v_{2,i}}{m_1+m_2}$$
Transform to CM frame (primes = CM-frame quantities)
$$\tilde v_{1,i}=v_{1,i}-v_{\rm cm},\quad\tilde v_{2,i}=v_{2,i}-v_{\rm cm}$$

Note $m_1\tilde v_{1,i}+m_2\tilde v_{2,i}=0$ by construction.

In the CM frame, post-explosion momenta are equal and opposite
$$m_1\tilde v_{1,f}+m_2\tilde v_{2,f}=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\tilde v_{2,f}=-\frac{m_1}{m_2}\tilde v_{1,f}$$
Use energy release to fix the speed in the CM frame
$$E_{\rm exp}=\tfrac{1}{2}m_1\tilde v_{1,f}^2+\tfrac{1}{2}m_2\tilde v_{2,f}^2=\tfrac{1}{2}\mu\left(\tilde v_{1,f}-\tilde v_{2,f}\right)^2$$
$$|\tilde v_{1,f}-\tilde v_{2,f}|=\sqrt{\frac{2E_{\rm exp}}{\mu}},\quad\mu=\frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1+m_2}$$
Solve and transform back to lab frame
$$\tilde v_{1,f}=+\sqrt{\frac{2E_{\rm exp}\,m_2}{m_1(m_1+m_2)}},\quad\tilde v_{2,f}=-\sqrt{\frac{2E_{\rm exp}\,m_1}{m_2(m_1+m_2)}}$$
$$v_{1,f}=v_{\rm cm}+\tilde v_{1,f},\qquad v_{2,f}=v_{\rm cm}+\tilde v_{2,f}$$
Verify momentum conservation in lab frame
$$m_1 v_{1,f}+m_2 v_{2,f}=m_1(v_{\rm cm}+\tilde v_{1,f})+m_2(v_{\rm cm}+\tilde v_{2,f})=(m_1+m_2)v_{\rm cm}+0=M v_{\rm cm}\;\checkmark$$
∫ Special Cases & Real Examples

① System initially at rest ($v_{\rm cm}=0$)

$$v_{1,f}=+\sqrt{\frac{2E\,m_2}{m_1 M}},\quad v_{2,f}=-\sqrt{\frac{2E\,m_1}{m_2 M}}\;\Longrightarrow m_1 v_{1,f}+m_2 v_{2,f}=0\;\checkmark$$

② Equal masses ($m_1=m_2=m$)

$$|v_{1,f}|=|v_{2,f}|=\sqrt{\frac{E}{m}}\;\Longrightarrow\text{fragments fly apart symmetrically}$$

③ Gun recoil ($m_1=m_{\rm bullet}\ll m_2=M_{\rm gun}$, from rest)

$$v_{\rm bullet}\approx\sqrt{\frac{2E}{m_{\rm bullet}}},\quad v_{\rm gun}\approx -\frac{m_{\rm bullet}}{M_{\rm gun}}\sqrt{\frac{2E}{m_{\rm bullet}}}\;\ll v_{\rm bullet}$$

The gun barely moves while the bullet exits at high speed — momentum conserved by the large velocity of the light bullet compensating for its small mass.

🔫 Ballistic Pendulum — Two-Stage Conservation

The ballistic pendulum is the classic device for measuring projectile speeds that are otherwise too fast to time directly. It works in two distinct stages that conserve different quantities — a subtlety often missed. Stage 1 (embedding) conserves momentum only: kinetic energy is lost to deformation and heat. Stage 2 (the swing) conserves mechanical energy only: momentum is not conserved here because the pivot exerts an external horizontal force on the system.

$$\text{Stage 1 (collision): } m\,v = (m+M)\,v'\qquad\text{momentum conserved, KE is NOT}$$
$$\text{Stage 2 (swing): } \tfrac12(m+M)v'^2=(m+M)gh\qquad\text{energy conserved, momentum is NOT}$$

This staged structure is what makes the device work as a measurement instrument: an unmeasurable quantity (bullet speed $v$) is converted, via an irreversible but momentum-conserving collision, into a measurable one (swing height / angle).

Exact vs. approximate note
The formulas above describe the exact idealized first swing with no air resistance or pivot friction — the standard textbook derivation. The simulation below adds a small damping term so you can watch the swing decay across several oscillations and see mechanical energy convert into heat $Q$ in real time; this is a approx (damped) refinement layered on top of the exact collision physics, not a correction to it.

Key Parameters

$m$Bullet (projectile) masskg
$M$Pendulum bob masskg
$v$Incoming bullet speed (the unknown being measured)m/s
$L$Pendulum length, pivot to bob centrem
$\theta$Maximum swing angle from verticaldeg
$h$Height risen by the bob+bullet centre of massm
Why not just use momentum throughout?
Once the bullet is embedded, the only forces on the bob+bullet system are gravity and the pivot's constraint force. The pivot force has a horizontal component during the swing, so horizontal momentum is not conserved after Stage 1 — only mechanical energy is, since the pivot does no work.
Why not just use energy throughout?
The embedding collision is inherently inelastic (deformation, friction, heat). Energy is not conserved across Stage 1 — only momentum is, because the collision happens too fast for external forces (gravity, pivot) to impart significant impulse.
∂ Derivation: Recovering $v$ from Swing Height
Stage 1 — momentum conservation across the embedding collision
$$m\,v + M\cdot 0 = (m+M)\,v'\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v'=\frac{m}{m+M}\,v$$

The collision is treated as instantaneous, so gravity's impulse during it is negligible.

Stage 1 — energy lost in the collision (for reference, not used further)
$$\Delta KE_{\rm lost}=\tfrac12 m v^2-\tfrac12(m+M)v'^2=\tfrac12\frac{mM}{m+M}v^2$$
Stage 2 — mechanical energy conservation during the swing
$$\tfrac12(m+M)v'^2=(m+M)g h\quad\Longrightarrow\quad h=\frac{v'^2}{2g}$$
Relate rise height to swing angle via pendulum geometry
$$h=L(1-\cos\theta)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta=\arccos\!\left(1-\frac{h}{L}\right)$$
Combine both stages to solve forward ($v\to\theta$) or invert ($\theta\to v$)
$$\boxed{\theta=\arccos\!\left(1-\frac{1}{2gL}\left(\frac{m}{m+M}\right)^{2}v^{2}\right)}$$
$$\boxed{v=\frac{m+M}{m}\sqrt{2gL(1-\cos\theta)}}\qquad\text{(the measurement formula)}$$
Verify: Stage 1 momentum and Stage 2 energy each check out independently
$$p_{\rm before}=mv,\quad p_{\rm after\ collision}=(m+M)v'=mv\;\checkmark\qquad E_{\rm bottom}=\tfrac12(m+M)v'^2=(m+M)gh=E_{\rm top}\;\checkmark$$
∫ Special Cases & Worked Example

① Light bullet, heavy bob  $m\ll M$

$$v'\approx\frac{m}{M}v\;\Longrightarrow\;\theta\ \text{small}\;\Longrightarrow\;\theta\approx\frac{m}{M}\sqrt{\frac{v^2}{gL}}$$

Most of the bullet's kinetic energy is lost in Stage 1; only a small fraction survives to lift the (much heavier) bob.

② Fraction of kinetic energy that survives Stage 1

$$\frac{KE_{\rm after}}{KE_{\rm before}}=\frac{\tfrac12(m+M)v'^2}{\tfrac12 m v^2}=\frac{m}{m+M}$$

For $m\ll M$ this ratio $\to 0$: nearly all the energy is dissipated, yet momentum conservation still lets us recover $v$ exactly.

⚫ Newton's Cradle — Chained Momentum Transfer

A Newton's cradle is a chain of $N$ identical pendulum bobs hung in a row, each just touching its neighbours at rest. Lift and release the end ball(s): the impulse propagates through the chain of near-elastic collisions, and — for equal masses — almost all of it emerges as motion of the ball(s) at the opposite end, while the balls in between barely move.

$$\text{Each contact conserves momentum: }\;m v_i + m v_{i+1} = m v_i' + m v_{i+1}'$$
$$\text{Whole system: } E_{\rm total}(t) = \sum_i KE_i(t) + \sum_i PE_i(t) + Q(t) = E_{\rm total}(0)\quad\text{always}$$
An important subtlety this module makes explicit
Total system momentum is exactly conserved only at the instant of each ball-to-ball contact — that is a genuine two-body collision with no external horizontal force. It is not conserved continuously through the gravity-driven swing between collisions, because each ball's own string/pivot exerts an external horizontal restoring force on it. Total energy, by contrast, is conserved continuously throughout — provided you track where it goes: kinetic, gravitational potential, and heat $Q$ lost to air resistance, string friction, and (for $e<1$) inelastic contacts.

Key Parameters

$N$Number of balls in the chaincount
$m$Mass of each ball (identical)kg
$L$String length, pivot to ball centrem
$\theta_0$Initial pull-back angle of the lifted end ball(s)deg
$e$Coefficient of restitution at each contact (real cradles: $e\approx0.97$–$0.99$)dimensionless
$c$Per-ball damping (air resistance / pivot friction)1/s
$Q$Cumulative heat: collision losses + friction lossesJ
Why does only the far ball move?
For equal masses and $e=1$, a head-on elastic collision exchanges velocities completely (see the Billiard Collision module, Case ①). The impulse hands off cleanly from ball to ball down the chain, so the middle balls end up at rest and only the last ball flies out.
Why real cradles slowly "die down"
With $e$ slightly below 1, a small fraction of kinetic energy is lost to heat at every single contact. Across hundreds of clacks, this — plus air resistance — drains the visible swing amplitude even though every joule is still accounted for as heat $Q$.
∂ Derivation: Chain Collision & Energy Bookkeeping
Each ball is an independent damped pendulum between contacts
$$\ddot\theta_i = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta_i - c\,\dot\theta_i \qquad \text{(same damped-pendulum ODE as the Ballistic Pendulum module)}$$
Contact detection: balls $i,i{+}1$ collide when their horizontal gap closes to zero
$$x_i = L\sin\theta_i \quad\text{(horizontal displacement)}\qquad x_{i+1}-x_i \le 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{contact}$$
Resolve the contact as a 1-D restitution collision (identical to the Billiard module, Step 4)
$$\dot x_i'=\frac{(m_i-e\,m_{i+1})\dot x_i+(1+e)m_{i+1}\dot x_{i+1}}{m_i+m_{i+1}},\quad \dot x_{i+1}'=\frac{(m_{i+1}-e\,m_i)\dot x_{i+1}+(1+e)m_i\dot x_i}{m_i+m_{i+1}}$$

Convert back to angular velocity via $\dot\theta = \dot x / (L\cos\theta) \approx \dot x/L$ near the bottom of the swing.

Heat from each collision adds directly to the running total
$$\Delta Q_{\rm contact}=\left(\tfrac12 m_i\dot x_i^2+\tfrac12 m_{i+1}\dot x_{i+1}^2\right)-\left(\tfrac12 m_i\dot x_i'^2+\tfrac12 m_{i+1}\dot x_{i+1}'^2\right)\ge 0$$
Heat from friction accumulates continuously between contacts (as in the Ballistic Pendulum module)
$$\dot Q_{\rm friction}=\sum_i m_i L^2 c\,\dot\theta_i^2 \ge 0$$
Full system energy identity — verified numerically each frame
$$\sum_i\left[\tfrac12 m_i L^2\dot\theta_i^2 + m_i g L(1-\cos\theta_i)\right] + Q_{\rm collision}+Q_{\rm friction} = E_{\rm total}(0)\quad\checkmark$$
∫ Special Cases

① Single ball lifted, perfectly elastic  $e=1,\;c=0$

$$\text{Ball 1 stops dead at first contact; ball }N\text{ leaves with exactly the incoming speed}$$

This is the idealized "classic" cradle behaviour — an unbroken chain of complete velocity exchanges (Billiard module, Case ①), repeated $N{-}1$ times down the line.

② Two balls lifted together

$$\text{Two balls leave the far end together, each near the incoming speed} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{momentum AND kinetic energy both split evenly across 2 balls, not 1}$$

A single ball leaving with double the momentum but the same speed as two balls would carry only half the kinetic energy of two — which is why the cradle "chooses" the two-ball outcome for a two-ball input, not a one-ball-at-double-speed outcome.

③ Slightly inelastic, real-world $e\approx0.98$

$$\text{Amplitude decays a few percent per full pass; }Q_{\rm collision}\text{ grows by a small increment at every clack}$$

This is the cleanest illustration in the whole suite of the difference between "conserved because no external force" (momentum, only at contact) and "conserved because accounted for as heat" (energy, always).

🎯 Realistic Billiards — A Table Full of External Forces

A real pool table is a richer momentum story than a single isolated collision. Ball-to-ball impacts conserve momentum exactly, just like the Billiard Collision module — but two external forces are constantly acting on every ball as it rolls: rolling friction from the felt, and impulsive cushion forces from the rails. Both are external to the ball, so between and during these events the total momentum of the ball system is not conserved — only at the instant of a ball-ball contact does it hold exactly, exactly as in the Newton's Cradle module.

$$\sum_i m_i\vec v_i(t)\;\text{conserved ONLY at the instant of a ball–ball contact}$$
$$E_{\rm total}(t)=\sum_i\tfrac12 m_i v_i^2 + \sum_i\tfrac12 I_i\omega_i^2 + Q(t) = E_{\rm total}(0)\qquad\text{always (energy bookkept as heat)}$$

Two extra ingredients appear here that the earlier collision modules simplified away: rolling friction continuously bleeds translational kinetic energy into heat, and each ball can carry spin ($\omega$, "English"), which is invisible to a simple momentum count but reappears as a sideways kick whenever the ball strikes a cushion.

Key Parameters

$\mu_r$Rolling friction coefficient — decelerates every ball's translational speeddimensionless
$e_{bb}$Restitution at ball–ball contact (real balls: $\approx0.95$–$0.98$)dimensionless
$e_c$Restitution at cushion (rail) contactdimensionless
$\omega$Spin ("English") about the vertical axis, imparted at the breakrad/s
$k_s$Spin-to-cushion coupling — fraction of spin converted to sideways velocity on rail contactdimensionless
Why friction breaks momentum conservation
The felt exerts a continuous horizontal force opposing each ball's motion. It's external to the ball system, so $\sum \vec p$ shrinks smoothly to zero as every ball eventually stops — even though not one joule of that lost momentum "goes" anywhere as momentum (it becomes heat via $\vec F\!\cdot\!d\vec x$, tracked as energy, not momentum).
Why cushions are impulsive, external, and spin-coupled
A rail delivers a large force over a very short time (an impulse) that reverses the ball's normal velocity component. Because the rail is bolted to the table (effectively infinite mass), it can absorb or supply momentum freely — and if the ball is spinning, friction between ball and rail during that brief contact converts some spin into extra sideways velocity, a real effect billiards players call "English."
∂ Derivation: Friction, Cushions, and Multi-Ball Collisions
Rolling friction — linear deceleration of speed
$$\vec F_{\rm fric}=-\mu_r m g\,\hat v \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d|\vec v|}{dt}=-\mu_r g\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |\vec v|(t)=\max\!\big(0,\,|\vec v|_0-\mu_r g\,t\big)$$

Direction $\hat v$ is preserved (friction opposes motion only); a ball rolls in a straight line while decelerating and stops in finite time $t_{\rm stop}=|\vec v|_0/(\mu_r g)$.

Spin decay — pivoting friction bleeds off English over time
$$\frac{d\omega}{dt}=-c_\omega\,\omega \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega(t)=\omega_0\,e^{-c_\omega t}$$
Cushion contact — reflect the normal component, apply restitution, couple spin into tangential velocity
$$v_n' = -e_c\,v_n,\qquad v_t' = v_t + k_s\,\omega R,\qquad \omega' = (1-k_s)\,\omega$$

The wall (infinite effective mass) absorbs whatever normal momentum is needed — the ball's momentum alone is not conserved here, only redirected by an external impulse. Part of the spin converts into extra tangential speed (the "curve" off the rail); the rest is left in $\omega'$.

Ball–ball contact — identical 2-D restitution physics as the Billiard Collision module
$$v_{1n}'=\frac{(m_1-e_{bb}m_2)v_{1n}+(1+e_{bb})m_2v_{2n}}{m_1+m_2},\quad v_{2n}'=\frac{(m_2-e_{bb}m_1)v_{2n}+(1+e_{bb})m_1v_{1n}}{m_1+m_2}$$

Tangential components pass through unchanged (frictionless contact assumption); this exactly conserves $m_1\vec v_1+m_2\vec v_2$ for that pair, instantaneously, regardless of what friction or cushions do a moment before or after.

Heat bookkeeping — every mechanism that isn't a ball-ball collision adds to $Q$
$$\dot Q_{\rm friction}=\sum_i \mu_r m_i g\,|\vec v_i|,\qquad \Delta Q_{\rm cushion}=\tfrac12 m(v_n^2-v_n'^2)\ge0,\qquad \Delta Q_{bb}=\Delta KE_{\rm collision}\ge0$$
Full identity — verified every frame in the simulation
$$\sum_i\tfrac12 m_i v_i^2(t) + Q_{\rm friction}(t)+Q_{\rm cushion}(t)+Q_{bb}(t) = E_{\rm total}(0)\quad\checkmark$$
∫ Key Regimes on the Table

① Break shot  high speed, tight cluster

$$\text{Cue ball carries large }|\vec p|\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{a rapid cascade of ball–ball contacts redistributes it across the cluster in milliseconds}$$

Each individual contact conserves momentum exactly, but the system's visible momentum keeps changing shape as balls scatter, bounce off rails, and decelerate — only $\sum m_iv_i^2/2+Q$ stays fixed throughout.

② Stun shot with backspin  $\omega<0$

$$\text{Cue ball's forward }v\text{ can reach zero at contact while }\omega\ne0\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{ball "sits" or draws back after impact}$$

③ Rail cut with English  $k_s\omega R \ne 0$

$$v_t' = v_t + k_s\omega R\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{rebound angle deviates from the simple mirror-reflection law}\;\theta_{\rm out}\ne\theta_{\rm in}$$

Without spin ($\omega=0$), a cushion bounce is a clean mirror reflection. With English, the outgoing angle bends toward the direction of spin — this is the entire basis of "banking" shots with side spin.

④ Long-run friction limit

$$\text{As }t\to t_{\rm stop}\text{ for every ball: }\sum_i m_i\vec v_i(t)\to 0,\quad Q_{\rm friction}\to \sum_i\tfrac12 m_i v_{i,0}^2\text{(that ball's share)}$$
Eventually every ball stops and all kinetic energy has become heat in the felt — momentum is not "conserved to zero," it simply isn't a conserved quantity once external friction and cushion forces are in play. Energy, tracked including $Q$, is conserved the entire time.

📡 Compton Scattering — Relativistic Photon–Electron Momentum

When a photon scatters off a free (or effectively free) electron, both energy and relativistic momentum are conserved. Unlike the billiard-ball case, the photon's momentum is carried by its energy alone ($p=E/c$, no rest mass), and the electron's recoil must be treated relativistically at high photon energies. The photon loses energy to the recoiling electron and shifts to a longer wavelength — the signature Compton shift.

$$\vec p_{\gamma,0} = \vec p_{\gamma}' + \vec p_e \qquad\text{(momentum conserved)}$$
$$E_{\gamma,0} + m_ec^2 = E_\gamma' + E_e \qquad\text{(total energy conserved)}$$
$$\boxed{\Delta\lambda = \lambda' - \lambda_0 = \lambda_C(1-\cos\theta)},\qquad \lambda_C=\frac{h}{m_ec}=2.426\ \text{pm}$$

Key Parameters

$E_0$Incident photon energykeV
$\theta$Photon scattering angle (from original direction)deg
$\phi$Electron recoil angle (on the opposite side)deg
$\lambda_C$Compton wavelength of the electronpm
$T_e$Kinetic energy transferred to the recoiling electronkeV
Why the shift is independent of $E_0$
$\Delta\lambda$ depends only on the scattering angle $\theta$, not on the incident photon's energy — a direct consequence of momentum conservation with a massless-momentum carrier. This is what makes Compton scattering a clean test of photon quantization.
Backscatter limit ($\theta=180°$)
Maximum energy loss occurs at direct backscatter: $\Delta\lambda_{\max}=2\lambda_C\approx4.86$ pm. At high $E_0$ this can mean the photon gives up most of its energy to the electron.
∂ Derivation of the Compton Formula
Write 2-D momentum conservation (electron initially at rest)
$$p_0 = p'\cos\theta + p_e\cos\phi \qquad 0 = p'\sin\theta - p_e\sin\phi$$
Eliminate $\phi$ by squaring and adding
$$p_e^2 = p_0^2 + p'^2 - 2p_0p'\cos\theta$$
Apply relativistic energy conservation: $E_0+m_ec^2=E'+E_e$, with $E_e^2=p_e^2c^2+m_e^2c^4$
$$(E_0-E'+m_ec^2)^2 = p_e^2c^2 + m_e^2c^4$$
Substitute $p_e^2c^2$ from Step 2 (using $p_0c=E_0,\ p'c=E'$) and simplify
$$\frac{1}{E'}-\frac{1}{E_0}=\frac{1}{m_ec^2}(1-\cos\theta)$$
Convert energies to wavelengths via $E=hc/\lambda$
$$\boxed{\lambda'-\lambda_0=\frac{h}{m_ec}(1-\cos\theta)}$$
Verify: momentum vector sum closes exactly
$$\vec p_{\gamma,0}-\vec p_\gamma' = \vec p_e \quad\checkmark\qquad\text{(true for any }\theta\text{, any }E_0\text{)}$$
∫ Special Cases & Numbers

① Forward scatter  $\theta\to0$

$$\Delta\lambda\to0,\quad T_e\to0\;\Longrightarrow\text{photon essentially unaffected}$$

② Backscatter  $\theta=180°$

$$\Delta\lambda_{\max}=2\lambda_C=4.86\ \text{pm}\;\Longrightarrow\text{maximum possible energy transfer to the electron}$$

③ Low-energy (Thomson) limit  $E_0\ll m_ec^2=511$ keV

$$E'\approx E_0\;\Longrightarrow\text{negligible recoil, classical elastic scattering}$$

Numerical example: $E_0=100$ keV, $\theta=90°$

At 90°, roughly 16% of the photon's energy transfers to the electron at this incident energy — the fraction grows with $E_0$, which is why Compton scattering dominates the mid-energy attenuation regime in your radiation transport work.

⚛️ Pair Production — A Photon Becomes Matter

A sufficiently energetic photon passing close to an atomic nucleus can convert entirely into an electron-positron pair: $\gamma \to e^-+e^+$. This is the mirror image of annihilation, and — like every process in this suite — it must conserve both energy and momentum exactly. The subtlety here is sharper than in any earlier module: a photon in free space can never do this alone, no matter how much energy it carries.

$$\gamma + \text{nucleus} \;\longrightarrow\; e^- + e^+ + \text{nucleus (recoiling)}$$
$$E_\gamma = T_- + T_+ + 2m_ec^2 \qquad\text{(nucleus kinetic energy negligible for large }M\text{)}$$
$$\boxed{E_{\rm thresh} = 2m_ec^2\left(1+\frac{m_e}{M}\right)}\;\xrightarrow{M\to\infty}\;2m_ec^2 = 1.022\ \text{MeV}$$

Key Parameters

$E_\gamma$Incident photon energyMeV
$m_ec^2$Electron/positron rest energy = 0.511 MeVMeV
$T_\mp$Kinetic energy of the electron / positronMeV
$\varphi$Opening angle between the $e^-$ and $e^+$ tracksdeg
$Mc^2$Rest energy of the recoiling body (nucleus, or electron for "triplet production")MeV
$\vec p_N$Recoil momentum absorbed by the third bodyMeV/c
Why a lone photon can never pair-produce
A photon has zero invariant mass: $E^2-(pc)^2=0$. Any real $e^-e^+$ pair has invariant mass $\ge 2m_ec^2>0$ (equality only if both particles move together with zero relative momentum). A single photon's four-momentum can never equal that of a genuine pair — energy and momentum simply cannot both balance with only two bodies in the final state.
The nucleus as a momentum sink
Exactly like the rail in the Billiards module or the pivot in the Ballistic Pendulum, the nucleus is an external body that can absorb (or supply) essentially unlimited momentum while taking almost no kinetic energy — because $T_N=p_N^2/2M\to0$ as $M\to\infty$. This is the missing third body that makes pair production possible.
∂ Derivation of the Threshold Energy
Write the total four-momentum before the interaction
$$E_{\rm tot}=E_\gamma+Mc^2,\qquad p_{\rm tot}c = E_\gamma\quad\text{(nucleus initially at rest)}$$
Form the conserved invariant mass $W$ of the whole system
$$W^2 = E_{\rm tot}^2-(p_{\rm tot}c)^2 = (E_\gamma+Mc^2)^2-E_\gamma^2$$
At threshold, the final products are created with minimum energy — moving together as one lump
$$W\Big|_{\rm threshold} = 2m_ec^2+Mc^2\quad\text{(pair + nucleus, all at rest relative to each other)}$$
Set the two expressions for $W$ equal and solve for $E_\gamma$
$$2E_{\rm thresh}Mc^2+M^2c^4=(2m_ec^2+Mc^2)^2=4m_e^2c^4+4m_ec^2Mc^2+M^2c^4$$
$$\boxed{E_{\rm thresh}=2m_ec^2\left(1+\frac{m_e}{M}\right)}$$
Above threshold — momentum bookkeeping with an idealized infinite-mass recoil ($M\to\infty$, $T_N\to0$)
$$\vec p_\gamma = \vec p_- + \vec p_+ + \vec p_N,\qquad E_\gamma = T_-+T_++2m_ec^2\;\;(\text{exact in this limit})$$

Any energy split $T_-\!:\!T_+$ and any opening angle $\varphi$ is kinematically allowed — the nucleus simply supplies whatever $\vec p_N$ is needed, at negligible energy cost, exactly like the cushion in the Billiards module absorbing momentum through an external impulse.

Verify: the finite-mass recoil correction really is negligible for real nuclei
$$T_N \approx \frac{p_N^2}{2Mc^2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{for lead, }Mc^2\approx1.9\times10^{5}\text{ MeV},\; T_N\sim10^{-9}\text{ MeV}\;\checkmark\;\text{(utterly negligible)}$$
∫ Special Cases & Numbers

① Heavy nucleus  $M \gg m_e$ (e.g. lead, $Mc^2\approx1.93\times10^5$ MeV)

$$E_{\rm thresh}\to 2m_ec^2 = 1.022\ \text{MeV},\qquad T_N\ \text{immeasurably small}$$

② Triplet production — recoil off an atomic electron  $M=m_e$

$$E_{\rm thresh}=2m_ec^2(1+1)=4m_ec^2=2.044\ \text{MeV}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{double the heavy-nucleus threshold}$$

Because the recoiling electron is exactly as light as the particles being created, it cannot be treated as an infinite-mass anchor — it necessarily carries away real, non-negligible kinetic energy, raising the threshold.

③ High-energy limit  $E_\gamma \gg m_ec^2$

$$\theta_{\rm characteristic}\sim\frac{m_ec^2}{E_\gamma}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{both leptons collimate sharply forward, within a degree or two at }E_\gamma\gtrsim10\ \text{MeV}$$
