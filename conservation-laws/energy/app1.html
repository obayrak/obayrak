// 01 — Foundations

The Physics of
Energy Conservation

Energy conservation is one of the most profound and universal laws in all of physics. First articulated in its modern form in the mid-19th century by Julius Robert Mayer, James Prescott Joule, and Hermann von Helmholtz, it states that the total energy of an isolated system remains constant — it is said to be conserved over time. Energy can be neither created nor destroyed; it can only be transformed or transferred from one form to another.

Forms of Energy in a System

Within any defined system boundary, energy can exist in three primary mechanical forms:

⚡

Kinetic Energy (K)

Energy associated with the motion of an object. For an object of mass m moving with speed v:

K = ½mv²
K is always ≥ 0; maximum when object moves fastest
🌍

Potential Energy (U)

Stored energy due to configuration or position. Gravitational potential energy near Earth's surface:

Ug = mgh
h = height above reference; elastic: Us = ½kx²
🔥

Internal Energy (Eint)

Energy associated with microscopic degrees of freedom — thermal, chemical, nuclear. Dissipated energy (friction, air resistance) increases internal energy:

ΔEint = fk · d
fk = friction force, d = displacement

Mechanical Energy

The mechanical energy of a system is defined as the sum of kinetic and all potential energies:

Emech = K + U
When only conservative forces act, mechanical energy is conserved: ΔEmech = 0

A conservative force is one for which the work done depends only on the initial and final positions of the object, not on the path taken between them. Gravity and the spring force are conservative. Friction is nonconservative — it converts kinetic energy irreversibly into internal energy.

Work–Energy Theorem

The work–kinetic energy theorem forms the bridge between forces and kinetic energy. The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy:

Wnet = ΔK = Kf − Ki
Work = ∫F·ds over the path; positive work increases kinetic energy

The General Conservation Equation

For any system, the most complete statement combines all energy storages and all energy transfer mechanisms across the system boundary:

ΔK + ΔU + ΔEint = W + Q + TMW + TMT + TET + TER
W = work by external forces  |  Q = heat transfer  |  TMW = mechanical waves  |  TMT = matter transfer  |  TET = electrical transmission  |  TER = electromagnetic radiation

In practice, a specific problem reduces this to just the relevant terms. Most classical mechanics problems involve only W, K, U, and sometimes Eint.

// 02 — System Types

Isolated vs.
Nonisolated Systems

The behavior of a system's energy depends critically on whether energy is permitted to cross the system boundary. This distinction is the basis of all energy analysis.

⬛ Isolated System

No energy crosses the system boundary by any method. The total energy is constant; it merely transforms among kinetic, potential, and internal forms.

ΔEsystem = 0

ΔK + ΔU + ΔEint = 0

If nonconservative forces are absent: ΔEmech = 0, or equivalently Ki + Ui = Kf + Uf

🔶 Nonisolated System

Energy crosses the boundary via work, heat, waves, matter transfer, electrical transmission, or electromagnetic radiation. The system energy changes by exactly the net energy transferred.

ΔEsystem = Σ T

ΔK + ΔU + ΔEint = W + Q + ...

Example: gas heated in a container — the system is nonisolated via heat transfer Q.

Energy Transfer Mechanisms

  • Work (W) — mechanical transfer via a macroscopic force through a displacement: W = ∫F·ds
  • Heat (Q) — transfer driven by temperature difference across the boundary (conduction, convection, radiation at the microscopic level)
  • Mechanical Waves (TMW) — energy carried by wave disturbances (sound, seismic waves)
  • Matter Transfer (TMT) — mass crossing the boundary carries kinetic and internal energy with it
  • Electrical Transmission (TET) — electric current carries energy across the boundary (circuits, motors)
  • Electromagnetic Radiation (TER) — photons carry energy (light, radio, infrared, UV, X-rays)
