Theory — Full Derivations
Complete step-by-step derivations behind every statistic and formula shown in the Simulator tab.
1 · Monte Carlo — Hit-or-Miss Estimator
\[ \hat{A} = A_{box}\cdot\frac{N_{in}}{N} \]

Enclose the shape in a box of known area \(A_{box}=(x_{max}-x_{min})(y_{max}-y_{min})\). Draw \(N\) points uniformly at random in the box. For a single point define the indicator

\[ X_i = \begin{cases} 1, & (x_i,y_i)\in\text{shape} \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

Each \(X_i\) is an independent Bernoulli(\(p\)) trial, where \(p = A_{shape}/A_{box}\) is the true area fraction (unknown — this is what we're estimating). The count \(N_{in}=\sum_i X_i\) is then Binomial(\(N,p\)), so \(E[N_{in}]=Np\) and the sample fraction \(\hat p = N_{in}/N\) is an unbiased estimator: \(E[\hat p]=p\). Multiplying by \(A_{box}\) gives the unbiased area estimator \(\hat A = A_{box}\hat p\).

2 · Standard Error — Full Derivation

Variance of a single Bernoulli trial: \(\text{Var}(X_i)=p(1-p)\). Since the \(X_i\) are i.i.d., variance of the sum adds:

\[ \text{Var}(N_{in}) = N\,p(1-p), \qquad \text{Var}(\hat p) = \frac{\text{Var}(N_{in})}{N^2} = \frac{p(1-p)}{N} \]

Since \(\hat A = A_{box}\hat p\) is a constant times \(\hat p\), \(\text{Var}(\hat A)=A_{box}^2\,\text{Var}(\hat p)\). Taking the square root gives the standard error actually used by the app (with \(p\) replaced by its sample estimate \(\hat p\), since \(p\) is unknown):

\[ \sigma_{\hat A} = A_{box}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{N}} \]

This is exactly the "Std Error (σ)" statistic shown in the Precision panel. Notice the \(N\) in the denominator sits inside a square root — this single fact is the origin of the famous \(1/\sqrt{N}\) convergence rate below.

3 · Confidence Interval (Central Limit Theorem)

Because \(N_{in}\) is a sum of \(N\) i.i.d. random variables, the Central Limit Theorem says that for large \(N\), \(\hat p\) is approximately Normal: \(\hat p \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(p,\ \frac{p(1-p)}{N}\right)\). Standardizing, \(Z=(\hat p - p)/\sigma_{\hat p}\) is approximately \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\). Requiring \(P(-z_* \le Z \le z_*) = 0.95\) gives \(z_*=1.96\), so the 95% confidence interval for the area is:

\[ \hat A \pm 1.96\,\sigma_{\hat A} \]

This is the "95% Conf. Interval" band drawn around the MC estimate line on the Convergence plot. It shrinks at the same \(1/\sqrt{N}\) rate as \(\sigma_{\hat A}\) itself, so quadrupling \(N\) only halves the interval width.

4 · Why the Error Scales as \(N^{-1/2}\)

From step 2, \(\sigma_{\hat A} = A_{box}\sqrt{p(1-p)}\cdot N^{-1/2}\). The prefactor \(A_{box}\sqrt{p(1-p)}\) does not depend on \(N\) — only the \(N^{-1/2}\) factor does. So:

\[ \frac{\text{error}(N)}{\text{error}(4N)} = \sqrt{\frac{4N}{N}} = 2 \]

To cut the typical error in half you must quadruple \(N\) — to cut it by 10× you need 100× more samples. This slow rate is the fundamental weakness of Monte Carlo, but critically it holds in any number of dimensions, since the derivation never used the dimensionality of the shape — only that each trial is an independent Bernoulli test.

5 · Simpson's Rule — Derivation from a Parabola

Take one panel \([x_0,x_2]\) of width \(2h\) with midpoint \(x_1=x_0+h\), and fit the unique quadratic \(P(x)=a+bx+cx^2\) through the three points \((x_0,f_0),(x_1,f_1),(x_2,f_2)\). Its exact integral over the panel, worked out from the interpolation coefficients, reduces to:

\[ \int_{x_0}^{x_2} P(x)\,dx = \frac{h}{3}\left[f_0+4f_1+f_2\right] \]

This is the single-panel Simpson rule — it is exact for any polynomial of degree \(\le 3\), not just \(\le 2\), because the odd cubic term integrates to zero by symmetry about the midpoint (a "free" extra order of accuracy).

6 · Composite Rule (n panels)

Split \([a,b]\) into \(n\) equal subintervals (\(n\) even), \(h=(b-a)/n\), nodes \(x_k=a+kh\). Applying the single-panel result to consecutive pairs and summing telescopes the shared endpoints into alternating weights 4, 2, 4, 2, …, 4:

\[ S = \frac{h}{3}\Big[f(x_0)+4f(x_1)+2f(x_2)+4f(x_3)+\cdots+4f(x_{n-1})+f(x_n)\Big] \]

This is exactly the formula the app evaluates in simpson1D() — interior odd-index nodes get weight 4, interior even-index nodes get weight 2, and the two endpoints get weight 1.

7 · Error Term — Where \(h^4\) Comes From

Expand \(f(x)\) in a Taylor series about the panel midpoint \(x_1\) and integrate term by term against the true integral vs. the parabola's prediction. The linear and cubic terms cancel by symmetry (as noted above); the leading surviving discrepancy comes from the 4th-derivative term, giving the classical single-panel error:

\[ E_{panel} = -\frac{h^5}{90}f^{(4)}(\xi), \qquad \xi\in[x_0,x_2] \]

Summing this over \(n/2\) panels (each of width \(h\), total interval length \(b-a=nh\)) gives the composite error:

\[ E_{total} = -\frac{(b-a)h^4}{180}f^{(4)}(\xi) \ \Longrightarrow\ E\propto h^4 \propto n^{-4} \]

Doubling \(n\) (halving \(h\)) shrinks the error by a factor of \(2^4=16\) — dramatically faster than Monte Carlo's factor of 2 for a 4× sample increase. This is why the "Subdivisions (n)" slider converges to machine precision almost instantly for smooth functions, provided \(f^{(4)}\) stays bounded.

8 · 2-D Extension and Why It Degrades on Shapes

For a shape boundary, replace \(f(x,y)\) with the indicator \(I(x,y)=1\) inside, \(0\) outside, and apply Simpson's rule on both axes (tensor product):

\[ \iint I(x,y)\,dx\,dy \approx \frac{h_x h_y}{9}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n} w_x^{(i)}\,w_y^{(j)}\,I(x_i,y_j) \]

exactly as simpson2D() computes, with weights \(w^{(k)}=1\) at endpoints, \(4\) at odd indices, \(2\) at even interior indices. The \(h^4\) error bound derived in step 7 assumed \(f^{(4)}\) exists and is bounded — but \(I(x,y)\) is discontinuous at the shape's boundary, so all derivatives blow up there.

Only the \(O(n)\) grid cells that straddle the boundary (out of \(O(n^2)\) total) contribute an \(O(h)=O(1/n)\) error each; the bulk of interior/exterior cells are exact. Net result: composite error degrades from \(O(n^{-4})\) to \(O(n^{-1})\) — the same order as a crude Riemann sum. This is precisely why, for the Heart Curve and Draw-Custom-Shape modes, Simpson's advantage over Monte Carlo shrinks or disappears.

9 · Worked Numerical Example (Unit Circle)

Take the unit circle, box area \(A_{box}=4\), true area \(\pi\approx 3.14159\) (\(p=\pi/4\approx 0.7854\)).

Monte Carlo, \(N=1000\): \(\sigma_{\hat A} = 4\sqrt{0.7854\cdot 0.2146/1000} \approx 4\times 0.01301 \approx\) 0.052. A typical single run lands within ±0.052 of π about 68% of the time, and within ±0.10 (the 95% CI) most of the time — consistent with the live Precision panel.

Simpson's, \(n=200\) (1-D quarter-circle \(f(x)=\sqrt{1-x^2}\)): with \(f^{(4)}\) bounded away from the endpoints, error stays below \(10^{-6}\) — visibly exact on the Comparison table, versus Monte Carlo needing \(N\approx 10^{10}\) samples to reach the same precision (from \(N\propto 1/\text{error}^2\)). This gap is the entire point of the app: quadrature wins hands-down on smooth 1-D problems, and loses its edge the moment the domain gets an irregular boundary.

When to prefer Simpson's: smooth 1-D integrands, known analytic form, high precision needed with few evaluations.

When to prefer Monte Carlo: irregular 2-D / n-D regions, no closed form, boundary complexity, or high dimensions where grid methods suffer the curse of dimensionality.
