What are Monte Carlo Methods?

Monte Carlo methods are a broad class of computational algorithms that rely on repeated random sampling to obtain numerical results. Named after the famous casino in Monaco, they harness the power of randomness to solve problems that are deterministically intractable.

💡
Key Insight: By sampling from probability distributions and aggregating results, we can estimate integrals, simulate physical systems, and quantify uncertainty — even in high-dimensional spaces where analytical solutions do not exist.
$$\langle f \rangle \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i), \qquad x_i \sim p(x)$$

A Monte Carlo estimator: the expectation of f(x) under distribution p(x) is approximated by the sample mean over N random draws.

🎯

Drawing samples from probability distributions to estimate quantities that resist direct calculation.
📊

Estimates improve as ∝ 1/√N — doubling precision requires quadrupling the number of samples.

Theory

Statistical Mechanics & Randomness

At its core, statistical mechanics describes macroscopic phenomena through the statistical behaviour of microscopic constituents. The probability of finding a system in microstate i with energy Ei at temperature T is:

$$P_i = \frac{e^{-E_i / k_B T}}{Z}, \quad Z = \sum_j e^{-E_j / k_B T}$$

The Boltzmann distribution — the cornerstone of all equilibrium statistical mechanics. Z is the partition function, normalising the distribution.

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} = D \nabla^2 \rho$$

The diffusion equation: the macroscopic description of random walk (Brownian) motion, where D is the diffusion coefficient.

Theory

Pseudo-Random Number Generation

Linear Congruential Generator

The simplest class of PRNG uses a recurrence relation. Modern engines (Mersenne Twister, PCG64) extend this idea with far longer periods and better statistical properties:

$$X_{n+1} = (a X_n + c) \mod m$$

JavaScript's Math.random() uses a xorshift128+ algorithm with a period of 2¹²⁸ − 1.

Box-Muller Transform

To convert uniform variates U₁, U₂ ∈ (0,1] into a standard normal:

$$Z_0 = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}\,\cos(2\pi U_2), \quad Z_1 = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}\,\sin(2\pi U_2)$$

Central Limit Theorem

The CLT guarantees that the mean of N i.i.d. samples from any distribution converges to a Gaussian as N → ∞:

$$\bar{X}_N \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\, \frac{\sigma^2}{N}\right)$$

χ² Goodness-of-Fit

We can test whether our sampled histogram matches the theoretical distribution using the chi-squared statistic:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}$$

If χ² / dof ≈ 1 the fit is good; much larger values indicate deviation from theory.

Theory

Diffusion & Brownian Motion

Mean-Squared Displacement

For a d-dimensional random walker with step variance σ² per unit time, the MSD grows linearly:

$$\langle r^2(t) \rangle = 2d\,D\,t, \qquad D = \frac{\sigma^2}{2}$$

Linear MSD growth is the hallmark of normal diffusion. Anomalous diffusion scales as tα, α ≠ 1.

Diffusion Equation

$$\frac{\partial P}{\partial t} = D\,\nabla^2 P$$

The probability density P(x,t) satisfies this PDE. Starting from P(x,0)=δ(x), the solution is a spreading Gaussian.

Green's Function Solution

$$P(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D t}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}\right)$$

Width σ(t)=√(2Dt) grows as √t. This Gaussian is drawn as the green envelope on the simulation.

Einstein–Smoluchowski Relation

$$D = \frac{k_B T}{6\pi \eta r}$$

For a sphere of radius r in viscosity η, D is set by temperature — connecting microscopic randomness to macroscopic transport.

📖
1905: Einstein derived this relation the same year as special relativity, providing the first quantitative evidence for atoms.

Theory

Radioactive Decay & Poisson Statistics

Exponential Decay Law

If each nucleus independently decays with probability λ per unit time, the expected number surviving is:

$$N(t) = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t}$$
$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{\lambda}$$

The half-life t½ is the time for half the sample to decay. The mean lifetime τ = 1/λ.

Activity & Poisson Fluctuations

The instantaneous activity (decays per second) is:

$$A(t) = \lambda\,N(t) = A_0\,e^{-\lambda t}$$

The number of decays in a short interval follows a Poisson distribution. The statistical uncertainty in a count of k events is simply √k:

$$\sigma_k = \sqrt{k}$$
☢
Geiger counters exploit this: counting pulses from decays to infer activity and identify isotopes from decay rates.

Theory

Monte Carlo Integration

Basic Estimator

For an integral I = ∫ f(x)dx over domain Ω of volume V, sample N random points xᵢ uniformly in Ω:

$$I \approx \frac{V}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N f(\mathbf{x}_i)$$

The statistical error shrinks as σ/√N regardless of the dimensionality d — MC's killer advantage over grid-based quadrature (which scales as N-k/d).

π via Hit-or-Miss

The unit circle inscribed in a 2×2 square has area π. The ratio of inside points gives:

$$\pi \approx 4\,\frac{N_\text{inside}}{N_\text{total}}$$
$$\sigma_\pi \approx \frac{4}{N}\sqrt{N_\text{in}\!\left(1 - \frac{N_\text{in}}{N}\right)}$$

The Ising Model & Metropolis Algorithm

Hamiltonian

$$\mathcal{H} = -J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} s_i s_j - h\sum_i s_i, \qquad s_i \in \{-1,+1\}$$

J > 0 favours alignment (ferromagnet). The sum ⟨i,j⟩ runs over nearest-neighbour pairs.

Onsager Exact Solution (2D)

$$k_B T_c = \frac{2J}{\ln(1+\sqrt{2})} \approx 2.269\,J$$

Lars Onsager derived this exact result in 1944 — a landmark in statistical mechanics.

Metropolis Algorithm

Pick a random spin sᵢ. Compute the energy change ΔE if it were flipped. Accept the flip with probability:

$$P(\text{accept}) = \min\!\left(1,\, e^{-\Delta E / k_B T}\right)$$

This guarantees the system relaxes to the Boltzmann distribution. One "sweep" = N² attempted flips.

🌡️
Order parameter: M = |Σsᵢ|/N → 0 above Tc (disordered phase) and M > 0 below (spontaneous magnetisation).

Theory

Radiation Transport & Beer–Lambert Law

Beer–Lambert Attenuation

$$I(x) = I_0\,e^{-\mu x}$$

μ (cm⁻¹) is the linear attenuation coefficient. The half-value layer is HVL = ln2/μ.

Mean Free Path

$$\lambda_\text{mfp} = \frac{1}{\mu}$$

Sampling Path Lengths

The path length to the next interaction is sampled from an exponential distribution:

$$\ell = -\frac{\ln U}{\mu}, \qquad U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$$
⚛
GEANT4, MCNP, and other production codes use exactly this method to simulate particle transport in complex geometries for nuclear reactor design and medical physics.

Theory

Kinetic Theory & Maxwell–Boltzmann Statistics

Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution

In 2D, the speed distribution of molecules at temperature T (m=1, k_B=1) is:

$$f(v) = \frac{v}{T}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{v^2}{2T}\right)$$
$$\langle v \rangle = \sqrt{\frac{\pi T}{2}}, \quad v_\text{rms} = \sqrt{2T}$$

Ideal Gas Law

Pressure arises from momentum transfer to the walls. Each wall collision transfers Δp = 2mv⊥. Summing over all collisions:

$$PV = Nk_BT, \quad \text{so } P \propto T \text{ at fixed } N,V$$
💨
Equipartition theorem: each degree of freedom contributes ½kBT to mean KE. In 2D: ⟨KE⟩ = kBT per particle (2 DOF).
