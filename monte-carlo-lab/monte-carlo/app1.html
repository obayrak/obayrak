Foundations

What are Monte Carlo Methods?

Monte Carlo methods are a broad class of computational algorithms that rely on repeated random sampling to obtain numerical results. Named after the famous casino in Monaco, they harness the power of randomness to solve problems that are deterministically intractable.

💡
Key Insight: By sampling from probability distributions and aggregating results, we can estimate integrals, simulate physical systems, and quantify uncertainty — even in high-dimensional spaces where analytical solutions do not exist.
$$\langle f \rangle \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i), \qquad x_i \sim p(x)$$

A Monte Carlo estimator: the expectation of f(x) under distribution p(x) is approximated by the sample mean over N random draws.

🎯
Random Sampling
Drawing samples from probability distributions to estimate quantities that resist direct calculation.
📊
Statistical Convergence
Estimates improve as ∝ 1/√N — doubling precision requires quadrupling the number of samples.
🌡️
Statistical Mechanics
Physical systems in thermal equilibrium are naturally described by Boltzmann-weighted probability distributions.
∫
High-Dim Integration
Classical quadrature fails in high dimensions; MC error is dimension-independent at O(N−½).
Theory

Statistical Mechanics & Randomness

At its core, statistical mechanics describes macroscopic phenomena through the statistical behaviour of microscopic constituents. The probability of finding a system in microstate i with energy Ei at temperature T is:

$$P_i = \frac{e^{-E_i / k_B T}}{Z}, \quad Z = \sum_j e^{-E_j / k_B T}$$

The Boltzmann distribution — the cornerstone of all equilibrium statistical mechanics. Z is the partition function, normalising the distribution.

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} = D \nabla^2 \rho$$

The diffusion equation: the macroscopic description of random walk (Brownian) motion, where D is the diffusion coefficient.

Module 01

🎲 Random Number Generator Lab

All Monte Carlo methods begin here — with high-quality random numbers. Explore how pseudo-random generators work, compare distributions, and verify statistical properties in real time.

📘 Student guide what this shows and what to try

The idea

A computer makes “random” numbers with a fixed formula (a pseudo-random generator). Here you draw thousands of them and watch the histogram take the shape of the distribution you chose.

What to notice

The error of the mean (SEM) shrinks like 1/√N. To make it 10 times smaller you need 100 times more samples. This rule appears in every Monte Carlo method.

Try this

  1. Start with Uniform. As samples pile up, the bars flatten, the mean approaches 0.5 and the variance approaches 1/12 ≈ 0.0833.
  2. Switch to Gaussian and move Std deviation σ. The bell curve gets wider or narrower.
  3. After a few thousand samples press χ² Test. A p-value above 0.05 means the histogram agrees with theory.
Check yourself: With 10 000 samples the SEM is about 0.003. Roughly what is it with 1 000 000 samples?

About 0.0003. That is 100 times more samples, so √100 = 10 times smaller error.

Theory

Pseudo-Random Number Generation

Linear Congruential Generator

The simplest class of PRNG uses a recurrence relation. Modern engines (Mersenne Twister, PCG64) extend this idea with far longer periods and better statistical properties:

$$X_{n+1} = (a X_n + c) \mod m$$

JavaScript's Math.random() uses a xorshift128+ algorithm with a period of 2¹²⁸ − 1.

Box-Muller Transform

To convert uniform variates U₁, U₂ ∈ (0,1] into a standard normal:

$$Z_0 = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}\,\cos(2\pi U_2), \quad Z_1 = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}\,\sin(2\pi U_2)$$

Central Limit Theorem

The CLT guarantees that the mean of N i.i.d. samples from any distribution converges to a Gaussian as N → ∞:

$$\bar{X}_N \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\, \frac{\sigma^2}{N}\right)$$

χ² Goodness-of-Fit

We can test whether our sampled histogram matches the theoretical distribution using the chi-squared statistic:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}$$

If χ² / dof ≈ 1 the fit is good; much larger values indicate deviation from theory.

🚶 Random Walk Simulator

Simulate stochastic motion in 1D, 2D and 3D. Watch diffusion emerge from pure randomness, measure mean-squared displacement, and compare against the exact analytical solution of the diffusion equation.

📘 Student guide what this shows and what to try

The idea

Each particle takes random steps. You cannot predict where one particle goes, but the group spreads in a very regular way. This is diffusion, the same process that spreads ink in water.

What to notice

The mean squared distance grows linearly with time, ⟨r²⟩ = 2dDt, where d is the number of dimensions. So the typical distance grows only like √t, not like t.

Try this

  1. Choose 1D Walk and watch the cloud of particles widen into a bell-shaped curve.
  2. Raise Particles. The mean-squared-displacement (MSD) graph becomes a smoother straight line.
  3. Compare 2D Brownian and 3D Diffusion. The MSD grows faster in more dimensions.
Check yourself: How many more steps does a random walker need, on average, to get twice as far from the start?

Four times as many. Distance grows like √t, and √4 = 2.

Theory

Diffusion & Brownian Motion

Mean-Squared Displacement

For a d-dimensional random walker with step variance σ² per unit time, the MSD grows linearly:

$$\langle r^2(t) \rangle = 2d\,D\,t, \qquad D = \frac{\sigma^2}{2}$$

Linear MSD growth is the hallmark of normal diffusion. Anomalous diffusion scales as tα, α ≠ 1.

Diffusion Equation

$$\frac{\partial P}{\partial t} = D\,\nabla^2 P$$

The probability density P(x,t) satisfies this PDE. Starting from P(x,0)=δ(x), the solution is a spreading Gaussian.

Green's Function Solution

$$P(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi D t}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}\right)$$

Width σ(t)=√(2Dt) grows as √t. This Gaussian is drawn as the green envelope on the simulation.

Einstein–Smoluchowski Relation

$$D = \frac{k_B T}{6\pi \eta r}$$

For a sphere of radius r in viscosity η, D is set by temperature — connecting microscopic randomness to macroscopic transport.

📖
1905: Einstein derived this relation the same year as special relativity, providing the first quantitative evidence for atoms.

☢ Radioactive Decay Simulator

Watch individual nuclei decay stochastically. Each nucleus has a fixed probability of decaying per time step — from this pure randomness, the exponential decay law and Poisson statistics emerge at the ensemble level.

📘 Student guide what this shows and what to try

The idea

Every nucleus has the same small chance of decaying in each time step, independent of the others. Nobody can say which one decays next, yet the total number follows the exponential decay law.

What to notice

Random fluctuations are about √N, so the relative noise √N/N = 1/√N is large for small samples and small for big ones. The activity is A = λN.

Try this

  1. Press Play and watch N(t) follow the dashed theory curve.
  2. Compare the measured half-life with the theory value ln 2 / λ.
  3. Set Initial nuclei N₀ small (about 20) and then large. With few nuclei the curve is jagged; with many it is smooth.
Check yourself: With λ = 0.025 per step, what is the half-life, and what fraction is left after three half-lives?

T½ = ln 2 / 0.025 ≈ 27.7 steps. After three half-lives, (1/2)³ = 1/8 is left.

Theory

Radioactive Decay & Poisson Statistics

Exponential Decay Law

If each nucleus independently decays with probability λ per unit time, the expected number surviving is:

$$N(t) = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t}$$
$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{\lambda}$$

The half-life t½ is the time for half the sample to decay. The mean lifetime τ = 1/λ.

Activity & Poisson Fluctuations

The instantaneous activity (decays per second) is:

$$A(t) = \lambda\,N(t) = A_0\,e^{-\lambda t}$$

The number of decays in a short interval follows a Poisson distribution. The statistical uncertainty in a count of k events is simply √k:

$$\sigma_k = \sqrt{k}$$
☢
Geiger counters exploit this: counting pulses from decays to infer activity and identify isotopes from decay rates.

∫ Monte Carlo Integration Lab

Estimate integrals by random sampling. Watch the estimate converge as points accumulate, and see why MC integration uniquely shines in higher dimensions.

📘 Student guide what this shows and what to try

The idea

To find an area, throw random points into a box around it. The fraction that lands inside, times the area of the box, estimates the area. An integral is just an area.

What to notice

The error falls like 1/√N in any number of dimensions. Grid methods get much worse as dimensions increase, which is why physicists use Monte Carlo for many-particle integrals.

Try this

  1. In π Estimation, watch the estimate approach 3.14159 and the relative error shrink.
  2. Switch to ∫₀π sin(x)dx. The exact answer is 2; see how close the estimate gets.
  3. Try 3D Sphere Vol. The same method works in three dimensions with no extra effort.
Check yourself: With 10 000 points your π estimate is off by about 0.01. About how many points give an error of 0.001?

About 1 000 000. Ten times smaller error needs 10² = 100 times more points.

Theory

Monte Carlo Integration

Basic Estimator

For an integral I = ∫ f(x)dx over domain Ω of volume V, sample N random points xᵢ uniformly in Ω:

$$I \approx \frac{V}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N f(\mathbf{x}_i)$$

The statistical error shrinks as σ/√N regardless of the dimensionality d — MC's killer advantage over grid-based quadrature (which scales as N-k/d).

π via Hit-or-Miss

The unit circle inscribed in a 2×2 square has area π. The ratio of inside points gives:

$$\pi \approx 4\,\frac{N_\text{inside}}{N_\text{total}}$$
$$\sigma_\pi \approx \frac{4}{N}\sqrt{N_\text{in}\!\left(1 - \frac{N_\text{in}}{N}\right)}$$
Module 05

🧲 Ising Model Simulator

Simulate a 2D ferromagnet using the Metropolis algorithm. Witness the spontaneous phase transition from magnetic order to chaos as temperature crosses the critical point Tc ≈ 2.269 J/kB.

📘 Student guide what this shows and what to try

The idea

A grid of tiny magnets (spins, up or down) that like to point the same way as their neighbours. Heat makes them flip at random. The Metropolis rule decides each flip: always accept it if the energy goes down, otherwise accept it with probability e−ΔE/kBT.

What to notice

The exact critical temperature for the 2D Ising model is Tc = 2/ln(1+√2) ≈ 2.269 J/kB (Onsager, 1944). A non-zero field h makes one direction preferred and smooths out the transition.

Try this

  1. At T = 1.5 the magnetisation stays near 1: almost all spins agree (ordered phase).
  2. Raise Temperature to about 3.5. The pattern breaks up and |M| falls near 0 (disordered phase).
  3. Set T close to 2.27. Clusters of every size appear and change slowly; this is the critical point.
  4. Press Run T-sweep to plot |M| against T and see the phase transition.
Check yourself: Why does the simulation take much longer to settle near Tc than at T = 1 or T = 4?

Near Tc the clusters become very large (the correlation length grows), so many single-spin flips are needed to change them. This is called critical slowing down.

Theory

The Ising Model & Metropolis Algorithm

Hamiltonian

$$\mathcal{H} = -J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} s_i s_j - h\sum_i s_i, \qquad s_i \in \{-1,+1\}$$

J > 0 favours alignment (ferromagnet). The sum ⟨i,j⟩ runs over nearest-neighbour pairs.

Onsager Exact Solution (2D)

$$k_B T_c = \frac{2J}{\ln(1+\sqrt{2})} \approx 2.269\,J$$

Lars Onsager derived this exact result in 1944 — a landmark in statistical mechanics.

Metropolis Algorithm

Pick a random spin sᵢ. Compute the energy change ΔE if it were flipped. Accept the flip with probability:

$$P(\text{accept}) = \min\!\left(1,\, e^{-\Delta E / k_B T}\right)$$

This guarantees the system relaxes to the Boltzmann distribution. One "sweep" = N² attempted flips.

🌡️
Order parameter: M = |Σsᵢ|/N → 0 above Tc (disordered phase) and M > 0 below (spontaneous magnetisation).

☀ Particle Transport Simulator

Simulate photon/neutron transport through a shielding material. Particles travel random distances between interactions, where they are either absorbed or scattered — giving rise to Beer–Lambert exponential attenuation.

📘 Student guide what this shows and what to try

The idea

Particles such as photons are followed one at a time through a slab of material. The distance to the next collision is random (exponential, mean 1/μ). At each collision the particle is either absorbed or scattered in a new direction.

What to notice

Particles that never collide follow the Beer–Lambert law I = I₀e−μx. Total transmission is higher than that, because some scattered particles still get through. In radiation shielding this is called build-up.

Try this

  1. Compare the Air and Lead presets and watch how many particles get through.
  2. Increase Slab thickness. The uncollided fraction should follow e−μx.
  3. Lower P(absorb). Particles scatter many times and wander before escaping or being absorbed.
Check yourself: With μ = 0.1 cm⁻¹ and a 20 cm slab, what fraction of particles passes without any collision?

e−0.1×20 = e−2 ≈ 0.135, about 13.5 %.

Theory

Radiation Transport & Beer–Lambert Law

Beer–Lambert Attenuation

$$I(x) = I_0\,e^{-\mu x}$$

μ (cm⁻¹) is the linear attenuation coefficient. The half-value layer is HVL = ln2/μ.

Mean Free Path

$$\lambda_\text{mfp} = \frac{1}{\mu}$$

Sampling Path Lengths

The path length to the next interaction is sampled from an exponential distribution:

$$\ell = -\frac{\ln U}{\mu}, \qquad U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$$
⚛

Theory

Kinetic Theory & Maxwell–Boltzmann Statistics

Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution

In 2D, the speed distribution of molecules at temperature T (m=1, k_B=1) is:

$$f(v) = \frac{v}{T}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{v^2}{2T}\right)$$
$$\langle v \rangle = \sqrt{\frac{\pi T}{2}}, \quad v_\text{rms} = \sqrt{2T}$$

Ideal Gas Law

Pressure arises from momentum transfer to the walls. Each wall collision transfers Δp = 2mv⊥. Summing over all collisions:

$$PV = Nk_BT, \quad \text{so } P \propto T \text{ at fixed } N,V$$
💨
Equipartition theorem: each degree of freedom contributes ½kBT to mean KE. In 2D: ⟨KE⟩ = kBT per particle (2 DOF).
Module 08

ψ Variational Monte Carlo

Guess a trial wavefunction with one parameter α, let Metropolis walkers sample |ψα|², and average the local energy. The variational principle guarantees E(α) ≥ E0, so the lowest energy you find is the best α.

📘 Student guide what this shows and what to try

The idea

In quantum mechanics, the energy you calculate from any guessed wavefunction is never lower than the true ground-state energy. So: guess a shape with one adjustable number α, compute its energy by random sampling, and look for the α that gives the lowest energy.

What to notice

The walkers never need the normalised wavefunction, only ratios |ψ(new)|²/|ψ(old)|². This is why the method works for atoms and molecules with many electrons, where normalising is impossible by hand.

Try this

  1. With the Harmonic oscillator at α = 0.8, compare the purple trial curve with the green exact one. ⟨E⟩ is above 0.5.
  2. Move α toward 0.5. The energy drops to 0.5 and Var(EL) goes to zero.
  3. Press Scan α to draw the whole energy curve, then Jump to minimum.
  4. Switch to Hydrogen atom. The best α = 1 gives E = −0.5 hartree = −13.6 eV, the true ground-state energy.
Check yourself: A student reports E = 0.48 for the harmonic oscillator with some α. Can that be right?

No, unless 0.48 is within the error bar of 0.5. The variational principle says E(α) ≥ E₀ = 0.5 for every trial function.

Theory

The variational principle, sampled

Energy as an average

Writing the energy of a trial state as an average over its own probability density turns a high-dimensional integral into a sampling problem:

$$E(\alpha)=\frac{\langle\psi_\alpha|\hat H|\psi_\alpha\rangle}{\langle\psi_\alpha|\psi_\alpha\rangle}=\int |\psi_\alpha|^2 E_L\,d\mathbf r\Big/\!\int|\psi_\alpha|^2 d\mathbf r,\qquad E_L=\frac{\hat H\psi_\alpha}{\psi_\alpha}$$

Metropolis moves r → r′ are accepted with probability min(1, |ψ(r′)|²/|ψ(r)|²), so the normalisation is never needed. The step size is tuned during burn-in to about 50 % acceptance.

The two systems (ħ = m = 1, atomic units)

Harmonic oscillator, ω = 1, ψ = e−αx²:

$$E_L=\alpha+x^2\!\left(\tfrac12-2\alpha^2\right),\qquad E(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{1}{8\alpha}\ \ge\ \tfrac12$$

Hydrogen atom, ψ = e−αr:

$$E_L=-\frac{\alpha^2}{2}+\frac{\alpha-1}{r},\qquad E(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha^2}{2}-\alpha\ \ge\ -\tfrac12$$

Zero-variance principle: when ψα is an exact eigenstate, EL is constant, so the statistical error vanishes. Error bars use block averaging, because successive Metropolis steps are correlated.
