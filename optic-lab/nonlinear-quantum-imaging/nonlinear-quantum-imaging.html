Nonlinear, Quantum and Imaging Optics — Theory

This chapter goes beyond linear, classical, ideal optics in three directions. Nonlinear optics: light intense enough to change the material it travels through, giving frequency doubling, intensity-dependent refraction and solitons. Quantum optics: light as photons, with statistics, two-photon interference, entanglement and cryptography that no classical wave can explain. Imaging science: how the transfer function, sampling, noise and the atmosphere limit real images, and how deconvolution, confocal and STED microscopy and adaptive optics push past those limits. Practice problems, a history and the limits of the models close the chapter.

1. The Nonlinear Polarisation

In linear optics the induced dipole moment per unit volume is proportional to the field, P = ε₀χE. Electrons are bound by a potential that is harmonic only for small displacements, so for strong fields the response contains higher powers:

P = ε₀ (χ⁽¹⁾E + χ⁽²⁾E² + χ⁽³⁾E³ + …)

The nonlinear terms become important when E approaches the field that binds the electrons, E_at = e/(4πε₀a₀²) ≈ 5 × 10¹¹ V/m. Roughly χ⁽²⁾ ~ 1/E_at ~ 10⁻¹² m/V and χ⁽³⁾ ~ 1/E_at² ~ 10⁻²² m²/V². Sunlight (E ~ 1 kV/m) is far too weak; a focused pulsed laser (10¹³ W/m², E ~ 10⁸ V/m) is not. Nonlinear optics therefore began only in 1961, a year after the laser.

New frequencies. With E = E₀ cos ωt, the quadratic term gives E₀² cos²ωt = ½E₀²(1 + cos 2ωt): a static polarisation (optical rectification) and a dipole oscillating at 2ω, which radiates the second harmonic. Two fields at ω₁ and ω₂ give terms at ω₁ + ω₂ and ω₁ − ω₂ (sum- and difference-frequency generation). The cubic term gives 3ω and an intensity-dependent contribution at ω itself: the Kerr effect.

Symmetry. In a material with a centre of inversion, reversing E must reverse P, which forces χ⁽²⁾ = 0. Second-order effects therefore need non-centrosymmetric crystals (quartz, KDP, BBO, LiNbO₃), whereas χ⁽³⁾ effects occur in every material, including glass, air and water.

2. Second-Harmonic Generation

The fundamental wave (wave number k₁ = 2πn₁/λ) drives a polarisation at 2ω whose phase varies as e^(2ik₁z). The harmonic it radiates travels with its own wave number k₂ = 2π n₂/(λ/2). Contributions generated at different depths add with a relative phase Δk z, where

Δk = k₂ − 2k₁ = (4π/λ)(n(2ω) − n(ω))
Coupled-wave solution (undepleted pump). The slowly varying amplitude of the harmonic obeys dA₂/dz = iκ A₁² e^(iΔkz), with κ ∝ d_eff. Integrating over the crystal length L:
A₂(L) = iκA₁² (e^(iΔkL) − 1)/(iΔk), so |A₂|² = κ²|A₁|⁴ L² sinc²(ΔkL/2).
In SI units, for plane waves:
η = I₂/I₁ = 8π² d_eff² L² I₁ / (ε₀ c n₁² n₂ λ²) × sinc²(ΔkL/2)

With Δk = 0 the harmonic grows as L², and the efficiency is proportional to the pump intensity, which is why pulsed lasers (high peak intensity) convert far better than continuous ones. When the conversion becomes large the fundamental is depleted and the exact solution is η = tanh²(√η₀), approaching 100%.

If Δk ≠ 0 the harmonic grows only over the coherence length L_c = π/|Δk|, then flows back into the fundamental, and the power oscillates with period 2L_c. Normal dispersion makes n(2ω) > n(ω): in lithium niobate at 1064 nm, Δn = 0.078 and L_c = λ/(4Δn) = 3.4 µm. Without a trick, only a few micrometres of crystal would be useful.

3. Phase Matching

Birefringent phase matching uses the two polarisations of an anisotropic crystal. In a negative uniaxial crystal (n_e < n_o) the extraordinary index at 2ω, which falls from n_o(2ω) to n_e(2ω) as the angle θ to the optic axis increases, can be made equal to the ordinary index at ω. For type I (ordinary fundamental, extraordinary harmonic):

n_e(2ω, θ_pm) = n_o(ω) ⇒ sin²θ_pm = [n_o(ω)⁻² − n_o(2ω)⁻²] / [n_e(2ω)⁻² − n_o(2ω)⁻²]

In the simulator's index diagram this is the crossing of the red circle n_o(ω) with the green ellipse n_e(2ω, θ). In type II the fundamental is split between both polarisations. Two side effects limit birefringent phase matching: the extraordinary beam walks off at an angle ρ (3.2° for the BBO harmonic at 532 nm), so tightly focused beams separate; and the angular acceptance is small, about 1 mrad·cm for BBO at 1064 nm, so the crystal must be aligned precisely and the beam well collimated. Some crystals (LBO, KTP) can be phase matched at θ = 90° by heating them (non-critical or temperature phase matching), which removes walk-off.

Quasi-phase matching (proposed by Armstrong, Bloembergen and co-workers in 1962) flips the sign of the nonlinear coefficient every coherence length. Just as the harmonic would start flowing back, the reversed domain makes it grow again. The poling period is

Λ = 2L_c = λ / (2[n(2ω) − n(ω)]), d_eff = (2/π) d₃₃

Ferroelectric crystals such as lithium niobate can be periodically poled with patterned electrodes and a high voltage (PPLN, PPKTP). QPM can use the largest coefficient d₃₃ (≈ 25 pm/V in LiNbO₃), has no walk-off and works at any wavelength the period is designed for, which is why it dominates in telecom, quantum optics and compact green lasers.

Worked example: why does a green laser pointer work?
A diode laser at 808 nm pumps Nd:YVO₄, which lases at 1064 nm. Inside the laser cavity the circulating power is high, and a phase-matched KTP crystal doubles it to 532 nm. Because the eye is about 30 times more sensitive at 532 nm than at 808 nm, a few milliwatts of green look very bright. (The leftover infrared is why cheap pointers without a filter can be hazardous.)

All of these require phase matching, which in each case is momentum conservation among the photons. SPDC is the time reverse of sum-frequency generation: a pump photon splits into a signal and an idler photon born at the same instant, with correlated energies, momenta and (with a suitable crystal arrangement) entangled polarisations.

5. The Optical Kerr Effect

The χ⁽³⁾ term at the original frequency makes the refractive index depend on intensity:

n = n₀ + n₂ I, n₂ = 3χ⁽³⁾/(4 n₀² ε₀ c)
Materialn₂ (m²/W)
Air (800 nm)≈ 3 × 10⁻²³
Fused silica2.6 × 10⁻²⁰
Sapphire3 × 10⁻²⁰
Carbon disulfide3 × 10⁻¹⁸
Chalcogenide glass≈ 10⁻¹⁷

The changes are tiny (Δn = 2.6 × 10⁻⁸ in silica at 1 GW/cm²) but they act over long distances or very high intensities. Self-focusing: a beam whose centre is brighter than its edges creates its own positive lens. Above the critical power

P_cr ≈ 3.77 λ² / (8π n₀ n₂)

(about 4 MW in silica at 1 µm and a few GW in air) self-focusing overcomes diffraction and the beam collapses until other effects stop it, damaging glass or forming plasma filaments in air that can stretch for kilometres. The same lensing in a titanium–sapphire crystal favours pulsed operation over continuous operation: Kerr-lens mode locking is how most femtosecond lasers produce pulses.

6. Self-Phase Modulation

In a fibre of effective core area A_eff, the Kerr phase of a pulse of power P(t) after length L is

φ_NL(t) = γ P(t) L, γ = 2π n₂ / (λ A_eff)

(γ ≈ 1.3 W⁻¹ km⁻¹ for standard single-mode fibre at 1550 nm). The phase follows the pulse shape, so the instantaneous frequency shifts by δω(t) = −dφ_NL/dt: the leading edge is shifted to lower frequencies and the trailing edge to higher ones. The pulse shape is unchanged but the spectrum broadens, and because each new frequency appears at two moments in the pulse, these interfere and the spectrum develops a series of peaks. Their number is approximately

M ≈ φ_max/π + ½

Spectral broadening by self-phase modulation is the first step of pulse compression and of supercontinuum generation, where photonic-crystal fibres turn femtosecond pulses into white light spanning more than an octave, used in optical frequency combs (Nobel Prize 2005) and in confocal and OCT light sources.

7. Dispersion, the NLS Equation and Solitons

In a fibre, group-velocity dispersion is described by β₂ = d²k/dω². In normal dispersion (β₂ > 0, below 1.3 µm in silica) red travels faster than blue; in anomalous dispersion (β₂ < 0, at 1550 nm, β₂ ≈ −21.7 ps²/km) blue is faster. Two length scales compare the effects for a pulse of width T₀ and peak power P₀:

L_D = T₀² / |β₂| (dispersion length), L_NL = 1/(γP₀) (nonlinear length)

Using ξ = z/L_D, τ = t/T₀ and u = A/√P₀, the pulse envelope obeys the nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation, which the simulator solves:

i ∂u/∂ξ − (sgn β₂/2) ∂²u/∂τ² + N² |u|² u = 0, N² = L_D/L_NL = γP₀T₀²/|β₂|
The fundamental soliton. In anomalous dispersion try u = sech(τ) e^(iξ/2). Since d²(sech τ)/dτ² = sech τ − 2 sech³τ, the dispersion term gives ½(sech − 2sech³) and the Kerr term N² sech³. They cancel exactly when N = 1: the self-phase-modulation chirp is just what is needed to undo the dispersive spreading. The pulse then travels without any change of shape or spectrum. Its peak power is
P₁ = |β₂| / (γT₀²), for example about 17 W for T₀ = 1 ps (FWHM 1.76 ps) in standard fibre.

Integer N > 1 gives higher-order solitons, which narrow, split and re-form periodically with the soliton period z₀ = (π/2)L_D. A pulse that is not exactly a soliton (for example a Gaussian with N near 1) sheds a little dispersive radiation and settles into one. In normal dispersion there are no bright solitons; dispersion and self-phase modulation together produce nearly rectangular, linearly chirped pulses with flat, broad spectra, ideal for compression.

Solitons were predicted in fibres by Hasegawa and Tappert (1973) and observed by Mollenauer, Stolen and Gordon (1980). They underpin soliton mode-locked fibre lasers, pulse compressors and the dissipative Kerr-soliton frequency combs in microresonators. The split-step Fourier method used here alternates small steps of dispersion (applied in the frequency domain as a phase exp(−iβ₂ω²h/2)) and nonlinearity (applied in the time domain as exp(iN²|u|²h)).

Worked example: a 2 ps (FWHM) soliton at 1550 nm in standard fibre. Peak power, dispersion length, soliton period?
T₀ = 2/1.763 = 1.13 ps. L_D = T₀²/|β₂| = 1.29/21.7 km = 59 m. P₁ = |β₂|/(γT₀²) = 21.7/(1.3 × 1.29) W = 13 W. z₀ = (π/2)L_D = 93 m.

8. Photon Statistics

A photodetector converts light into discrete clicks. For light of steady intensity, counts in a fixed interval follow a distribution whose shape depends on the source:

The second-order correlation function compares the probability of detecting photons at times t and t + τ with the product of single probabilities:

g⁽²⁾(τ) = ⟨I(t) I(t+τ)⟩ / ⟨I⟩²

For any classical field ⟨I²⟩ ≥ ⟨I⟩² (a variance cannot be negative), so g⁽²⁾(0) ≥ 1. Thermal light, whose intensity fluctuates randomly, gives g⁽²⁾(0) = 2 falling to 1 over the coherence time: photons arrive bunched. Hanbury Brown and Twiss discovered this in 1956 and used it to measure star diameters. A single atom, ion, molecule, quantum dot or colour centre cannot emit a second photon until it has been re-excited, so g⁽²⁾(0) = 0: photons are antibunched (Kimble, Dagenais and Mandel, 1977). Antibunching has no classical explanation and is the standard test of a single-photon source.

An attenuated laser is not a single-photon source: at n̄ = 0.1, 90% of pulses are empty and about 5% of the non-empty ones contain two or more photons, which an eavesdropper could split off (section 12).

9. Single Photons and Complementarity

Send single photons into a Mach–Zehnder interferometer. Each is detected at one output only, but after many photons the counts at the two outputs follow P_A = cos²(φ/2), P_B = sin²(φ/2). Quantum mechanics assigns an amplitude to each path and adds the amplitudes before squaring:

P_A = |½ + ½e^(iφ)|² = cos²(φ/2)

Each photon interferes with itself. If anything records which path the photon took (here, rotating the polarisation in one arm to make the paths distinguishable), the amplitudes no longer interfere and P_A = P_B = ½ at every phase. This is complementarity: path information and interference visibility obey V² + D² ≤ 1, where D is the distinguishability of the paths. Grangier, Roger and Aspect (1986) showed both halves with the same single-photon source: no coincidences behind a beam splitter (the photon is not split), yet full interference in an interferometer.

10. Hong–Ou–Mandel Interference

Two photons enter a 50 : 50 beam splitter from opposite sides. To get one photon at each output, either both are transmitted (amplitude t·t) or both are reflected (amplitude r·r). For a lossless beam splitter t = 1/√2 and r = i/√2, so

amplitude(one in each output) = t² + r² = ½ − ½ = 0

If the photons are identical in every respect (frequency, polarisation, arrival time), they always leave together: coincidences vanish. Delaying one photon by more than its coherence time makes them distinguishable and the coincidence probability returns to ½. Scanning the delay traces the HOM dip (Hong, Ou and Mandel, 1987):

P_c(τ) = ½ [1 − V exp(−(πΔν τ)²/(4 ln 2))], V = |⟨ψ₁|ψ₂⟩|²

for photons with Gaussian spectra of width Δν. The width of the dip (about 0.88/Δν, a few hundred femtoseconds for nanometre bandwidths) measures the photons' coherence time, and its depth V measures how indistinguishable they are; partly rotated polarisations give a shallower dip. The effect has no classical analogue with visibility above 50%. It is the basic operation of linear-optics quantum computing, entanglement swapping and boson sampling.

Worked example: photons at 810 nm with 5 nm bandwidth. How wide is the dip?
Δν = cΔλ/λ² = 3 × 10⁸ × 5 × 10⁻⁹/(810 × 10⁻⁹)² = 2.29 × 10¹² Hz. FWHM = 4 ln 2/(πΔν) = 386 fs, i.e. 116 µm of path: the delay stage must be set to a few micrometres.

11. Entanglement and Bell's Theorem

SPDC can produce photon pairs in the polarisation state

|Φ⁺⟩ = (|H⟩_A|H⟩_B + |V⟩_A|V⟩_B)/√2

which cannot be written as a product of separate states for A and B: the pair is entangled. Each photon alone is completely unpolarised (50% passes any polariser), yet when Alice and Bob measure with polarisers at angles a and b the results agree with probability cos²(a − b), and the correlation (+1 for agreement, −1 for disagreement) is

E(a, b) = cos 2(a − b)
Bell's inequality (CHSH form). Suppose each pair carries hidden instructions λ fixing in advance the result A(a, λ) = ±1 for any setting Alice might choose, and likewise B(b, λ) = ±1 (locality: Bob's result cannot depend on Alice's setting). For each λ,
A(a)[B(b) − B(b′)] + A(a′)[B(b) + B(b′)] = ±2,
because one bracket is 0 and the other ±2. Averaging over λ gives
|S| = |E(a, b) − E(a, b′) + E(a′, b) + E(a′, b′)| ≤ 2.
Quantum mechanics with a = 0°, a′ = 45°, b = 22.5°, b′ = 67.5° gives S = 4 cos 45° = 2√2 ≈ 2.83, the largest value any quantum state allows (Tsirelson's bound).

A source with visibility V (entanglement mixed with noise) gives S = 2√2 V, so violation needs V > 1/√2 ≈ 0.71. The best local model (each photon carries a hidden polarisation) gives a straight-line correlation that touches the quantum curve at 0°, 45° and 90° but falls short in between, and exactly S = 2.

Experiments by Freedman and Clauser (1972) and Aspect, Grangier and Roger (1982, with settings switched during the photons' flight) violated the inequality; in 2015 three groups closed the remaining loopholes simultaneously. Nature is not locally realistic. Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger received the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics. The correlations cannot carry messages (each result alone is random), so there is no conflict with relativity, but they are a resource for cryptography, teleportation and quantum computing.

12. Quantum Key Distribution

BB84 (Bennett and Brassard, 1984) lets two parties create a shared secret key whose security rests on quantum mechanics rather than on unproven mathematical difficulty.

StepWhat happens
1. SendAlice encodes random bits on single photons in a random basis: + (H = 0, V = 1) or × (+45° = 0, −45° = 1)
2. MeasureBob measures each photon in a random basis. Matching basis: correct bit. Wrong basis: random result.
3. SiftOver a public channel they compare bases (not bits) and keep only matching cases: about half
4. Estimate errorsThey reveal a random sample to estimate the quantum bit error rate (QBER)
5. Error correctionCorrect the remaining errors (leaking some information, ≈ h(Q) per bit)
6. Privacy amplificationHash the key down to remove whatever Eve could know (another ≈ h(Q) per bit)

Why eavesdropping is detected. Eve cannot copy an unknown quantum state (the no-cloning theorem). If she measures and resends, she picks the wrong basis half the time, and then Bob's result is wrong half the time: each intercepted photon causes an error with probability ¼. Intercepting everything raises the QBER to 25%. The fraction of the sifted key that can be made secret against general attacks is

r = 1 − 2h(Q), h(Q) = −Q log₂Q − (1 − Q) log₂(1 − Q)

which falls to zero at Q ≈ 11% (Shor and Preskill, 2000). Above that, Alice and Bob abort. Practical systems use attenuated lasers with decoy states (randomly varied intensities) to detect photon-number-splitting attacks. QKD runs over metropolitan fibre networks today; the Chinese satellite Micius distributed keys and entangled photons over more than 1000 km in 2017. Its security is only as good as the devices: real attacks have exploited detector imperfections, which has led to measurement-device-independent protocols.

Worked example: Eve intercepts 40% of photons on an otherwise perfect channel. Is a key still possible?
QBER = 0.40 × ¼ = 10%. h(0.10) = 0.469, so r = 1 − 0.938 = 6%: a short secret key can still be distilled, but only just. At 44% interception the key rate reaches zero.

13. Image Formation: PSF and MTF

For incoherent light, every point of the object produces a copy of the point-spread function (PSF) in the image, weighted by its brightness. The image is therefore a convolution, and in the frequency domain a product:

i(x, y) = o(x, y) ⊗ PSF(x, y) ⇔ I(ν) = O(ν) · OTF(ν)

The optical transfer function (OTF) is the Fourier transform of the PSF; its magnitude, the modulation transfer function (MTF), gives the fraction of contrast transferred at each spatial frequency ν (in line pairs per mm). For a diffraction-limited lens with a circular aperture at f-number N:

MTF(ν) = (2/π)[arccos x − x√(1 − x²)], x = ν/ν_c, ν_c = 1/(λN)

No detail finer than the cut-off ν_c can be imaged at all (455 lp/mm at f/4 and 550 nm). Aberrations lower the MTF below this curve. Defocus is a wavefront error W₂₀ρ² across the pupil, with W₂₀ = δz/(8N²) for a focus error δz; beyond about a quarter wave the MTF drops and can even turn negative, so that fine bars appear with reversed contrast (spurious resolution, visible in the simulator's Siemens star). The depth of focus for W₂₀ ≤ λ/4 is ±2λN².

The MTF of a system is the product of the MTFs of its parts (lens, anti-aliasing filter, pixel aperture, image processing), which is why camera and lens tests quote MTF50, the frequency at which contrast falls to 50%.

14. Sampling and Aliasing

A sensor with pixel pitch p samples the image. By the Nyquist–Shannon theorem, frequencies up to

ν_N = 1/(2p)

are recorded faithfully. Higher frequencies are not removed; they reappear as false lower frequencies ν_alias = |ν − k/p|. This aliasing makes moiré patterns on fabrics, false colours at edges with Bayer colour filters and the jagged ring in the centre of a Siemens star. Once recorded, aliased detail cannot be told apart from real detail.

Two filters act before sampling. The pixel aperture itself averages light over the pixel, with MTF |sinc(πpν)|, which is only 64% at Nyquist. An optical low-pass filter (OLPF) made of birefringent plates splits each point into four, one pixel apart, with MTF |cos(πpν)| that is zero at 1/(2p). The lens is also a low-pass filter; the ratio

Q = λN/p

compares them. With Q ≥ 2 the lens cut-off lies below Nyquist and the system is fully sampled with no aliasing (astronomical cameras, high-resolution microscopy). Most consumer cameras have Q ≈ 0.5–1: sharper images at the cost of some aliasing, which is why many recent high-resolution cameras omit the OLPF and rely on lens softness and processing.

15. Noise in Images

Light arrives as photons, so a pixel expecting N photo-electrons actually records a Poisson-distributed number with standard deviation √N: shot noise. To it the electronics add read noise σ_r (1–3 e⁻ in modern CMOS sensors) and thermal dark current. The signal-to-noise ratio is

SNR = N / √(N + σ_r² + N_dark)

In bright light SNR ≈ √N: to double the SNR you need four times the light. In dim light read noise dominates. A full-well capacity of 30 000 e⁻ limits the best single-pixel SNR to about 170. Noise sets the ultimate limit to resolution, because fine detail has low contrast after the MTF and drowns in noise first. Astronomers and microscopists therefore speak of resolution and sensitivity together.

16. Deconvolution

Since blur multiplies the spectrum by the OTF H(ν), the obvious remedy is to divide by it: the inverse filter F̂ = G/H. But the recorded spectrum is G = HF + N with noise N, so F̂ = F + N/H. Where |H| is small (high frequencies, or zeros of the OTF for defocus and motion blur) the noise is multiplied enormously and the result is useless, as the simulator shows.

The Wiener filter. Choose a linear filter W(ν) that minimises the mean squared error ⟨|WG − F|²⟩ given the signal power S_f and noise power S_n at each frequency. Setting the derivative with respect to W* to zero gives
W = H* / (|H|² + S_n/S_f).
Where the signal dominates, W ≈ 1/H (full restoration). Where noise dominates, W → 0 (the frequency is suppressed rather than amplified). In practice S_n/S_f is replaced by a constant K adjusted by eye or from the noise level.

Richardson–Lucy (1972, 1974) is an iterative method that maximises the likelihood of the image under Poisson noise:

f_{k+1} = f_k · [ PSF* ⊗ ( g / (PSF ⊗ f_k) ) ]

It keeps the image positive and conserves total flux. Early iterations recover large-scale structure; later ones sharpen detail but amplify noise, so the number of iterations acts as the regularisation. It became famous restoring images from the Hubble Space Telescope, whose main mirror had been ground with spherical aberration (1990), until corrective optics were installed in 1993. Today deconvolution is standard in fluorescence microscopy, astronomy, and, combined with machine learning, in phone cameras. Blind deconvolution estimates the PSF and the image together.

17. Resolution in Microscopy

A microscope objective collects light within a cone of half-angle α in a medium of index n; its numerical aperture NA = n sin α (up to 0.95 in air, about 1.45 with oil immersion) sets the resolution:

CriterionLateral resolutionλ = 600 nm, NA = 1.4
Abbe: finest grating period passedd = λ/(2NA)214 nm
Rayleigh: peak on first zero0.61 λ/NA261 nm
PSF FWHM (widefield)≈ 0.51 λ/NA219 nm
Confocal, pinhole ≪ 1 AU≈ 0.37 λ/NA159 nm
Axial (widefield depth of field)≈ 2nλ/NA²≈ 930 nm

Confocal microscopy (Minsky, 1957) scans a focused laser spot and places a pinhole in front of the detector, conjugate to the spot. The effective PSF is the product of the excitation and detection PSFs, which is narrower by up to √2. More important is optical sectioning: light from above or below the focal plane is spread out at the pinhole and rejected, so thick samples can be imaged slice by slice in three dimensions. The pinhole is measured in Airy units (1 AU = 1.22λ/NA in the sample); 1 AU keeps most of the signal with good sectioning, and smaller pinholes trade light for resolution. Two-photon microscopy excites fluorescence only in the focus (the signal scales as I²), giving sectioning without a pinhole and deeper penetration in tissue.

18. Breaking the Diffraction Limit

The diffraction limit applies to focusing light, not to locating or switching molecules. Fluorescence microscopy exploits this in two ways (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2014: Betzig, Hell and Moerner).

STED (stimulated emission depletion; Hell and Wichmann, 1994). After the excitation pulse, a red-shifted, doughnut-shaped pulse with a dark centre forces excited molecules back to the ground state by stimulated emission before they can fluoresce. The probability of surviving falls as exp(−I/I_sat). Near the centre the doughnut intensity rises as r², so only a small spot can still fluoresce. Its width shrinks with the depletion intensity:

d_STED ≈ λ / (2NA √(1 + I_max/I_sat))

With I/I_sat = 30–100, resolutions of 20–50 nm are routine; the limit is photobleaching at the high depletion intensities (I_sat ~ MW/cm², peak intensities ~ GW/cm²). RESOLFT does the same with photoswitchable proteins at much lower intensity.

Single-molecule localisation (PALM, STORM, 2006). If only a sparse random subset of molecules glows at any moment, each produces an isolated PSF whose centre can be found far more precisely than its width:

σ_loc ≈ σ_PSF / √N_photons

(≈ 100 nm/√1000 ≈ 3–20 nm in practice). Thousands of camera frames, each with different molecules switched on, build up the super-resolved image. MINFLUX (2017) combines a doughnut with localisation and reaches 1–3 nm. Structured illumination (SIM) doubles the resolution by shifting high frequencies into the passband with striped illumination, and expansion microscopy physically swells the sample 4–20 times in a hydrogel.

Worked example: what depletion ratio gives 30 nm resolution at 600 nm with NA 1.4?
√(1 + I/I_sat) = λ/(2NA × 30 nm) = 600/84 = 7.14, so I/I_sat = 50.

19. Atmospheric Turbulence and Seeing

Wind mixes air of slightly different temperatures, producing refractive-index fluctuations that follow Kolmogorov's turbulence spectrum (1941), with strength C_n²(h) at height h. A plane wavefront from a star accumulates random phase. Its statistics are described by a single length, the Fried parameter r₀ (1966):

r₀ = [0.423 k² sec ζ ∫ C_n²(h) dh]^(−3/5) ∝ λ^(6/5)

r₀ is the diameter over which the rms phase error is about 1 radian, typically 10–20 cm at 500 nm at good sites. The phase power spectrum is Φ(f) = 0.023 r₀^(−5/3) f^(−11/3), and the mean-square phase over an aperture of diameter D is (Noll, 1976)

σ² = 1.03 (D/r₀)^(5/3) rad², of which tip-tilt removed leaves 0.134 (D/r₀)^(5/3)

A telescope larger than r₀ is limited not by its own diffraction (λ/D) but by seeing:

FWHM_seeing ≈ 0.98 λ/r₀

about 1″ at 500 nm for r₀ = 10 cm, the same for a 10 cm and a 10 m telescope. In a short exposure the image is a cloud of about (D/r₀)² speckles, each of diffraction-limited size λ/D; in a long exposure they average into the seeing disc. Because r₀ ∝ λ^(6/5) while the seeing FWHM ∝ λ^(−1/5), seeing improves slowly towards the infrared, and D/r₀ falls quickly: correction is much easier at 2 µm than at 0.5 µm. Two more scales matter: the coherence time τ₀ ≈ 0.314 r₀/v (milliseconds for wind speed v) and the isoplanatic angle θ₀ ≈ 0.314 r₀/h̄ (a few arcseconds), the field over which the distortion is the same.

20. Adaptive Optics

Proposed by Babcock in 1953, adaptive optics measures the wavefront and cancels it in real time.

ComponentFunction
Wavefront sensor (Shack–Hartmann)A lenslet array divides the pupil; each lenslet's spot moves in proportion to the local wavefront slope
Deformable mirrorHundreds to thousands of actuators reshape a thin face sheet by micrometres
Real-time computerConverts slopes to actuator commands at 0.5–3 kHz
Guide starA bright star near the target, or a laser guide star: sodium atoms at ≈ 90 km excited at 589 nm

The residual phase variance is a sum of error terms; the Strehl ratio (peak brightness relative to a perfect telescope) then follows from the Maréchal approximation S ≈ exp(−σ²):

σ² = σ²_fit + σ²_time + σ²_aniso + σ²_noise …, σ²_fit ≈ 0.28 (d/r₀)^(5/3), σ²_time ≈ (τ/τ₀)^(5/3), σ²_aniso ≈ (θ/θ₀)^(5/3)

where d = D/N is the actuator spacing, τ the loop delay and θ the angle from the guide star. A good correction needs about (D/r₀)² actuators: a few hundred for an 8 m telescope in the near-infrared, which is why AO works best there, and tens of thousands for visible light on the 39 m Extremely Large Telescope. AO made ground-based telescopes sharper than Hubble in the infrared; it allowed Genzel and Ghez to follow stars orbiting the black hole at the centre of the Galaxy (Nobel Prize 2020). The same techniques now correct the eye's aberrations to image individual retinal cells, and the aberrations of tissue in deep microscopy.

Worked example: an 8 m telescope with r₀ = 10 cm at 500 nm, observing at 2.2 µm. Seeing, diffraction limit and actuators needed?
r₀(2.2 µm) = 10 cm × (4.4)^1.2 = 59 cm. Seeing = 0.98 × 2.2 µm/0.59 m = 3.6 µrad = 0.75″. Diffraction limit 1.03λ/D = 0.058″, 13 times sharper. (D/r₀)² = (8/0.59)² ≈ 180 actuators across the pupil area.

22. Practice Problems

1. Using n_o(1064) = 1.6545, n_o(532) = 1.6742 and n_e(532) = 1.5547 for BBO, find the type-I phase-matching angle.
sin²θ = (1.6545⁻² − 1.6742⁻²)/(1.5547⁻² − 1.6742⁻²) = (0.36531 − 0.35677)/(0.41372 − 0.35677) = 0.1500 → θ = 22.8°.
2. Lithium niobate has n(532) − n(1064) = 0.078 for extraordinary light. What is the coherence length, and what poling period is needed?
L_c = λ/(4Δn) = 1.064/(0.312) µm = 3.4 µm; Λ = 2L_c = 6.8 µm.
3. In the undepleted regime, how does the SH power change if the crystal length is doubled? If the pump power is doubled?
Doubling L multiplies the efficiency by 4 (∝ L²). Doubling the pump power doubles the efficiency (∝ I), so the harmonic power rises 4 times (∝ I²).
4. What is the critical power for self-focusing in fused silica (n₂ = 2.6 × 10⁻²⁰ m²/W) at 1064 nm?
P_cr = 3.77 × (1.064 × 10⁻⁶)²/(8π × 1.45 × 2.6 × 10⁻²⁰) = 4.3 MW.
5. A pulse acquires a peak nonlinear phase of 4.5π by SPM. How many spectral peaks appear?
M ≈ φ_max/π + ½ = 4.5 + 0.5 = 5.
6. A 1 ps (T₀) pulse at 1550 nm in standard fibre (β₂ = −21.7 ps²/km, γ = 1.3 W⁻¹km⁻¹). What peak power makes a fundamental soliton, and what is its soliton period?
P₁ = 21.7/(1.3 × 1) W = 16.7 W. L_D = 1/21.7 km = 46 m, z₀ = (π/2) × 46 m = 72 m.
7. Thermal light with n̄ = 3 photons per interval. Find P(0) and the variance; compare with a laser.
Thermal: P(0) = 1/(1 + 3) = 0.25, Var = 3 + 9 = 12. Laser: P(0) = e⁻³ = 0.050, Var = 3. The thermal source is much noisier.
8. In a Bell test with visibility V = 0.8, what is the CHSH value? Is it a violation?
S = 2√2 × 0.8 = 2.26 > 2: yes, provided the statistical error is small enough (with 10⁴ pairs per setting σ_S ≈ 0.02).
9. A BB84 link shows a QBER of 3%. What fraction of the sifted key becomes secret key?
h(0.03) = 0.194, r = 1 − 0.389 = 61%.
10. A camera has 4 µm pixels and an f/8 lens at 550 nm. Find ν_c, ν_N and Q. Will it alias?
ν_c = 1/(0.55 µm × 8) = 227 lp/mm; ν_N = 125 lp/mm; Q = 0.55 × 8/4 = 1.1. The lens still passes ≈ 35% contrast at Nyquist, so fine periodic detail will alias somewhat.
11. A pixel collects 400 photo-electrons with 5 e⁻ read noise. SNR? How much more light doubles it?
SNR = 400/√(400 + 25) = 19.4. Doubling needs about 4× the light (SNR = 1600/√1625 = 39.7).
12. A single molecule emits 2500 detected photons with a PSF of σ = 110 nm. How precisely can it be localised?
σ_loc ≈ 110/√2500 = 2.2 nm (in practice background and pixelation make it 2–3 times worse).
13. At a site with r₀ = 15 cm at 500 nm, what is the seeing at 500 nm and at 1.65 µm?
500 nm: 0.98 × 0.5 µm/0.15 m = 3.27 µrad = 0.67″. At 1.65 µm, r₀ = 15 × 3.3^1.2 = 63 cm, seeing = 0.98 × 1.65/0.63 µrad = 2.57 µrad = 0.53″.
14. An AO system with 16 × 16 actuators on a 4 m telescope, r₀ = 50 cm in the H band. Estimate the fitting error and Strehl.
d = 4/16 = 0.25 m; σ² = 0.28 × (0.25/0.5)^(5/3) = 0.088 rad²; S ≈ e^(−0.088) = 92% (the simulator's preset gives the same). Real systems also have temporal and noise errors, typically giving 50–80%.

24. Limits of the Models in This Module

Frequency doubling: plane waves, no absorption, no walk-off or focusing in the growth curve, approximate d_eff values, and the tanh² depletion law (exact only at Δk = 0); Sellmeier equations at room temperature. Fibre: the basic NLS equation without higher-order dispersion, Raman scattering or self-steepening (which shift and break up high-order solitons in real fibres); split-step on a 1024-point grid. Photons: idealised sources (a single-mode thermal field, a two-step emitter), ideal 50 : 50 beam splitters and detectors with no dead time or dark counts. Bell test: perfect detectors and fair sampling; the local model shown is one simple example. BB84: ideal single photons, intercept-resend attack only, asymptotic key rate. Imaging: scalar diffraction, a single wavelength, space-invariant blur, periodic boundaries in the FFT. Noise: fixed random seed for repeatability. Microscopy: two-dimensional scalar PSFs; the STED doughnut is a model profile; no noise or bleaching. Adaptive optics: a single Kolmogorov screen with sub-harmonics, ideal Fourier-domain correction (no servo lag, noise or aliasing in the wavefront sensor), and spot displacements drawn schematically.
