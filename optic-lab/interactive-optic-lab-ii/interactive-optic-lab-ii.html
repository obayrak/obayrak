Physics of Optical Systems

Optics is the branch of physics that studies the behaviour and properties of light and its interactions with matter. Geometric optics, which underlies all simulations here, treats light as rays that travel in straight lines and obey the laws of reflection and refraction.

1. Fundamental Laws

Law of Reflection

When a light ray strikes a smooth surface, the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal to the surface at the point of incidence.

θ_i = θ_r

Law of Refraction (Snell's Law)

When light passes from one medium to another, it bends according to Snell's Law:

n₁ · sin(θ₁) = n₂ · sin(θ₂)

For thin lenses in air (n₁ = n₂ = 1), the Lensmaker's Equation governs the focal length.

2. Sign Convention (New Cartesian)

All distances are measured from the optical centre (pole for mirrors, optical centre for lenses). Distances in the direction of incident light (left → right) are positive. Distances against incident light are negative. Heights above the principal axis are positive; below are negative.

3. Concave Mirror

A concave (converging) mirror has its reflecting surface on the inner side of a spherical shell. The centre of curvature C lies in front of the mirror, and the focal length f = R/2 is negative in the Cartesian convention.

Mirror Formula: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u   |   Magnification: m = −v/u

4. Convex Mirror

A convex (diverging) mirror has its reflecting surface on the outer side of a spherical shell. The focal point F and centre of curvature C are behind the mirror (virtual), making f positive in the Cartesian convention.

Mirror Formula: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u   |   f > 0 always
Object PositionImage PositionNatureSize
Any position in frontBetween P and F (behind mirror)Virtual, ErectDiminished

Applications: Rear-view mirrors in vehicles, security mirrors in shops and road intersections.

5. Convex Lens (Converging)

A convex (biconvex) lens is thicker at the centre than at the edges. It converges parallel rays to a real focal point F on the far side. The focal length f is positive.

Lens Formula: 1/f = 1/v − 1/u   |   Magnification: m = v/u

6. Concave Lens (Diverging)

A concave (biconcave) lens is thinner at the centre than at the edges. It diverges parallel rays as if they originated from a virtual focal point F on the same side as the incoming light. The focal length f is negative.

Lens Formula: 1/f = 1/v − 1/u   |   f < 0 always
Object PositionImage PositionNatureSize
Any positionSame side as object, between F and lensVirtual, ErectDiminished

Applications: Correcting myopia (short-sightedness), wide-angle camera lenses, Galilean telescope eyepiece.

7. Compound Microscope

A compound microscope uses two convex lenses to achieve very high magnification of nearby small objects. The objective lens has a very short focal length (fo), and the eyepiece has a moderate focal length (fe).

Objective magnification: m_o = v_o / u_o
Eyepiece magnification: m_e = D / f_e  (D = 25 cm, near-point distance)
Total magnification: M = m_o × m_e ≈ (L / f_o) × (D / f_e)

where L is the tube length (distance between the second focal point of the objective and the first focal point of the eyepiece).

ComponentRoleFocal Length
ObjectiveForms real, inverted, magnified intermediate imageVery short (1–5 mm)
EyepieceActs as magnifier for intermediate imageShort-moderate (10–25 mm)

Final image: Virtual, inverted (relative to original object), highly magnified, formed at near point or infinity.

8. Astronomical Telescope

An astronomical telescope is designed to view distant objects. The objective lens has a long focal length and large aperture to collect maximum light. The eyepiece has a short focal length to provide high angular magnification.

Angular magnification (normal adjustment): M = f_o / f_e
Tube length (normal adjustment): L = f_o + f_e
ComponentRoleFocal Length
ObjectiveForms real, inverted, diminished image at focal planeLong (several cm to metres)
EyepieceMagnifies the intermediate imageShort

Final image: Virtual, inverted, highly magnified, formed at infinity (normal adjustment) or near point.

User Manual

This manual provides a complete guide to using the Interactive Optics Simulator. The application consists of three tabs: Simulation, Physics, and Manual (this page).

Overview of Optical Systems

🪞 Concave Mirror

Converging mirror. Reflects light inward. Used in headlights, telescopes, and makeup mirrors.

🪞 Convex Mirror

Diverging mirror. Always produces virtual, erect, diminished images. Used in rear-view mirrors.

🔵 Convex Lens

Converging lens. Can produce real or virtual images depending on object position. Used in cameras and eyes.

🔵 Concave Lens

Diverging lens. Always produces virtual, erect, diminished images. Used to correct myopia.

🔬 Microscope

Two-lens system for magnifying small nearby objects. Objective + Eyepiece arrangement.

🔭 Telescope

Two-lens system for viewing distant objects. Large objective + short-focal eyepiece.

How to Use the Simulation

1
Select an Optical System from the dropdown at the top of the left panel. The canvas and controls will update automatically.
2
Adjust the sliders to change the optical parameters. For single elements (mirrors/lenses), you can control Focal Length, Object Distance, and Object Height. For compound systems (Microscope/Telescope), you control the focal lengths of both lenses and the separation between them.
3
Observe the ray diagram on the canvas. Rays are colour-coded: orange (Ray 1), cyan (Ray 2), pink (Ray 3). Dashed lines indicate virtual extensions of rays. The blue arrow is the object, red arrow is the final image, and purple arrow is the intermediate image (for compound systems).
4
Read the calculated results in the Results panel below the sliders. This shows the image distance (v), magnification (m), image nature (Real/Virtual, Erect/Inverted), and the formula applied.
5
Explore edge cases by placing the object at the focal point (u = f) to see rays become parallel, or between the focal point and the lens/mirror to produce a virtual image.

Tips and Tricks

Tip 1: For a concave mirror, move the object distance slider slowly through the focal length value. You will see the image jump from real (in front) to virtual (behind the mirror) — this is the critical transition point.
Tip 2: For the microscope, set a very short objective focal length and a larger eyepiece focal length to achieve maximum magnification. The intermediate image should form between the two lenses.
Tip 3: For the telescope, increasing the objective focal length relative to the eyepiece focal length increases the angular magnification (M = fo/fe).
Note: All distances in the simulation are in arbitrary pixel units for visualisation purposes. The physics and ratios are accurate, but the absolute values do not correspond to real-world millimetres or centimetres.
