1. The Fourier Transform in Optics

Any picture can be written as a sum of sinusoidal gratings of different spatial frequencies (ν_x, ν_y), in lines per millimetre. The amplitude of each grating is given by the two-dimensional Fourier transform

F(ν_x, ν_y) = ∬ t(x, y) exp[−2πi(ν_x x + ν_y y)] dx dy

Coarse features are low frequencies, fine detail and sharp edges are high frequencies. The Fraunhofer diffraction pattern of a transparency t(x, y) is exactly this transform: a grating of period d sends light at angles sin θ = λν with ν = 1/d.

A lens performs the transform. A thin lens of focal length f brings every plane wave travelling at angle θ to one point x_F = f tan θ ≈ fθ in its back focal plane. A grating component of frequency ν diffracts at θ = λν, so it lands at
x_F = λ f ν.
If the transparency is in the front focal plane, the field in the back focal plane is U_F(x_F) ∝ F(x_F/λf): the lens is an analogue Fourier computer working at the speed of light.

2. The 4f System and Spatial Filtering

Two lenses of focal length f separated by 2f, with the object f before the first lens and the image f after the second: the total length is 4f. Lens 1 forms the transform in the middle plane (the Fourier or filter plane); lens 2 transforms it back, giving an inverted image of the object. Putting a mask H(x_F) in the filter plane multiplies each spatial frequency by H:

image = FT⁻¹{ H · FT{object} }   (a convolution of the object with the transform of H)

The iris of every real lens is an unwanted low-pass filter: a lens with aperture radius r in the Fourier plane passes frequencies up to ν_c = r/(λf), so it cannot image periods smaller than 1/ν_c. This is the Fourier view of the resolution limit.

In the 4f system (f = 200 mm, 633 nm) an iris of radius 0.5 mm is placed in the filter plane. Finest period that is still imaged?
ν_c = r/(λf) = 0.5 mm/(633 nm × 200 mm) = 3.95 lines/mm → period ≈ 0.25 mm. Finer gratings disappear completely: their first orders hit the iris.

3. Abbe's Theory of Image Formation

Ernst Abbe (1873), working with Carl Zeiss, explained imaging as a two-step process: the object diffracts the light into orders; the lens collects some of them; the image is the interference pattern of the collected orders. An image of a periodic structure is formed only if at least two orders (usually the zero and a first order) enter the lens.

Abbe limit. A grating of period d diffracts the first order at sin θ = λ/d. The objective accepts angles up to α (NA = n sin α). With axial illumination the first order is collected if λ/d ≤ NA → d ≥ λ/NA. With oblique illumination the zero order can be put at one edge of the aperture and the first order at the other:
d_min = λ / (2 NA).

Abbe–Porter experiment (Porter, 1906): a wire mesh imaged through a horizontal slit in the filter plane shows only vertical wires; rotate the slit and the horizontal wires appear instead. The image contains only what passes the filter. Try the preset.

An objective with NA 0.25 is used at 550 nm. Smallest grating period it can image (axial and oblique illumination)?
Axial: d = λ/NA = 2.2 µm. Oblique: d = λ/(2NA) = 1.1 µm.

4. Phase Contrast and Dark Field

Living cells are almost transparent: they change the phase of light (t = e^{iφ(x)}), not its amplitude, and the eye sees only intensity |t|² = 1. For a weak phase object e^{iφ} ≈ 1 + iφ: a strong zero order (the "1") plus weak diffracted light (iφ) that is 90° out of phase with it, so they do not interfere to change the brightness.

Zernike's trick (1935). Shift the zero order by a quarter wave (multiply it by i) with a small phase plate in the filter plane:
image ∝ |i + iφ|² = |1 + φ|² ≈ 1 + 2φ.
The phase now appears as brightness. Dimming the zero order by a factor a increases the contrast to ≈ 2φ/a. Zernike received the Nobel Prize in 1953.

Dark field: block the zero order completely; the image becomes |iφ|² = φ², dark background with bright edges and particles. Schlieren photography of shock waves and the Foucault knife-edge test of telescope mirrors use the same idea.

A cell shifts the phase by 0.1 rad. Contrast in phase contrast, and in dark field?
Phase contrast: ΔI/I ≈ 2φ = 20 %, easily visible. Dark field: intensity φ² = 1 % of the background that was removed, visible against black.

5. Holography

A photograph records only intensity; the phase (the direction and depth of light) is lost. Dennis Gabor (1948) showed that adding a coherent reference wave R turns phase into recordable fringes.

Recording. Object wave O and reference R meet at the film: the exposure is
H = |O + R|² = |O|² + |R|² + O R* + O* R.
Replay. Illuminating the developed film with R gives
R·H = R(|O|² + |R|²) + |R|² O + R² O*.
The middle term is the original object wave: you see the object in 3D, with parallax, exactly where it was (virtual image). The last term is the conjugate (twin) image, a real image on the other side.

In Gabor's in-line hologram all three terms travel in the same direction and the twin image blurs the picture. Leith and Upatnieks (1962), with a laser and an off-axis reference at angle θ, separated them: the terms leave at angles 0, +θ and −θ (three separate spots in the hologram's spectrum, as in the simulator). The fringe spacing λ/sin θ must be resolved by the film: holographic emulsions resolve over 3000 lines/mm.

Applications: security holograms (embossed rainbow holograms), holographic interferometry (measuring deformations of nanometres), holographic optical elements, data storage, digital holography (recording with a camera and reconstructing by computer, as here), and Denisyuk's white-light reflection holograms.

A He–Ne hologram uses a reference beam at 30° to the object beam. Fringe spacing and required resolution?
λ/sin θ = 633 nm/0.5 = 1.27 µm → about 790 lines/mm. Ordinary photographic film (≈ 100 lines/mm) cannot record it.
Why must the object and mirrors not move by more than about λ/4 during the exposure?
A movement of λ/4 changes the path by λ/2 (light goes there and back) and shifts the fringes by half a period, washing them out. Holography tables are isolated against vibration for this reason.

6. Colour Vision

Colour is not a property of light alone but of light plus the eye. The retina has rods (night vision, no colour) and three types of cones with broad, overlapping sensitivities: S (peak ≈ 420 nm), M (≈ 534 nm) and L (≈ 564 nm). Every spectrum, however complex, is reduced to three numbers, the responses of the three cone types. This is trichromacy (Young 1802, Helmholtz 1850, Maxwell 1855).

Consequences: (1) any colour can be matched by mixing three suitable primaries; (2) physically different spectra can look identical: metamers (try the preset); (3) colour blindness: about 8 % of men lack or have shifted M or L cones; (4) a single wavelength between red and green (yellow, 580 nm) and a mixture of red and green light look the same. The eye's luminance sensitivity V(λ) peaks at 555 nm (daylight) and 507 nm (night).

7. Colorimetry: the CIE 1931 System

Tristimulus values. The CIE defined three colour-matching functions x̄(λ), ȳ(λ), z̄(λ) (ȳ = V(λ), the brightness sensitivity). For a spectrum S(λ):
X = ∫S x̄ dλ,  Y = ∫S ȳ dλ,  Z = ∫S z̄ dλ.
Y is the luminance. The chromaticity coordinates remove the brightness:
x = X/(X + Y + Z),  y = Y/(X + Y + Z).

On the (x, y) diagram the pure spectral colours form the horseshoe-shaped spectral locus; the straight line of purples joins red and violet (magenta has no single wavelength). All real colours lie inside. Mixtures of two lights lie on the straight line between them; mixtures of three lie inside their triangle. White (D65 daylight) is near (0.313, 0.329).

Dominant wavelength: extend the line from white through the colour to the locus. Purity: how far along that line the colour is (0 % white, 100 % spectral). A screen's three phosphors or LEDs form its gamut triangle; colours outside it (deep spectral greens, cyans) cannot be shown. The sRGB triangle covers about 35 % of the visible colours; laser projectors with spectral primaries cover much more.

A colour has X = 40, Y = 30, Z = 30. Chromaticity?
x = 40/100 = 0.40, y = 30/100 = 0.30: a pinkish (purplish-red) colour below and right of white.

Each subtractive primary removes one additive primary: cyan absorbs red, magenta absorbs green, yellow absorbs blue. Printers add black ink (K) because three real inks never absorb perfectly. Objects are coloured because they absorb part of the illuminating spectrum; under a sodium lamp (one wavelength) every object looks yellow or black.

9. Thermal Radiation: Planck, Wien and Stefan–Boltzmann

Planck's law (1900). A blackbody at temperature T emits a spectral radiance
B(λ, T) = (2hc²/λ⁵) · 1/(exp(hc/λk_BT) − 1).
Planck had to assume that energy comes in quanta E = hf: the birth of quantum theory.
Wien's displacement law: λ_max T = b = 2.898 mm·K.
Stefan–Boltzmann law: total power per area M = σT⁴, σ = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴.

The Sun has T = 5778 K and radius 6.96×10⁸ m. Total power?

Where does a human body (310 K) radiate most, and how much per square metre?
λ_max = 2.898 mm·K / 310 K = 9.3 µm; σT⁴ = 524 W/m² (most of it is balanced by absorbing the surroundings' radiation).

10. Photometry and Light Sources

Radiometry measures energy (watts); photometry weighs it by the eye's sensitivity V(λ). The luminous flux is

Φ_v = 683 lm/W · ∫ V(λ) S(λ) dλ

A watt of 555 nm light gives 683 lumens, the maximum possible. The luminous efficacy of radiation (LER) of a spectrum is Φ_v divided by its radiant power; the efficacy of a lamp also includes its electrical efficiency. Other units: luminous intensity (candela = lm/sr), illuminance (lux = lm/m²; office 500 lx, sunlight 100 000 lx).

Why is an incandescent bulb so inefficient?
At 2700 K the peak is at 1.07 µm in the infrared; only about 10 % of the radiation is visible (simulator: 9.8 %). Raising T would help, but tungsten melts at 3695 K.

11. Scattering: Why the Sky Is Blue and Sunsets Are Red

Rayleigh scattering (1871). A molecule much smaller than λ is polarised by the light's field and re-radiates as an oscillating dipole. Dipole radiation power ∝ ω⁴, so the scattered fraction is
σ ∝ 1/λ⁴.
Blue light (450 nm) is scattered (650/450)⁴ = 4.4 times more than red (650 nm). The scattered intensity varies as 1 + cos²Θ (Θ = scattering angle) and the light scattered at 90° is completely polarised: P = sin²Θ/(1 + cos²Θ).

The blue sky. Looking away from the Sun you see sunlight scattered by air; it is enriched in short wavelengths. It looks blue rather than violet because the Sun emits less violet, ozone and the eye's sensitivity fall off, and the mixture of all scattered colours is a whitish blue.

Sunsets. The transmitted sunlight loses blue along its path: T(λ) = exp(−τ(λ)·m), with zenith optical depth τ ≈ 0.22 at 450 nm and 0.05 at 650 nm and air mass m ≈ 1/sin h (≈ 38 at the horizon). At sunset blue is completely removed and the Sun is red. Clouds lit from below glow orange; ozone, which absorbs orange (Chappuis band), keeps the twilight zenith blue.

Mie scattering. Particles comparable to or larger than λ (haze, droplets, clouds) scatter almost independently of wavelength and strongly forward: clouds are white, hazy skies whitish, and a bright aureole surrounds the Sun. Very small particles of similar size give special colours: blue moons after volcanic eruptions, the blue of distant mountains, the reddish tint of smoke-filled sunsets.

How much more is 400 nm violet scattered than 700 nm red?
(700/400)⁴ = 9.4 times.
The Sun is 60° high. What fraction of blue (450 nm) sunlight reaches the ground (Rayleigh only)?
m = 1/sin 60° = 1.155; T = exp(−0.223 × 1.155) = 0.77 (77 %). At 1° elevation (m ≈ 26) it is e⁻⁵·⁸ ≈ 0.3 %.

12. Halos and Sun Dogs

Cirrus clouds contain hexagonal ice prisms (n ≈ 1.31). Light entering one side face and leaving through the next face but one passes a 60° prism; through a side face and an end face, a 90° prism. As with the rainbow, the deviation has a minimum, and light piles up there.

Minimum deviation of a prism with apex angle A:
D_min = 2 arcsin(n sin(A/2)) − A.
A = 60°, n = 1.31: D = 2 arcsin(0.655) − 60° = 21.8° → the 22° halo.
A = 90°: D = 2 arcsin(0.926) − 90° = 45.7° → the 46° halo.
No light is deviated less than D_min, so the sky inside the halo is darker, and the inner edge is red (red is deviated least).

Sun dogs (parhelia). Flat plate crystals float horizontally; their vertical faces form 60° prisms for rays that are not horizontal. The effective index for a ray at the Sun's elevation h is n' = √(n² − sin²h)/cos h, so the sun dogs lie at 22° when the Sun is low and move outwards as it rises. Above about 61° elevation the rays cannot pass the crystal and sun dogs disappear. Other members of the family: parhelic circle, circumzenithal arc, sun pillars (reflection).

Where are the sun dogs when the Sun is 20° high?
n' = √(1.31² − sin²20°)/cos 20° = 1.346; D = 2 arcsin(1.346 × 0.5) − 60° = 24.6° from the Sun, about 2.8° outside the 22° halo.

13. How a Laser Works

LASER = Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. Three processes connect two energy levels E₁ and E₂ (hf = E₂ − E₁): absorption (rate B₁₂ρN₁), spontaneous emission (rate A₂₁N₂, random direction and phase) and stimulated emission (rate B₂₁ρN₂), which produces a photon identical to the incoming one: same frequency, direction, phase and polarisation (Einstein, 1917).

Einstein relations. Requiring equilibrium with Planck radiation gives B₁₂ = B₂₁ (equal degeneracies) and A₂₁/B₂₁ = 8πhf³/c³.
Gain needs population inversion. Net amplification ∝ (N₂ − N₁). In thermal equilibrium N₂/N₁ = exp(−hf/k_BT) ≪ 1, so light is absorbed. Pumping must make N₂ > N₁.

Three- and four-level lasers. In a two-level system pumping can only equalise the populations. In a three-level laser (ruby, 1960) more than half of all atoms must be pumped. In a four-level laser (Nd:YAG, He–Ne) the lower laser level empties quickly, so any pumping gives inversion: much lower threshold.

Threshold. In a cavity of length L with mirror reflectances R₁, R₂ and internal loss α, light grows after a round trip if
R₁R₂ exp[2(g − α)L] ≥ 1 → g_th = α + (1/2L) ln(1/R₁R₂).
Above threshold the gain is clamped at g_th: extra pumping produces photons, not inversion, and the output rises linearly with pump power (slope efficiency).

Properties of laser light: monochromatic (narrow line), coherent (long coherence length), directional (divergence ≈ λ/πw₀), bright (high radiance), and it can be made into extremely short pulses.

Population ratio N₂/N₁ for the He–Ne transition (1.96 eV) at room temperature?
exp(−1.96 eV / 0.0259 eV) = exp(−75.8) ≈ 10⁻³³: essentially no atoms are excited without pumping.
A 30 cm cavity has mirrors R₁ = 100 %, R₂ = 99 % and no other loss. Threshold gain?
g_th = (1/0.6 m) ln(1/0.99) = 0.0168 m⁻¹: the medium must amplify by 1.7 % per metre, which a He–Ne discharge does.

14. Laser Dynamics: Relaxation Oscillations, Q-Switching, Mode Locking

Rate equations (normalised: n = N/N_th, s = photon number in units of the saturation level, p = pump/threshold):
dn/dt = (p − n − n s)/τ,   ds/dt = [(n − 1) s + ε n]/τ_c
τ = upper-level lifetime, τ_c = cavity photon lifetime (τ_c = 2L/[c(1 − R₁R₂)] for small losses), ε = spontaneous emission into the mode.
Steady state above threshold: n = 1, s = p − 1.
Small disturbances oscillate at ω_r = √((p − 1)/(τ τ_c)) and decay in ≈ 2τ/p: relaxation oscillations.

Solid-state lasers (τ/τ_c ≈ 10⁴) switch on with a train of spikes; semiconductor lasers oscillate at GHz, which limits how fast they can be modulated; gas lasers (τ ≈ τ_c) switch on smoothly.

Q-switching (McClung and Hellwarth, 1962). Keep the cavity loss high (low Q) while pumping, so the inversion builds to N_i far above threshold; then suddenly switch to low loss. The stored energy leaves in a single giant pulse a few τ_c long. The final inversion follows from N_i − N_f = N_th ln(N_i/N_f). Typical Nd:YAG: 10 ns, 100 mJ → 10 MW peak; used for tattoo removal, LIDAR, laser ablation and pumping other lasers.

Longitudinal modes and mode locking. A cavity supports frequencies spaced by Δν_FSR = c/2L. All modes inside the gain bandwidth Δν lase: N ≈ Δν/(c/2L). With random phases the output is noisy. If the phases are locked, the N modes add like a grating in time: a single pulse circulates in the cavity, the output is a train with period 2L/c and pulse width
Δt ≈ 1/Δν (0.44/Δν for Gaussian pulses), peak power ≈ N × average power.
Gain mediumΔνShortest pulse

Femtosecond pulses freeze molecular motion (femtochemistry, Nobel 1999), make frequency combs for atomic clocks (Nobel 2005), and after chirped-pulse amplification (Strickland and Mourou, Nobel 2018) reach petawatt powers; high-harmonic generation then yields attosecond pulses (Nobel 2023).

A mode-locked laser has 1 W average power, 100 MHz repetition rate and 10 fs pulses. Pulse energy and peak power?
Energy = 1 W / 10⁸ s⁻¹ = 10 nJ. Peak ≈ 10 nJ / 10 fs = 1 MW.
How many longitudinal modes fit in the gain of a 30 cm He–Ne laser?
c/2L = 500 MHz; 1.5 GHz / 500 MHz = 3 modes. A 10 cm tube (1.5 GHz spacing) runs on a single mode.

15. Fibre-Optic Communication

Light is guided in a glass core by total internal reflection (the Advanced Optics module treats the ray optics). For communication three questions matter: how much light is lost, how much pulses spread, and how much power the receiver needs.

Attenuation in decibels. Loss(dB) = 10 log₁₀(P_in/P_out); power in dBm = 10 log₁₀(P/1 mW). Losses simply add: P_out(dBm) = P_in(dBm) − αL − connector and splice losses.
Silica loss α(λ) has three parts: Rayleigh scattering ∝ 1/λ⁴ (dominant below 1.3 µm), infrared absorption of Si–O bonds (above 1.6 µm), and an OH absorption peak at 1383 nm. Minimum: 0.2 dB/km at 1550 nm (1 % left after 100 km).
Dispersion (pulse spreading).
• Modal, step-index multimode: rays at the critical angle travel farther: Δt = L n₁ Δ / c, Δ = (n₁ − n₂)/n₁. For Δ = 1 %: 49 ns/km.
• Graded-index multimode: outer rays travel through lower index, faster: Δt = L n₁ Δ²/(8c) ≈ 0.06 ns/km, 800 times better.
• Single-mode: no modal dispersion; chromatic dispersion remains: Δt = |D| Δλ L, with D ≈ 0 at 1310 nm and D ≈ 17 ps/(nm·km) at 1550 nm.
Rule of thumb: maximum bit rate B ≈ 1/(2Δt); when pulses overlap the receiver's "eye" closes (intersymbol interference).

How the capacity grew: narrow-line DFB lasers reduced chromatic dispersion; dispersion-compensating fibre and digital signal processing undo the rest; erbium-doped fibre amplifiers (1987) amplify all channels at once without converting to electronics; wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) sends 80+ wavelengths in one fibre. A modern undersea cable carries over 200 Tb/s. Charles Kao predicted (1966) that purified glass could reach 20 dB/km, and received the Nobel Prize in 2009.

A 0 dBm laser feeds 100 km of fibre at 0.2 dB/km with 2 dB of connector loss. Received power? Is it enough for a −30 dBm receiver?
P = 0 − 20 − 2 = −22 dBm (6.3 µW). Margin 8 dB: enough.
A 1550 nm DFB laser with 0.1 nm linewidth sends over 100 km of standard fibre. Pulse spreading and maximum bit rate?
Δt = 17 × 0.1 × 100 = 170 ps; B ≈ 1/(2Δt) ≈ 2.9 Gb/s. Higher rates need narrower lasers, compensation or coherent detection.
Compare 1 km of step-index and graded-index multimode fibre (n₁ = 1.48, Δ = 1 %).
Step: 1000 × 1.48 × 0.01 / 3×10⁸ = 49 ns → about 10 Mb/s. Graded: 1000 × 1.48 × 10⁻⁴ / (8 × 3×10⁸) = 62 ps → several Gb/s.

17. Practice Problems

P1. A 4f system has f = 300 mm and λ = 532 nm. A mesh of period 50 µm is the object. How far apart are the spots in the Fourier plane?
λf/d = 532 nm × 300 mm / 50 µm = 3.19 mm.
P2. Which filter turns the image of a mesh into horizontal lines only?
A vertical slit: it passes only frequencies along the vertical axis (variations in y), which belong to horizontal lines.
P3. An oil objective (NA 1.4) is used with green light (550 nm) and oblique illumination. Abbe limit?
d = λ/(2NA) = 196 nm.
P4. A hologram is recorded with a reference at 10° (λ = 633 nm). Fringe spacing?
633 nm / sin 10° = 3.6 µm (≈ 270 lines/mm).
P5. Red light (x, y) = (0.70, 0.30) and green light (0.20, 0.70) are mixed with equal X + Y + Z. Chromaticity of the mixture?
With equal "weights" X + Y + Z the mixture is the midpoint: (0.45, 0.50), a yellow.
P6. A star's spectrum peaks at 380 nm. Its temperature? How much more power per area than the Sun?
T = 2.898 mm·K / 380 nm = 7630 K; (7630/5778)⁴ = 3.0 times.
P7. A lamp gives 1600 lm from 10 W of electrical power. Efficacy? Compare with the 683 lm/W limit.
160 lm/W, 23 % of the theoretical maximum for 555 nm light (white light has a lower limit, about 250–350 lm/W).
P8. Why are clouds white and smoke from a distance bluish?
Cloud droplets (≈ 10 µm) are much larger than λ: Mie scattering is nearly wavelength-independent. Fine smoke particles (≪ λ) scatter like molecules, ∝ λ⁻⁴, so the scattered light is bluish.
P9. For ice the 22° halo uses a 60° prism. What would the halo radius be for glass prisms (n = 1.5)?
D = 2 arcsin(1.5 × 0.5) − 60° = 2 × 48.6° − 60° = 37.2°.
P10. A diode laser cavity is 300 µm long (n = 3.5). Mode spacing in GHz?
c/(2nL) = 3×10⁸/(2 × 3.5 × 3×10⁻⁴) = 143 GHz.
P11. A Q-switched laser stores N_i = 3 N_th. What fraction of the stored energy is extracted?
Solve N_i − N_f = ln(N_i/N_f) with N_i = 3: N_f ≈ 0.18, so (3 − 0.18)/3 = 94 %.
P12. A 10 Gb/s link at 1550 nm (D = 17 ps/(nm·km)) uses a laser with 0.05 nm linewidth. Dispersion-limited length?
Δt ≤ 1/(2B) = 50 ps → L = 50 / (17 × 0.05) ≈ 59 km.

19. Limits of the Models in This Module

Fourier optics: scalar, paraxial, coherent illumination; 256 × 256 samples with periodic boundaries; the Fourier display uses a logarithmic scale. Holography: thin amplitude hologram, linear film response, angular-spectrum propagation on a 1 mm grid. Colour: CIE 1931 2° observer (analytic fit), equal-energy or 6500 K white, LED and filter spectra are idealised; screen colours outside sRGB are clipped. Sky: single scattering in a plane-parallel atmosphere with Rayleigh, one aerosol type and ozone absorption; multiple scattering, which brightens the twilight sky and lowers polarisation, is not included. Halos: minimum-deviation positions only. Lasers: single-mode normalised rate equations; mode locking adds ideal modes with Gaussian amplitudes. Fibres: simplified loss curve, rule-of-thumb bit-rate limit and receiver sensitivity; nonlinear effects and polarisation-mode dispersion are ignored.
