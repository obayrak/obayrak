Interferometry, Polarisation and Photonic Structures — Theory

This chapter treats light as a wave with a phase and a direction of vibration, and follows what happens when that phase and polarisation are put to work. The first part covers interferometers and coherence: the Michelson, Mach–Zehnder and Fabry–Pérot instruments, the connection between fringe visibility and the spectrum, Fourier-transform spectroscopy and gravitational-wave detectors. The second part covers polarisation: the Jones and Stokes descriptions, the Poincaré sphere, birefringent crystals, stress, optical activity and liquid crystals. The third part covers layered and patterned media: thin-film coatings, photonic crystals, Fresnel's equations, evanescent waves, surface plasmons and negative-index metamaterials. Practice problems, a history and the limits of the models close the chapter.

1. Two-Beam Interference

When two waves of the same frequency and polarisation overlap, their fields add. A detector measures the time-averaged intensity, which for fields E₁ and E₂ with a phase difference φ is

I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂) cos φ, φ = 2πΔ/λ

where Δ is the optical path difference (geometric path × refractive index). Bright fringes occur where Δ = mλ, dark fringes where Δ = (m + ½)λ. The contrast of the pattern is measured by the visibility

V = (I_max − I_min)/(I_max + I_min) = 2√(I₁I₂)/(I₁ + I₂)

Equal beams give V = 1. Beams with a 9 : 1 intensity ratio still give V = 0.6, which is why interference is easy to see even with unbalanced beams.

Derivation. With E₁ = A₁ cos(ωt) and E₂ = A₂ cos(ωt − φ), the intensity is proportional to ⟨(E₁ + E₂)²⟩ = A₁²/2 + A₂²/2 + A₁A₂⟨2 cos ωt cos(ωt − φ)⟩. The last average is cos φ, because 2 cos a cos b = cos(a − b) + cos(a + b) and the sum term averages to zero. Writing I = A²/2 gives the formula above.

Two arrangements produce the two beams. Wavefront division takes light from different parts of the wavefront (Young's slits, Lloyd's mirror). Amplitude division splits every part of the wavefront with a partly reflecting surface (thin films, beam splitters). Amplitude division works with extended sources and gives bright fringes; all the interferometers in this module use it.

2. The Michelson Interferometer

A beam splitter sends light to two mirrors M1 and M2 and recombines the reflected beams. If M1 is a distance d further from the splitter than the image of M2, the two beams act like reflections from an air film of thickness d. For light travelling at angle θ to the axis the path difference is

Δ = 2d cos θ

With an extended source and parallel mirrors, all rays at the same θ have the same Δ, so the fringes are concentric rings at infinity (Haidinger fringes), seen in the focal plane of a lens or with the eye relaxed. Bright rings satisfy 2d cos θ_m = mλ. The centre has the highest order m₀ = 2d/λ; the p-th bright ring from a bright centre has angular radius

θ_p ≈ √(pλ/d)

so as d grows the rings crowd together, and as d → 0 the centre ring expands until the whole field is uniform. If one mirror is tilted by a small angle α, the film becomes a wedge and the fringes become straight lines parallel to the tilt axis, spaced λ/(2α) on the mirror (Fizeau fringes).

Fringe counting. Moving the mirror by λ/2 changes Δ by λ: one fringe passes the centre. Counting N fringes measures a displacement d = Nλ/2. With a stabilised He–Ne laser this is the basis of displacement-measuring interferometers that calibrate machine tools to a few nanometres over metres of travel.

3. The Mach–Zehnder Interferometer

Here the two beams travel separate arms and are recombined at a second beam splitter, which has two outputs. If the phase difference between the arms is φ, the outputs receive

P_A = P₀ cos²(φ/2), P_B = P₀ sin²(φ/2), P_A + P_B = P₀

Energy is conserved: light that interferes destructively at one port appears at the other. The two outputs are complementary because the reflected and transmitted waves at a lossless beam splitter differ in phase by π/2, which follows from energy conservation itself (the beam-splitter matrix must be unitary).

Because each arm is traversed only once and can be far from the other, the Mach–Zehnder is used to measure phase objects: gas flows and shock waves in wind tunnels, plasma density, and temperature fields. In integrated optics it is the standard electro-optic modulator: a voltage changes the index of a lithium niobate waveguide in one arm, switching the light between the outputs at tens of gigahertz. It is also the basic element of quantum optics experiments with single photons, where it shows that one photon takes both paths (see Module 8).

Worked example: what extra path in one arm switches all the light from port A to port B?
φ must change by π, so Δ = λ/2 = 316 nm at 633 nm. In a lithium niobate modulator the voltage that does this is called V_π, typically 2–5 V.

4. Temporal Coherence

Real sources emit a range of wavelengths. Each wavelength makes its own fringe pattern; at zero path difference all patterns have a bright fringe together, but as Δ grows they drift out of step and the fringes wash out. The visibility as a function of path difference is the degree of temporal coherence |γ(τ)| with τ = Δ/c. The Wiener–Khinchin theorem states that γ is the Fourier transform of the normalised spectrum S(ν):

γ(τ) = ∫ S(ν) e^(−2πiντ) dν / ∫ S(ν) dν
Why. Each frequency ν gives the two-beam signal S(ν)[1 + cos(2πντ)]. Integrating over the spectrum, the constant terms give ∫S and the cosine terms give the real part of ∫S e^(−2πiντ) dν. For a symmetric line about ν₀ this equals cos(2πν₀τ) × |γ(τ)|: fast fringes under a slowly varying envelope, the envelope being the Fourier transform of the line shape.

A line of width Δν therefore gives an envelope of width τ_c ≈ 1/Δν, the coherence time, and a coherence length

l_c = c τ_c ≈ λ²/Δλ

The sodium doublet. Two lines of intensities I₁ and I₂ separated by Δλ = 0.597 nm beat against each other. The visibility oscillates as

V(Δ) = √(I₁² + I₂² + 2I₁I₂ cos(2πΔ Δλ/λ²)) / (I₁ + I₂)

It is smallest, V_min = |I₁ − I₂|/(I₁ + I₂), when Δ = λ²/(2Δλ) = 0.29 mm, that is when the mirror has moved 0.145 mm. For the D lines (intensity ratio about 2 : 1) V_min = 1/3. Michelson used exactly this measurement in 1892 to discover that the red cadmium line was single and nearly ideal, which led to its use as a length standard.

White-light fringes. With a white source only a handful of coloured fringes appear, within about 1 µm of zero path difference. They are used to find Δ = 0 exactly, for example to adjust the arms of a stellar interferometer or to measure surface profiles in white-light scanning interferometry (each pixel's envelope peak gives its height to a few nanometres). Optical coherence tomography uses the same principle with a broadband infrared source: the short coherence length acts as a depth gate, giving cross-sections of the retina with 5–10 µm resolution.

5. Spatial Coherence

Temporal coherence concerns one point at two times; spatial coherence concerns two points at one time. Light from an extended incoherent source of angular diameter α interferes well only between points closer than the coherence width

w ≈ λ/α (for a uniform disc, the first zero of visibility is at 1.22 λ/α)

This is the van Cittert–Zernike theorem: the degree of spatial coherence is the Fourier transform of the source's brightness distribution, just as the Fraunhofer pattern is the transform of an aperture. Sunlight (α = 0.53°) is coherent over about 60 µm, which is why Young's slits lit directly by the Sun must be very close together. A star of 0.05″ is coherent over several metres.

Michelson turned this around in 1920: by spreading two mirrors on a 6 m beam across the 100-inch Mount Wilson telescope until the fringes from Betelgeuse vanished, he measured its angular diameter, 0.047″, the first measurement of the size of a star other than the Sun. Modern optical interferometers (VLTI, CHARA) and radio arrays (VLA, the Event Horizon Telescope) work on the same principle.

6. The Fabry–Pérot Interferometer

Two parallel mirrors of reflectance R, a distance g apart, make a multiple-beam interferometer. Each round trip adds a phase

δ = 4π n g cos θ / λ
Derivation (Airy function). Let each mirror have amplitude reflection r and transmission t, with R = r², T = t² and R + T = 1 (no loss). The transmitted field is the sum of beams that made 0, 1, 2, … round trips:
E_t = E₀ t² (1 + r²e^(iδ) + r⁴e^(2iδ) + …) = E₀ T / (1 − R e^(iδ)).
Multiplying by the complex conjugate: I_t/I₀ = T² / (1 + R² − 2R cos δ). Using 1 − cos δ = 2 sin²(δ/2) and T = 1 − R,
I_t/I₀ = 1 / (1 + F sin²(δ/2)), F = 4R/(1 − R)²

Transmission is 100% whenever δ = 2πm, whatever R is; between peaks it falls to 1/(1 + F). For high R the peaks become very narrow. Their full width at half maximum in δ is 4/√F, so the ratio of peak spacing (2π) to peak width is the finesse

ℱ = π√F / 2 = π√R / (1 − R)

Neighbouring orders are separated by the free spectral range, and the smallest resolvable wavelength difference is the free spectral range divided by the finesse:

FSR: Δν = c/(2ng), Δλ = λ²/(2ng); δλ_min = FSR/ℱ; λ/δλ = m ℱ, m = 2ng/λ

Worked example: an etalon with g = 5 mm and R = 0.9 at 633 nm. Find F, ℱ, the FSR and the resolution.

Worked example: how can an etalon resolve the sodium doublet (0.597 nm) if its FSR is only about 0.09 nm?
Each line makes its own ring system; the systems are shifted by Δλ/FSR orders. Only the fractional part matters: choose g so that Δλ/FSR = k + ½ and the rings of the two lines interleave exactly. With g = 1.89 mm, FSR = 91.8 pm and Δλ/FSR = 6.5 (the simulator's preset).

7. Fourier-Transform Spectroscopy

If a Michelson mirror is scanned and the on-axis intensity is recorded, the signal (the interferogram) is

I(x) = ∫ S(σ) [1 + cos(2πσx)] dσ, σ = 1/λ (wavenumber), x = path difference

Apart from a constant, the interferogram is the cosine transform of the spectrum, so the spectrum is recovered by the inverse transform:

S(σ) ∝ ∫₀^x_max [I(x) − ½I(0)] cos(2πσx) dx
Resolution from a finite scan. A single line σ₀ gives cos(2πσ₀x). Integrating only to x_max, the recovered line is ∫₀^x_max cos(2πσ₀x) cos(2πσx) dx ≈ (x_max/2) sinc[2π(σ − σ₀)x_max]. This instrument line shape has a full width at half maximum of 0.60/x_max and negative side lobes (−22%). Two lines are resolved roughly when their separation exceeds this width:
δσ ≈ 0.6/x_max, δλ ≈ 0.6 λ²/x_max.
To resolve the sodium doublet (δσ = 17 cm⁻¹) needs x_max ≳ 0.35 mm; the simulator shows the lines separating as the scan lengthens.

Apodisation. Multiplying the interferogram by a window that falls smoothly to zero (triangular, Happ–Genzel, Norton–Beer) suppresses the side lobes at the cost of a wider line: a triangular window gives a sinc² line shape, FWHM 0.89/x_max, side lobes only 4.5%.

Sampling. The interferogram must be sampled at least twice per period of the shortest wavelength, Δx ≤ λ_min/2 (Nyquist). Commercial FTIR spectrometers sample at the zero crossings of a He–Ne reference laser's fringes, which also gives an absolute wavelength scale (the Connes advantage).

Why FT spectrometers win in the infrared. The Fellgett (multiplex) advantage: every wavelength is measured during the whole scan, so with detector-noise-limited detection the signal-to-noise ratio improves by √M for M spectral elements compared with a scanning monochromator. The Jacquinot (throughput) advantage: a circular aperture passes far more light than a narrow slit at the same resolution. Fast Fourier transform algorithms (Cooley and Tukey, 1965) made the method practical; FTIR is now the standard instrument for identifying molecules by their vibrational spectra.

8. Gravitational-Wave Detectors

A gravitational wave passing through a Michelson stretches one arm and squeezes the other by a fraction h/2 (the strain h). The signal is the difference of arm lengths, ΔL = hL. The first detection, GW150914 from two merging black holes 1.3 billion light years away, had a peak strain of 1.0 × 10⁻²¹. On LIGO's 4 km arms this is ΔL ≈ 4 × 10⁻¹⁸ m, about one thousandth of a proton diameter and about 10⁻¹¹ of a fringe.

TechniquePurpose

The phase shift from ΔL is 4πΔL/λ per round trip; the shot-noise limit on measuring a phase with N photons is about 1/√N. Using many photons (high power), many round trips (cavities) and squeezed vacuum, LIGO reaches a displacement sensitivity of about 10⁻²⁰ m/√Hz near 100 Hz. Weiss, Barish and Thorne received the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics.

9. Describing Polarisation

For a plane wave travelling along z, the transverse field can be written as a complex two-component Jones vector E = (E_x, E_y). In this module the physical field is Re[E e^(i(ωt − kz))], and the handedness is named as seen by an observer facing the oncoming beam: right circular light rotates clockwise for that observer (the traditional optics convention).

StateJones vectorStokes (S₁, S₂, S₃)/S₀On the Poincaré sphere
Horizontal (H)(1, 0)(1, 0, 0)equator, +S₁
Vertical (V)(0, 1)(−1, 0, 0)equator, −S₁
Linear +45°(1, 1)/√2(0, 1, 0)equator, +S₂
Linear −45°(1, −1)/√2(0, −1, 0)equator, −S₂
Right circular (R)(1, i)/√2(0, 0, 1)north pole
Left circular (L)(1, −i)/√2(0, 0, −1)south pole
Unpolarised—(0, 0, 0)centre

Jones vectors describe fully polarised light. Partly polarised light needs the four real Stokes parameters, which are directly measurable intensities:

S₀ = I_H + I_V, S₁ = I_H − I_V, S₂ = I₊₄₅ − I₋₄₅, S₃ = I_R − I_L

The degree of polarisation is p = √(S₁² + S₂² + S₃²)/S₀. For the polarised part with azimuth ψ (orientation of the major axis) and ellipticity angle χ (tan χ = ratio of minor to major axis, positive for right-handed):

S₁ = pS₀ cos 2χ cos 2ψ, S₂ = pS₀ cos 2χ sin 2ψ, S₃ = pS₀ sin 2χ

So (S₁, S₂, S₃)/S₀ is a point at longitude 2ψ and latitude 2χ on a sphere of radius p: the Poincaré sphere. Linear states lie on the equator, circular states at the poles, elliptical states in between and partly polarised states inside. Orthogonal states (which a polariser can separate completely) are diametrically opposite. Note the doubled angles: H and V are 90° apart in space but 180° apart on the sphere.

Conventions differ: many physics texts use e^(i(kz − ωt)), which swaps the sign of the imaginary unit in the Jones vectors, and some name handedness as seen from the source. Always check which convention a book or instrument uses.

10. Jones and Mueller Calculus

Each linear optical element is a 2 × 2 complex Jones matrix; a train of elements multiplies the matrices in the order the light meets them (the last element on the left). With the rotation matrix R(θ) = [cos θ, sin θ; −sin θ, cos θ] that takes the lab frame into the element's frame:

ElementJones matrix
Linear polariser, axis at θ[cos²θ, cos θ sin θ; cos θ sin θ, sin²θ]
Retarder, fast axis at θ, retardance ΓR(−θ) diag(1, e^(−iΓ)) R(θ)
Quarter-wave plate (Γ = π/2)R(−θ) diag(1, −i) R(θ)
Half-wave plate (Γ = π)[cos 2θ, sin 2θ; sin 2θ, −cos 2θ]
Rotator (optical activity, Faraday) by ρ[cos ρ, −sin ρ; sin ρ, cos ρ]
Worked example: horizontal light through a quarter-wave plate at 45°. R(45°)(1, 0) = (1, −1)/√2. diag(1, −i) gives (1, i)/√2. Rotating back, R(−45°)(1, i)/√2 = ½(1 − i, 1 + i) = ((1 − i)/2)(1, i). Apart from a phase factor this is (1, i)/√2: right circular light. On the Poincaré sphere the retarder rotated the state by 90° about its axis point (+S₂), carrying it from the equator to the north pole.

Half-wave plates reflect linear polarisation about the fast axis: light at angle ψ leaves at 2θ − ψ. Turning the plate by θ turns the polarisation by 2θ, which is how laser power is adjusted with a half-wave plate and a polariser.

For partly polarised light the 4 × 4 real Mueller matrices act on the Stokes vector. Those used in the simulator (c = cos 2θ, s = sin 2θ) are:

Polariser: ½[1, c, s, 0; c, c², cs, 0; s, cs, s², 0; 0, 0, 0, 0]
Retarder: [1, 0, 0, 0; 0, c² + s² cos Γ, cs(1 − cos Γ), −s sin Γ; 0, cs(1 − cos Γ), s² + c² cos Γ, c sin Γ; 0, s sin Γ, −c sin Γ, cos Γ]

Geometry on the sphere. A retarder rotates the sphere by Γ about the axis through its fast-axis point (longitude 2θ on the equator). A rotator turns the sphere by 2ρ about the polar axis. A polariser projects the state onto its own axis point and transmits a fraction cos²(Θ/2), where Θ is the angle on the sphere between the state and the axis point (this is Malus's law, generalised to any polarisation).

Worked example: unpolarised light meets polarisers at 0°, 45° and 90°. What fraction is transmitted?
½ × cos²45° × cos²45° = ½ × ½ × ½ = 12.5%. Without the middle polariser nothing passes. (With polarised input at 0°, as in the simulator's preset, the result is 25%.)
Worked example (Sénarmont method): how do you measure an unknown elliptical state?
Rotate a quarter-wave plate until its axis is along the major axis of the ellipse; the light becomes linear at ψ ± χ. A polariser then finds the extinction angle, from which χ follows. On the sphere: the quarter-wave plate rotates the point down to the equator, and the polariser at the opposite point extinguishes it.

11. Birefringent Crystals

In an anisotropic crystal the electric permittivity depends on the direction of the field. The refractive index for a wave travelling in a given direction is found from the index ellipsoid x²/n_x² + y²/n_y² + z²/n_z² = 1: the plane through the centre perpendicular to the wave vector cuts an ellipse, whose semi-axes are the two allowed refractive indices and whose axes are the two allowed polarisation directions.

In a uniaxial crystal n_x = n_y = n_o ≠ n_z = n_e. The direction z is the optic axis: light travelling along it sees no birefringence. For a wave at angle θ to the optic axis:

ordinary wave: n = n_o (E perpendicular to the plane containing the optic axis and k)
extraordinary wave: 1/n(θ)² = cos²θ/n_o² + sin²θ/n_e²

The extraordinary wave's energy (Poynting vector) is not parallel to its wave vector. The walk-off angle ρ between them is

tan ρ = ½ sin 2θ (1/n_e² − 1/n_o²) / (cos²θ/n_o² + sin²θ/n_e²)

So a beam entering a crystal at normal incidence splits into two parallel beams separated by t tan ρ after thickness t: double refraction. For calcite cut along its natural cleavage faces (optic axis at 44.6° to the face normal), ρ = 6.2°, so a 15 mm rhomb separates the images by 1.6 mm. The two images are polarised at right angles; rotating the crystal makes the extraordinary image circle around the ordinary one.

12. Retarders, Interference Colours and Photoelasticity

A plate of birefringent material cut with the optic axis in its face delays the slow polarisation component relative to the fast one by

Γ = 2π Δn t / λ (retardance; as a path, Δn t)

A quarter-wave plate has Δn t = λ/4, a half-wave plate λ/2. A zero-order quartz quarter-wave plate at 589 nm is only t = λ/(4Δn) = 16 µm thick, so practical plates are either multi-order (thicker, very sensitive to wavelength and temperature) or zero-order compounds of two plates with crossed axes.

Interference colours. Between crossed polarisers a retarder with its axes at 45° transmits

T(λ) = sin²(πΔn t/λ) (crossed), T(λ) = cos²(πΔn t/λ) (parallel)

With white light, wavelengths for which Δn t = mλ are blocked, and the remaining light is coloured. The sequence of colours with increasing retardance (grey, white, yellow, red, blue, green… repeating in paler orders) is the Michel-Lévy chart, used by geologists to identify minerals in thin sections. Parallel polarisers give the complementary colours. Layers of cellophane tape (100–250 nm each) make the same colours: this is the basis of polarisation art.

Photoelasticity. Stress makes transparent materials birefringent in proportion to the difference of the principal stresses (the stress-optic law, Brewster 1816):

Δn = C(σ₁ − σ₂), N = Γ/λ = t(σ₁ − σ₂)/f_σ, f_σ = λ/C

C is the stress-optic coefficient (about 78 × 10⁻¹² Pa⁻¹ for polycarbonate, giving the material fringe value f_σ ≈ 7 kN/m at 546 nm). In a plane polariscope two families of dark lines appear: isochromatics (coloured fringes, constant σ₁ − σ₂) and isoclinics (black lines where a principal-stress direction is parallel to the polariser). Adding two quarter-wave plates (a circular polariscope) removes the isoclinics. For a disc of diameter D and thickness t compressed by a force P across a diameter, the centre has σ₁ − σ₂ = 8P/(πDt), a classic calibration test. Engineers used photoelastic models to design dams, gear teeth and aircraft parts before computer stress analysis; the method is still used to find stress in glass (car windows, phone screens) and in transparent injection mouldings.

Worked example: a 40 mm polycarbonate disc is compressed with 500 N. What fringe order is seen at the centre?
N = 8P/(πD f_σ) = 8 × 500 /(π × 0.04 × 7000) = 4.5 (the thickness cancels). The centre shows the colours between the 4th and 5th order.

13. Optical Activity and the Faraday Effect

Chiral molecules (which differ from their mirror image) and some crystals rotate the plane of linear polarisation. The cause is circular birefringence: right and left circular light travel with slightly different indices n_R and n_L. Linear light is the sum of the two circular states; after length L they have a relative phase 2π(n_L − n_R)L/λ, which rotates the plane by half of it:

ρ = π(n_L − n_R) L / λ

For solutions the rotation is written with the specific rotation [α] (degrees per decimetre of path per g/mL):

α = [α]_λ c L
Substance[α] at 589 nm
Sucrose+66.5°
D-glucose (equilibrium)+52.7°
D-fructose−92°
Crystal quartz (along the axis)21.7° per mm

The rotation grows strongly towards the blue (approximately as 1/λ², Drude's law), so between polarisers a sugar solution shows colours that change as the analyser turns. Measuring α in a saccharimeter gives the sugar concentration; the "inversion" of sucrose to glucose plus fructose changes the sign of the rotation, which gives "invert sugar" its name.

The Faraday effect (1845) is a rotation produced by a magnetic field B along the beam: ρ = 𝒱BL, with 𝒱 the Verdet constant (for terbium gallium garnet about 134 rad T⁻¹ m⁻¹ at 633 nm). Unlike optical activity it is non-reciprocal: the rotation has the same sense relative to B whichever way the light travels, so a reflected beam is rotated twice rather than undone. A 45° Faraday rotator between polarisers at 0° and 45° is an optical isolator: light passes forwards, but returning light arrives at 90° to the input polariser and is blocked. Isolators protect lasers from their own reflections.

Worked example: a 2 dm tube of sucrose solution rotates sodium light by 39.9°. What is the concentration?
c = α/([α]L) = 39.9/(66.5 × 2) = 0.30 g/mL, i.e. 30 g per 100 mL.

14. Liquid Crystals and Displays

Nematic liquid crystals are fluids of rod-shaped molecules that line up along a common direction (the director). They are strongly birefringent (Δn ≈ 0.1–0.2), and the director can be set by rubbed alignment layers on the glass and turned by an electric field of only a few volts per micrometre.

In a twisted-nematic (TN) cell the two alignment layers are at 90°, so the director twists by 90° across the cell gap d. If the twist is slow compared with the birefringent phase (Δn d ≫ λ/2, the Mauguin condition), the polarisation follows the director like light in a waveguide and leaves rotated by 90°. Between crossed polarisers the cell is then bright. The exact result for a 90° twist (Gooch and Tarry, 1975) is

T_crossed = 1 − sin²((π/2)√(1 + u²)) / (1 + u²), u = 2Δn d/λ

Transmission is 100% at u = √3, √15, √35, …; displays use the first minimum, u = √3 (d ≈ 4.8 µm for Δn = 0.1 at 550 nm), because it is least sensitive to wavelength and viewing angle.

Above a threshold voltage (the Fréedericksz transition, V_th = π√(K/ε₀Δε), typically 1–2 V and independent of the cell thickness) the molecules tilt towards the field. The effective birefringence and the twist guiding disappear, the polarisation is no longer rotated and the crossed analyser blocks the light: the pixel turns dark. Colour displays put red, green and blue filters over three sub-pixels, each driven by a thin-film transistor.

Modern panels mostly use other liquid-crystal modes (in-plane switching, vertical alignment) for wider viewing angles, but all work on the same principle: a voltage-controlled retarder between polarisers. The same physics drives spatial light modulators used in holographic displays and optical tweezers.

15. Thin-Film Coatings

A stack of thin layers is analysed with characteristic matrices. Layer j, of index n_j and thickness d_j, has phase thickness and optical admittance

δ_j = 2π n_j d_j cos θ_j / λ, η_j = n_j cos θ_j (s-polarisation), η_j = n_j / cos θ_j (p-polarisation)
Derivation. In each layer the tangential fields are the sum of a forward and a backward wave. The tangential E and H at the two faces of the layer are related by
[E₁; H₁] = [cos δ, (i/η) sin δ; iη sin δ, cos δ] [E₂; H₂].
Because tangential E and H are continuous across every interface, the matrices of all the layers simply multiply. With the substrate (admittance η_s) at the end, [B; C] = M₁M₂…M_N [1; η_s], the stack behaves like a single surface of admittance Y = C/B, and
r = (η₀ − Y)/(η₀ + Y), R = |r|².

A quarter-wave layer (δ = π/2) has the matrix [0, i/η; iη, 0], which turns an admittance Y into η²/Y. This makes quarter-wave designs easy to analyse by hand.

Single-layer anti-reflection. One quarter-wave layer of index n₁ on a substrate n_s gives Y = n₁²/n_s and

R = ((n₀n_s − n₁²)/(n₀n_s + n₁²))², zero when n₁ = √(n₀n_s)

For glass (1.52) the ideal index is 1.23; the nearest durable material, MgF₂ (1.38), leaves 1.3% at the design wavelength, down from 4.3%. A camera lens with 10 air–glass surfaces would lose about 35% of the light without coatings and suffer ghost images. Two-layer V-coats reach zero at one wavelength (laser optics); multi-layer broadband coatings reach below 0.5% across the visible.

Quarter-wave mirrors. Alternating high (H) and low (L) index quarter-wave layers make every reflection add in phase. For N pairs with H outermost on a substrate n_s:

Y = (n_H/n_L)^(2N) n_s, R = ((n₀ − Y)/(n₀ + Y))²

The high-reflection band has a width (in frequency, relative to its centre)

Δg = (4/π) arcsin((n_H − n_L)/(n_H + n_L)), Δλ ≈ λ₀ Δg

16. Photonic Crystals and Structural Colour

An infinite periodic stack is a one-dimensional photonic crystal. By Bloch's theorem its waves have the form u(z)e^(iKz) with u periodic, and the Bloch wave number K follows from the trace of the matrix of one period Λ = d_H + d_L:

cos(KΛ) = cos δ_H cos δ_L − ½(n_H/n_L + n_L/n_H) sin δ_H sin δ_L

Where the right side has magnitude greater than 1, K is complex: waves decay into the crystal instead of propagating. These frequency ranges are photonic band gaps, the analogue of electronic band gaps in semiconductors, and they are exactly the reflection bands of the multilayer mirror. For quarter-wave layers the gaps are centred on λ₀, λ₀/3, λ₀/5, … (odd orders); the simulator's band diagram shows them shaded.

17. Fresnel Equations and Total Internal Reflection

At a plane boundary between non-magnetic media n₁ and n₂, the tangential components of E and H must be continuous. Solving for the reflected and transmitted amplitudes (θ_t from Snell's law n₁ sin θ_i = n₂ sin θ_t) gives the Fresnel equations:

r_s = (n₁ cos θ_i − n₂ cos θ_t)/(n₁ cos θ_i + n₂ cos θ_t), t_s = 2n₁ cos θ_i/(n₁ cos θ_i + n₂ cos θ_t)
r_p = (n₂ cos θ_i − n₁ cos θ_t)/(n₂ cos θ_i + n₁ cos θ_t), t_p = 2n₁ cos θ_i/(n₂ cos θ_i + n₁ cos θ_t)

The reflectances are R = |r|², and T = 1 − R (the transmitted intensity includes the factor n₂ cos θ_t / n₁ cos θ_i from the change in beam width and speed). At normal incidence both reduce to R = ((n₁ − n₂)/(n₁ + n₂))², 4.3% for air–glass.

Brewster's angle. r_p = 0 when θ_i + θ_t = 90°, i.e. tan θ_B = n₂/n₁ (56.3° for air–glass, 53.1° for air–water). The reflected light is then pure s-polarised: polarising sunglasses with a vertical transmission axis cut glare from water and roads, and laser windows set at Brewster's angle transmit p-light without loss.

Total internal reflection. From dense to rare (n₁ > n₂), beyond the critical angle sin θ_c = n₂/n₁ there is no real refracted angle: R = 1 for both polarisations. Each polarisation acquires a phase on reflection (with n = n₂/n₁):

tan(φ_s/2) = √(sin²θ − n²)/cos θ, tan(φ_p/2) = √(sin²θ − n²)/(n² cos θ)

The difference φ_p − φ_s turns linear into elliptical light. Fresnel used two total reflections at 54.6° in glass (45° each) to make a perfectly achromatic quarter-wave retarder, the Fresnel rhomb. Total internal reflection guides light in optical fibres, folds light in binocular prisms and is responsible for the sparkle of diamond (n = 2.42, θ_c = 24.4°).

18. Evanescent Waves and Tunnelling

Under total internal reflection the field does not stop abruptly at the boundary. Matching the tangential wave vector k_x = (2π/λ) n₁ sin θ in the second medium requires k_z² = (2π/λ)²(n₂² − n₁² sin²θ) < 0, so k_z = iκ and the field decays as e^(−κz) without carrying energy away. The amplitude falls to 1/e in the penetration depth

d_p = 1/κ = λ / (2π √(n₁² sin²θ − n₂²))

typically 100–300 nm for visible light. The wave crests in the evanescent field run along the surface (the simulator shows them), and the reflected beam is shifted slightly sideways (the Goos–Hänchen shift, about a wavelength).

Frustrated total internal reflection. If a second dense medium comes within a few penetration depths, the evanescent field becomes a propagating wave again on the far side: light tunnels across the gap, with transmission falling roughly as e^(−2κd) for wide gaps. This is the optical twin of quantum tunnelling through a barrier, with the gap playing the role of the classically forbidden region. Variable-gap prisms make adjustable beam splitters; fingerprint scanners image where skin ridges touch the glass and frustrate the reflection.

TIRF microscopy uses evanescent illumination about 100 nm deep to excite fluorescence only near the coverslip, imaging single molecules at a cell membrane without background from the rest of the cell. Prism and grating couplers use evanescent coupling to launch light into thin-film waveguides, and evanescent-field sensors detect changes in the medium touching a fibre or waveguide.

Worked example: penetration depth for 633 nm light in glass (1.52) hitting air at 60°.
√(1.52² × sin²60° − 1) = √(1.733 − 1) = 0.856; d_p = 633 nm/(2π × 0.856) = 118 nm. The intensity falls to 1/e in half that, 59 nm.

19. Surface Plasmons

In a metal the free electrons respond to light with the Drude permittivity

ε_m(ω) = 1 − ω_p²/(ω² + iγω), ω_p² = n_e e²/(ε₀ m)

Below the plasma frequency (ultraviolet for gold and silver) ε_m is negative, which is why metals reflect. A boundary between a metal and a dielectric (ε_d) supports a wave bound to the surface, the surface plasmon polariton: a ripple of electron density coupled to an evanescent field on both sides. Solving Maxwell's equations for a p-polarised wave decaying away from the interface on both sides gives its wave vector

k_sp = (ω/c) √(ε_m ε_d / (ε_m + ε_d))
Derivation sketch. Take H_y ∝ e^(ik_x x) e^(−κ_d z) in the dielectric (z > 0) and e^(ik_x x) e^(κ_m z) in the metal (z < 0). Continuity of H_y and of E_x ∝ (1/ε)∂H_y/∂z requires κ_d/ε_d = −κ_m/ε_m. Since both κ are positive, this needs ε_m < 0. Combining with κ² = k_x² − εω²/c² in each medium gives the formula above. There is no s-polarised solution: only p-light can excite plasmons.

k_sp is larger than the wave vector of light in the dielectric, so a plasmon cannot be excited by light coming through air or water. In the Kretschmann configuration light arrives through a glass prism (index n_p) onto a thin metal film (≈ 50 nm of gold); its parallel wave vector (ω/c) n_p sin θ matches Re k_sp at one angle, and the evanescent field tunnels through the film and excites the plasmon on the far side. Energy is absorbed and the p-reflectance shows a deep, narrow dip:

n_p sin θ_sp = Re √(ε_m ε_d / (ε_m + ε_d))

The dip angle depends on the refractive index within about 200 nm of the gold surface. When molecules bind to antibodies fixed on the gold, the local index rises by about 10⁻³ and the dip moves by about 0.1–0.2°. SPR biosensors follow this shift in real time without labels, measuring binding rates and affinities of drugs, antibodies and proteins; resolutions of 10⁻⁶ refractive-index units are routine. Silver gives a sharper resonance (smaller Im ε) but tarnishes; gold is chemically stable.

Localised plasmons. In metal nanoparticles much smaller than the wavelength the electrons oscillate as a whole; resonance occurs when ε_m = −2ε_d (the Fröhlich condition), at about 520 nm for gold spheres in water. This absorption makes colloidal gold red and gives the Roman Lycurgus cup and medieval stained glass their colours; today it is used in lateral-flow tests (the red line of a pregnancy or COVID-19 test is gold nanoparticles), in surface-enhanced Raman scattering and in photothermal therapy.

Worked example: gold (ε_m = −11.5 + 1.4i) in water (ε_d = 1.777) at 633 nm, BK7 prism (1.515). Where is the dip?
ε_mε_d/(ε_m + ε_d) ≈ (−20.4)/(−9.72) = 2.10, √ = 1.450. sin θ = 1.450/1.515 = 0.957 → θ ≈ 73°. The full three-layer calculation in the simulator, which includes the film thickness, gives 72.6°.

20. Negative-Index Metamaterials

In 1968 Veselago asked what would happen if both ε and μ were negative. Maxwell's equations then still allow propagating waves, but E, H and k form a left-handed set: the wave vector (and the phase velocity) points opposite to the energy flow. The refractive index must be taken as negative, n = −√(εμ), and Snell's law n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ puts the refracted ray on the same side of the normal as the incident ray.

The impedance Z = √(μ/ε) determines reflection: a medium with ε = μ = −1 has the same impedance as vacuum, so it does not reflect at any angle. Pendry showed in 2000 that a flat slab of n = −1 focuses light from a point source twice, once inside and once beyond, and in principle also restores the evanescent waves that carry detail finer than λ/2: a perfect lens not limited by diffraction.

No natural material has μ < 0 at optical frequencies, so negative index requires metamaterials: arrays of structures much smaller than the wavelength that act as artificial atoms. Split-ring resonators provide negative μ near their resonance, and thin wires provide negative ε. Negative refraction was demonstrated at microwave frequencies in 2001 (Shelby, Smith and Schultz) and later in the near-infrared with "fishnet" metal–dielectric stacks. Absorption in the metals limits optical metamaterials; the practical successes are related ideas such as the silver superlens (2005, about 60 nm resolution with negative ε alone in the near field), microwave cloaks, flat metalenses made of nanopillars that control the phase locally, and hyperbolic media.

22. Practice Problems

1. A Michelson with a 633 nm laser shows 250 fringes passing. How far did the mirror move?
d = Nλ/2 = 250 × 316.4 nm = 79.1 µm.
2. An 850 nm LED has a 40 nm bandwidth. What is its coherence length, and why is that useful in OCT?
l_c = λ²/Δλ = (850)²/40 nm = 18 µm. Interference occurs only when the reference and sample paths match within ≈ 18 µm, so each reflection is located in depth to that precision.
3. A Michelson is lit by the mercury green line (Δλ ≈ 0.02 nm). Over what mirror travel do the fringes stay clearly visible?
l_c = (546)²/0.02 nm = 15 mm of path difference, i.e. about 7.5 mm of mirror travel.
4. A Fabry–Pérot has R = 0.95 and g = 2 mm. Find ℱ, the FSR at 633 nm in GHz and in pm, and the resolvable Δλ.
ℱ = π√0.95/0.05 = 61.2. FSR = c/2g = 75 GHz = λ²/2g = 100 pm. δλ = 100/61.2 = 1.6 pm.
5. An FTIR spectrometer must resolve lines 0.5 cm⁻¹ apart. What maximum path difference is needed (boxcar)?
x_max ≈ 0.6/δσ = 0.6/0.5 cm⁻¹ = 1.2 cm of path difference, i.e. 6 mm of mirror travel.
6. How thick is a zero-order quartz quarter-wave plate for 589 nm (Δn = 0.009)?
t = λ/(4Δn) = 589 nm/0.036 = 16.4 µm.
7. Light polarised at 30° passes a half-wave plate with its fast axis at 0°. What is the output?
A half-wave plate reflects the polarisation about its axis: 2 × 0° − 30° = −30°, still linear.
8. Right-circular light passes a polariser. What fraction is transmitted, and does it depend on the polariser angle?
The pole is 90° from every point on the equator, so T = cos²(45°) = 50% for any angle.
9. Design a single-layer AR coating for a lens of n = 1.75 at 550 nm.
Ideal index √1.75 = 1.32, close to MgF₂ (1.38). Thickness λ/(4n) = 550/(4 × 1.38) = 99.6 nm. R = ((1.75 − 1.9044)/(1.75 + 1.9044))² = 0.18%, much better than on low-index glass.
10. Find the Brewster angle for light reflected from water, and the critical angle for light trying to leave water.
θ_B = arctan 1.33 = 53.1°; θ_c = arcsin(1/1.33) = 48.8°.
11. A diver looks up at the surface. Through what cone does she see the outside world?
All the sky is compressed into a cone of half-angle θ_c = 48.8° (Snell's window); outside it she sees the reflection of the underwater scene by total internal reflection.
12. How far into the air does the evanescent field extend (1/e of intensity) for TIR in glass (1.5) at 45°, 633 nm?
√(2.25 × 0.5 − 1) = 0.354, d_p = 633/(2π × 0.354) = 285 nm for the amplitude, 142 nm for the intensity.
13. A Morpho scale is a 5-pair chitin/air stack tuned to 460 nm. Estimate the width of the reflection band.
Δg = (4/π) arcsin(0.56/2.56) = 0.281, so Δλ ≈ 0.28 × 460 nm ≈ 130 nm: a broad, bright blue.
14. The SPR dip of a sensor moves by 0.10° when a protein layer binds. With a sensitivity of 180°/RIU, what index change is that?
Δn = 0.10/180 = 5.6 × 10⁻⁴ refractive-index units.

24. Limits of the Models in This Module

Interferometers: ideal 50 : 50 beam splitters and perfect mirrors; ring patterns are calculated for a lens of fixed angular field; spectral lines are Gaussian; the Fabry–Pérot is lossless and the source's line width is not convolved with the Airy function. FT spectroscopy: noiseless, perfectly sampled one-sided interferogram, no phase correction. Polarisation: ideal elements, no reflection losses; retardance does not depend on wavelength. Crystals: calcite indices at 589 nm without dispersion; the stressed disc uses the two-dimensional elastic solution with point loads and a wavelength-independent stress-optic coefficient; the LCD uses a simple tilt-versus-voltage model and the Gooch–Tarry formula, which strictly applies only without tilt. Multilayers: lossless, non-dispersive layers with exact thicknesses; incoherent back-surface reflection of the substrate is ignored. Boundary: plane waves; metal optical constants are interpolated from tabulated values; the negative-index medium is lossless with ε = n and μ = −1 when n ≠ −1 (impedance mismatch causes some reflection).

The left panel is a snapshot of the wave field (animated), the right panel the reflectance against angle. Beyond the critical angle the transmitted wave becomes evanescent; bring a second prism within a wavelength and it tunnels through. In the Kretschmann geometry a dip in p-reflectance marks the surface plasmon; change the sample index and watch it move. With a negative index the refracted ray bends to the same side of the normal.
