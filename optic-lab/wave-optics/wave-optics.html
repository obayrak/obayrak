Wave Optics — Interference, Diffraction, Thin Films and Polarisation

Ray optics treats light as straight lines that bend at surfaces. It works when everything is much larger than the wavelength (λ ≈ 0.4–0.7 µm). When light meets slits, small apertures, thin layers or polarising materials, its wave nature decides what we see: fringes, spectra, the size of a star's image, the colours of a soap bubble and the darkness of crossed polarisers. This chapter builds the theory from the wave equation to real instruments, with derivations, worked examples and practice problems.

1. Light as an Electromagnetic Wave

Maxwell (1865) showed that light is a transverse electromagnetic wave. A plane wave travelling along x is

E(x, t) = E₀ cos(kx − ωt + φ),   k = 2π/λ,  ω = 2πf,  c = fλ

The electric field E oscillates perpendicular to the direction of travel, with the magnetic field B = E/c perpendicular to both. In a medium of refractive index n the speed is c/n and the wavelength shortens to λ/n, while the frequency (and the colour) stays the same.

Intensity. Detectors (eye, film, camera) are far too slow to follow the field (f ≈ 5×10¹⁴ Hz); they measure the time-averaged power per area, I = ½ c ε₀ n E₀², so intensity ∝ (amplitude)². Every interference calculation therefore adds fields first and squares afterwards.

Optical path length. A path of length L in a medium n has OPL = nL and produces a phase φ = 2π·OPL/λ. Phase differences, not distances, decide interference: δ = 2π Δ(OPL)/λ.

Phase change on reflection. At normal incidence the reflected amplitude is r = (n₁ − n₂)/(n₁ + n₂). When light reflects from a medium of higher index (n₂ > n₁), r is negative: a phase shift of π (half a wavelength). From a lower-index medium there is no shift. This single rule explains the black top of a soap film and the dark centre of Newton's rings.

A 1 mW laser pointer makes a 1 mm² spot. What is the field amplitude?
I = 10⁻³ W / 10⁻⁶ m² = 1000 W/m². E₀ = √(2I/(cε₀)) = √(2000/(3×10⁸ × 8.85×10⁻¹²)) ≈ 870 V/m.
Green light (532 nm in air) enters glass (n = 1.5). Wavelength, speed and frequency inside?
λ = 532/1.5 = 355 nm; v = c/1.5 = 2.0×10⁸ m/s; f = c/λ_air = 5.64×10¹⁴ Hz, unchanged. The light is still green: colour is set by frequency.

2. Superposition and Interference

Maxwell's equations are linear, so fields from several sources simply add. For two waves of the same frequency arriving with phase difference δ:

Derivation. E = E₁cos(ωt) + E₂cos(ωt + δ). Time-averaging E² gives
⟨E²⟩ = ½E₁² + ½E₂² + E₁E₂⟨2cos(ωt)cos(ωt + δ)⟩ = ½E₁² + ½E₂² + E₁E₂ cos δ.
Multiplying by cε₀: I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂) cos δ. The last term is the interference term.

Constructive interference: δ = 2πm, path difference = mλ, I = (√I₁ + √I₂)². Destructive: δ = (2m + 1)π, path difference (m + ½)λ, I = (√I₁ − √I₂)². For equal beams I = 4I₁ cos²(δ/2): between 0 and four times one beam. Energy is not destroyed; it is redistributed from dark to bright fringes (the average is still 2I₁).

Fringe visibility V = (I_max − I_min)/(I_max + I_min) = 2√(I₁I₂)/(I₁ + I₂): 1 for equal beams, small if one beam is much weaker or if the light is partially incoherent.

Phasors. Represent each wave as an arrow of length E and angle φ; the resultant is the vector sum. Phasors make the N-slit and single-slit results almost obvious (sections 7 and 8).

Two beams have intensities I and 4I. Find I_max, I_min and the visibility.
I_max = (1 + 2)²I = 9I, I_min = (2 − 1)²I = I. V = (9 − 1)/(9 + 1) = 0.8.

3. Coherence: When Can Light Interfere?

Two independent lamps never produce fringes: their phases wander randomly in about 10⁻¹⁴–10⁻⁹ s and the interference term averages to zero. Interference needs light split from the same source, with path differences smaller than the coherence length.

Temporal coherence depends on the spread of wavelengths Δλ: l_c ≈ λ²/Δλ, coherence time τ_c = l_c/c. Fringes survive only for path differences below l_c, i.e. about l_c/λ = λ/Δλ fringes.

Spatial coherence depends on the angular size of the source: two slits a distance d apart are illuminated coherently if d < λ/α, where α is the angle the source subtends. Young used a pinhole in front of the sunlit window to make the source small. Michelson used exactly this idea (1920) to measure the diameter of the star Betelgeuse.

Why does a soap film 10 µm thick show no colours in sunlight?
The path difference 2nt ≈ 27 µm is far beyond the 1 µm coherence length of white light: reflections from the two surfaces add incoherently and the film looks white. Only films below about 1 µm are coloured (try the "Thick film" preset).
The Sun subtends 0.53°. How close must two slits be to show fringes in direct sunlight at 550 nm?
α = 0.53° = 9.25×10⁻³ rad; d < λ/α = 0.55 µm/9.25×10⁻³ ≈ 60 µm. Ordinary slits (0.25 mm) need a pinhole to shrink the source.

4. Young's Double-Slit Experiment

Derivation. Two slits a distance d apart are lit by the same wave. At a distant point at angle θ the path difference is Δ = d sin θ.
Bright fringes: d sin θ = mλ; dark fringes: d sin θ = (m + ½)λ, m = 0, ±1, ±2 …
On a screen at distance L ≫ d: y = L tan θ ≈ Lθ, so y_m = mλL/d and the fringe spacing is Δy = λL/d.
With δ = 2πd sin θ/λ: I(θ) = 4I₁ cos²(πd sin θ/λ).
Real slits have width a; each slit gives the single-slit pattern (section 7), so I = I₀ cos²(πd sin θ/λ) · [sin β/β]², β = πa sin θ/λ.

What changes the fringes. Δy grows with λ and L and shrinks with d. In a medium of index n, λ becomes λ/n and the fringes move closer. A thin transparent plate (index n, thickness t) over one slit adds an optical path (n − 1)t and shifts the whole pattern by (n − 1)t/λ fringes, without changing the spacing.

Missing orders. When a bright fringe falls on a zero of the single-slit envelope (d/a an integer) it disappears. Inside the central envelope there are 2(d/a) − 1 bright fringes.

White light. The zero-order fringe is white for all colours (Δ = 0); higher orders separate (blue inside, red outside) and wash out after about λ/Δλ ≈ 2 orders: the coherence length again.

One photon at a time. G. I. Taylor (1909) recorded the fringes with light so dim that only one photon was in the apparatus at a time; Tonomura (1989) did the same with single electrons. Each particle lands at one point, but the probability of landing is given by the wave interference pattern. Asking "which slit?" (covering one slit or detecting the path) destroys the fringes. Feynman called this "the only mystery" of quantum mechanics.

Slits 0.25 mm apart, He–Ne laser (633 nm), screen 1.0 m away. Fringe spacing?
Δy = λL/d = 633×10⁻⁹ × 1.0 / 0.25×10⁻³ = 2.53 mm.
d = 0.20 mm, a = 0.05 mm. Which orders are missing and how many bright fringes are inside the central envelope?
d/a = 4: orders ±4, ±8 … are missing. Inside the central envelope (|m| < 4) there are 2×3 + 1 = 7 bright fringes.
The same apparatus is put under water (n = 1.33). New fringe spacing?
λ_water = 633/1.33 = 476 nm, so Δy = 2.53/1.33 = 1.90 mm.
A mica sheet (n = 1.58, t = 5 µm) covers one slit (λ = 550 nm). By how many fringes does the pattern shift?
Extra path (n − 1)t = 0.58 × 5 µm = 2.9 µm = 5.27 λ: the pattern moves by 5.3 fringes towards the covered slit. This is how Young-type interferometers measure tiny thicknesses and indices.

5. Other Interferometers

Lloyd's mirror (1834): light from a slit interferes with its own reflection from a glass plate at grazing incidence. The fringe at the mirror surface, where the path difference is zero, is dark: direct proof of the π phase shift on reflection. Fresnel's biprism (1819) makes two virtual slits by refraction; Newton's rings and wedges are treated in section 10.

Michelson interferometer (1881). A beam splitter sends light along two perpendicular arms to mirrors and recombines it. Moving one mirror by d changes the path by 2d; each fringe that passes a point corresponds to Δd = λ/2, so 2Δd = Nλ. With tilted mirrors you see straight fringes, with spherical waves circular ones, 2d cos θ = mλ.

Uses: Michelson and Morley (1887) found no change in the speed of light with the Earth's motion, a key step towards relativity. The metre was defined from interferometry with cadmium and krypton lines (1960–1983). Fourier-transform spectrometers record the fringe signal while scanning the mirror. LIGO (4 km arms, light bouncing hundreds of times) detected gravitational waves in 2015 by measuring mirror motions of about 10⁻¹⁸ m, a thousandth of a proton diameter.

While a Michelson mirror moves, 200 bright fringes pass the detector (He–Ne, 633 nm). How far did the mirror move?
Δd = Nλ/2 = 200 × 633 nm / 2 = 63.3 µm.
A 2.0 cm gas cell in one arm is slowly evacuated and 22 fringes pass (633 nm). What is n − 1 for the gas?
The optical path changes by 2(n − 1)L = Nλ → n − 1 = 22 × 633 nm / (2 × 0.020 m) = 3.5×10⁻⁴ (about that of air at room conditions; the light crosses the cell twice).

6. Diffraction: the Huygens–Fresnel Principle

Huygens (1678): every point of a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets; the new wavefront is their envelope. Fresnel (1818) added that the wavelets interfere. Diffraction is therefore not a separate phenomenon: it is the interference of infinitely many wavelets from one aperture.

Fresnel or Fraunhofer? The Fresnel number F = a²/(Lλ) (a = half-width of the aperture) decides. For F ≳ 1 (near field) the pattern changes with distance and resembles the aperture with fringed edges (Fresnel diffraction). For F ≪ 1 (far field, or in the focal plane of a lens) the shape is fixed and only scales with distance: Fraunhofer diffraction, which this module simulates.

Fraunhofer integral. The far-field amplitude at angle θ is the sum of wavelets across the aperture with transmission A(x):
E(θ) ∝ ∫ A(x) exp(−i k x sin θ) dx,
i.e. the Fourier transform of the aperture. Narrow features give wide patterns and vice versa: Δx · Δ(sin θ) ≈ λ. With p = h/λ this is the Heisenberg uncertainty relation Δx Δp_x ≈ h.

Babinet's principle: an opaque object and a hole of the same shape give the same Fraunhofer pattern (except at the very centre). A hair and a slit of the same width diffract identically, so a laser pointer can measure a hair's thickness. The Poisson–Arago spot (1818): Poisson tried to ridicule Fresnel's theory by deducing a bright spot at the centre of a disc's shadow; Arago looked, and found it.

Is a 0.1 mm slit lit by a He–Ne laser, with the screen 1 m away, in the Fraunhofer regime?
F = (0.05 mm)²/(1 m × 633 nm) = 0.004 ≪ 1: yes, far field.
A hair in a 650 nm laser beam gives dark fringes 13 mm apart on a wall 2 m away. Hair thickness?
By Babinet the hair acts like a slit: a = λL/Δy = 650 nm × 2 m / 13 mm = 100 µm.

7. Single-Slit Diffraction

Zone argument. Divide a slit of width a into two halves. Each wavelet in the top half has a partner a/2 lower. If (a/2) sin θ = λ/2 every pair cancels → first dark fringe at a sin θ = λ. Dividing into 4, 6 … zones gives all minima: a sin θ = mλ, m = ±1, ±2 … (m ≠ 0).
Integral. With A(x) = 1 for |x| < a/2: E(θ) ∝ ∫ exp(−ikx sin θ)dx = a · sin β/β, β = πa sin θ/λ, so
I(θ) = I₀ (sin β / β)².
Phasors. The wavelets form an arc of total length E₀ bent through angle 2β; the chord is E₀ sin β/β.

Features. The central maximum spans −λ/a to +λ/a: it is twice as wide as the others (width on a screen 2λL/a). Side maxima lie near β = 1.43π, 2.46π, 3.47π with intensities 4.7 %, 1.7 % and 0.8 % of the centre. A narrower slit spreads light more: halving a doubles the pattern. When a ≤ λ the central lobe fills the whole half-space.

A 0.10 mm slit, He–Ne light (633 nm), screen 1.0 m away. Width of the central maximum?
θ₁ = λ/a = 6.33 mrad; width = 2Lθ₁ = 12.7 mm (preset "Single slit 0.1 mm").
The second dark fringes of a slit pattern are 18 mm apart on a screen 1.5 m away (λ = 589 nm). Slit width?
y₂ = 9 mm = 2λL/a → a = 2 × 589 nm × 1.5 m / 9 mm = 0.196 mm.

8. Multiple Slits and Diffraction Gratings

N slits. Adding N equal phasors, each turned by δ = 2πd sin θ/λ relative to the last, gives a regular polygon:
E_N = E₁ sin(Nα)/sin α, α = πd sin θ/λ. Including the slit width:
I(θ) = I₀ (sin β/β)² · [sin(Nα)/(N sin α)]² (normalised to 1 at θ = 0).
• Principal maxima where sin α = 0: d sin θ = mλ (the grating equation), intensity ∝ N².
• N − 1 zeros and N − 2 weak subsidiary maxima between neighbouring orders.
• Angular half-width of a principal maximum: Δθ = λ/(N d cos θ): peaks get N times sharper.

Resolving power. Two wavelengths λ and λ + Δλ are just resolved (Rayleigh) when the peak of one falls on the first zero of the other. In order m this gives

R = λ/Δλ = mN   (N = number of illuminated lines)

Other key quantities: angular dispersion dθ/dλ = m/(d cos θ); highest order m_max = ⌊d/λ⌋; oblique incidence d(sin θ_m − sin θ_i) = mλ; free spectral range Δλ_FSR = λ/m (orders overlap beyond it). Reflection gratings work the same way; blazed gratings tilt each groove so most light goes into one chosen order. Atoms in a crystal form a 3D grating for X-rays: Bragg's law 2d sin θ = mλ (1913) opened X-ray crystallography (DNA, 1953).

Spectroscopy. Each element emits a fingerprint of lines (sodium 589.0/589.6 nm, hydrogen Balmer 656, 486, 434, 410 nm). A grating measures them absolutely from d and θ. Fraunhofer (1821) built the first wire gratings and found the dark lines in sunlight; Rowland's ruled gratings (1882) made precision spectroscopy possible.

Can a 600 lines/mm grating, 1 mm illuminated, separate the sodium D lines (589.0 and 589.6 nm) in first order?
Needed: R = 589.3/0.6 ≈ 982. Available: R = mN = 1 × 600 = 600 < 982, so not resolved. Illuminate 2 mm (N = 1200) or use second order (R = 1200). The simulator's 3 mm preset (R = 1800) shows two clear lines.
Green light (532 nm) on a 600 lines/mm grating. First-order angle and highest order?
d = 1/600 mm = 1.667 µm. sin θ₁ = 0.532/1.667 = 0.319 → θ₁ = 18.6°. m_max = ⌊1.667/0.532⌋ = 3.
Hydrogen light on the same grating: where are the Balmer lines in first order?
sin θ = λ/d: Hα 656.3 nm → 23.2°, Hβ 486.1 nm → 17.0°, Hγ 434.0 nm → 15.1°, Hδ 410.2 nm → 14.3°.
White light on this grating: do the first and second orders overlap? Second and third?
First order ends at 700 nm, second starts at 2 × 400 = 800 nm equivalent: no overlap. Second-order red (2 × 700 = 1400 nm) lands where third-order 467 nm does: second and third orders overlap.
A CD has tracks 1.6 µm apart. At what angle is first-order green (550 nm) reflected under normal illumination? A DVD (0.74 µm)?
CD: sin θ = 0.55/1.6 → 20.1°. DVD: sin θ = 0.55/0.74 → 48.0°: the DVD spreads colours much more widely.

9. Circular Apertures, the Airy Disc and the Limit of Resolution

Airy pattern (Airy, 1835). For a circular aperture of diameter D the Fraunhofer integral over the disc gives
I(θ) = I₀ [2J₁(x)/x]², x = πD sin θ/λ, with J₁ the Bessel function of order 1.
Dark rings at sin θ = 1.22λ/D, 2.23λ/D, 3.24λ/D. The central Airy disc holds 83.8 % of the light; the first ring 7.2 %, the second 2.8 %.

Rayleigh criterion (1879): two incoherent point sources are just resolved when the centre of one Airy disc falls on the first dark ring of the other:

θ_R = 1.22 λ/D

The summed intensity then dips by about 26 % between the peaks. Other criteria: Sparrow (dip just vanishes, ≈ 0.95 θ_R) and the empirical Dawes limit for double stars, 116″/D(mm).

Why the Optical Instruments module has limits. Every lens, mirror and pupil is a circular aperture, so even a perfect, aberration-free instrument images a point as an Airy disc:

Microscopes. For a microscope the relevant quantity is the numerical aperture NA = n sin α (α = half-angle of the cone collected). Abbe (1873) showed that detail of period d is imaged only if d ≥ λ/(2NA); the Rayleigh form is d = 0.61λ/NA. Hence oil immersion (n = 1.52 raises NA to ≈ 1.4) and blue or UV light. The useful magnification is about 500–1000 × NA; beyond that the image only gets bigger, not sharper ("empty magnification"). Super-resolution methods (STED, PALM; Nobel Prize 2014) beat the limit with fluorescent molecules switched on and off, not with better lenses.

Beating diffraction with size. Radio telescopes combine dishes thousands of km apart; the Event Horizon Telescope (2019) imaged the black hole in M87 with an Earth-sized aperture.

Rayleigh limit of the eye (3 mm pupil, 550 nm). How far apart must two dots at 25 cm be?
θ_R = 1.22 × 550 nm / 3 mm = 2.24×10⁻⁴ rad = 46″. At 25 cm: 0.25 m × 2.24×10⁻⁴ = 56 µm. On the retina (17 mm) the Airy disc radius is 3.8 µm, about the spacing of the cones: nature matched the sensor to the optics.
Can the Hubble Space Telescope (D = 2.4 m) see the Apollo landers on the Moon (384 000 km)?
θ_R = 1.22 × 550 nm / 2.4 m = 2.8×10⁻⁷ rad. At the Moon: 384×10⁶ m × 2.8×10⁻⁷ = 107 m. The 4 m landers are far below the limit. A telescope would need D ≈ 65 m.
An oil-immersion objective (NA 1.4) is used with blue light (450 nm). Smallest resolvable detail?
Rayleigh: d = 0.61 × 450 nm / 1.4 = 196 nm. Abbe: λ/(2NA) = 161 nm. Bacteria (1 µm) are easy; viruses (100 nm) are not.
Two car headlights 1.4 m apart: from how far can the eye still separate them at night (pupil 7 mm, 550 nm)?
θ_R = 1.22 × 550 nm / 7 mm = 9.6×10⁻⁵ rad. Distance = 1.4 m / 9.6×10⁻⁵ ≈ 15 km (in practice less, because of atmospheric turbulence and aberrations of the wide-open eye).

10. Thin Films: Soap Bubbles, Oil Slicks, Newton's Rings

Derivation. Light reflects from the top and bottom of a film (index n₁, thickness t). Inside the film the ray travels at angle θ₁ (Snell: sin θ₀ = n₁ sin θ₁). The optical path difference between the two reflected beams is
Δ = 2 n₁ t cos θ₁,
plus λ/2 for each reflection from a higher-index medium.
• One π shift (soap film in air, oil on water, n₁ > both neighbours): bright reflection at 2n₁t cos θ₁ = (m + ½)λ, dark at 2n₁t cos θ₁ = mλ.
• Zero or two shifts (coating with n_air < n₁ < n_glass): bright at 2n₁t cos θ₁ = mλ, dark at (m + ½)λ.

Colours. White light contains all λ; a given thickness reflects some strongly and others weakly, so the film looks coloured (the simulator computes the colour from the full spectrum with the CIE eye response). As t increases the colours run through Newton's colour sequence: black, grey-white, yellow, red, purple, blue, green, yellow, red … ; above about 1 µm several orders overlap and the film becomes white again. Tilting a film shortens 2n₁t cos θ₁ and shifts colours towards blue.

The black film. As a soap film drains it thins from the top. When t ≪ λ the path difference vanishes but the π shift remains: the two reflections cancel and the top looks black (Newton's black film, 5–30 nm) just before it bursts.

Newton's rings. A lens of radius R on a flat plate leaves an air gap t ≈ r²/(2R). In reflection the centre is dark (one π shift) and the dark rings have radii r_m = √(mλR). Opticians use them to test lens surfaces; Newton described them in 1704 without accepting the wave theory.

Air wedge. Two flat plates touching at one edge and separated by a spacer at angle α give straight fringes spaced λ/(2α); each fringe is a contour line of height λ/2, used to test flatness to a fraction of a micrometre.

A soap film (n = 1.33) reflects strongly at 600 nm in first order. Thickness? Next orders?
2nt = ½λ → t = λ/(4n) = 600/(4 × 1.33) = 113 nm. The next orders: 2nt = 3λ/2 → 338 nm, 5λ/2 → 564 nm.
Oil (n = 1.47) 300 nm thick floats on water (n = 1.33). Which visible wavelength reflects most strongly?
One π shift (air/oil only, since n_water < n_oil). 2nt = 882 nm = (m + ½)λ → λ = 1764, 588, 353 nm … Only 588 nm is visible: the slick looks yellow-orange.
Newton's rings with sodium light (589 nm) and a lens of R = 1.0 m. Radius of the 10th dark ring?
r₁₀ = √(10 × 589 nm × 1.0 m) = 2.43 mm.
Two glass plates 10 cm long are separated at one end by a 0.05 mm paper strip. Fringe spacing at 550 nm?
α = 0.05 mm / 100 mm = 5×10⁻⁴ rad. Spacing = λ/(2α) = 550 nm / 10⁻³ = 0.55 mm (about 180 fringes along the plates).

11. Multilayer Coatings and the Fabry–Pérot Interferometer

Anti-reflection (AR) coating. Uncoated glass reflects R = ((n − 1)/(n + 1))² = 4.3 % per surface (n = 1.52). A layer of optical thickness λ/4 makes the two reflections leave half a wavelength apart (both with a π shift), so they cancel. For a quarter-wave layer the exact result is

R = ((n₀n₂ − n₁²)/(n₀n₂ + n₁²))²,   zero when n₁ = √(n₀n₂)

For glass the ideal index is √1.52 = 1.23; the practical material MgF₂ (n = 1.38) gives 1.26 % at the design wavelength. Because the cancellation is exact for only one λ, the residual reflection of red and blue gives coated lenses their purple tint. Multi-layer "broadband" coatings bring R below 0.3 % across the visible spectrum; a camera lens with 15 surfaces would lose half its light and flare badly without them.

Dielectric mirrors. Stacks of alternating high/low-index quarter-wave layers (e.g. TiO₂ n = 2.4 / SiO₂ n = 1.46) make all reflections add in phase: R = ((n₀n_L^{2N} − n_s n_H^{2N})/(n₀n_L^{2N} + n_s n_H^{2N}))². Four pairs give 95 %, eight pairs 99.9 %: better than any metal, used in laser cavities. The same structure makes the iridescent colours of morpho butterflies, peacock feathers, beetles and opals (structural colour).

Fabry–Pérot interferometer (1899). Two parallel mirrors of reflectance R make many beams interfere. The transmission is the Airy function

T = 1 / (1 + F sin²(δ/2)),   F = 4R/(1 − R)²,   δ = 4πnd cos θ/λ

Transmission peaks are very sharp; their sharpness is the finesse ℱ = π√R/(1 − R), and the resolving power is mℱ. Laser cavities, wavelength-division filters in fibre networks and astronomical spectrographs are all Fabry–Pérots.

Reflectance of a quarter-wave MgF₂ (n = 1.38) coating on glass (n = 1.52) at its design wavelength?
R = ((1 × 1.52 − 1.38²)/(1.52 + 1.38²))² = ((1.52 − 1.904)/(3.424))² = 0.0126 = 1.26 % (the simulator's AR preset gives the same).
How thick is the MgF₂ layer for 550 nm?
t = λ/(4n) = 550/(4 × 1.38) = 99.6 nm.
Finesse of a Fabry–Pérot with R = 0.90?
ℱ = π√0.9/(1 − 0.9) = 29.8: the transmission peaks are about 30 times narrower than their spacing.

12. Polarisation and Malus's Law

Because light is transverse, the direction of E carries information: the polarisation. Writing E = (Eₓ, E_y) with complex amplitudes gives the Jones vector:

How light becomes polarised: (1) dichroism: long molecules in a Polaroid sheet (Land, 1938) absorb E along their length; (2) reflection, complete at Brewster's angle (section 13); (3) scattering: light scattered at 90° by air molecules is strongly polarised, so the blue sky is polarised (bees and possibly Viking navigators used this); (4) birefringence, which splits light into two polarisations (section 14).

Malus's law (1809). An ideal polariser passes only the field component along its axis. Polarised light at angle θ to the axis has transmitted amplitude E₀ cos θ, so
I = I₀ cos² θ.
Unpolarised light: averaging cos² over all angles gives I = I₀/2.
Jones matrix of a polariser at angle θ: P = [[cos²θ, cos θ sin θ], [cos θ sin θ, sin²θ]].

Three-polariser surprise. Crossed polarisers transmit nothing. Insert a third at 45° between them and ½ × cos²45° × cos²45° = 1/8 of the light passes: a polariser does not just block, it projects the field onto a new direction.

Unpolarised light passes polarisers at 0°, 30° and 90°. What fraction gets through?
½ × cos²30° × cos²60° = 0.5 × 0.75 × 0.25 = 0.094 (9.4 %).
Polarised light must be rotated by 90° with as little loss as possible using N polarisers at equal steps. Transmission for N = 2, 6, ∞?
T = cos²ᴺ(90°/N): N = 2 → cos⁴45° = 25 %; N = 6 → cos¹²15° = 66 %; N → ∞ → 100 %. (A half-wave plate does it in one step without loss.)

13. Fresnel Equations and Brewster's Angle

Matching E and B at a boundary (Fresnel, 1823) gives separate reflection coefficients for the s component (E perpendicular to the plane of incidence) and the p component (E in that plane):

r_s = (n₁cos θᵢ − n₂cos θ_t)/(n₁cos θᵢ + n₂cos θ_t),   r_p = (n₂cos θᵢ − n₁cos θ_t)/(n₂cos θᵢ + n₁cos θ_t),   R = r²

At normal incidence both give R = ((n₁ − n₂)/(n₁ + n₂))²; at grazing incidence both go to 100 %. In between, R_p drops to zero.

Brewster's angle (1815). r_p = 0 when the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular, θᵢ + θ_t = 90°: the oscillating dipoles in the second medium then point along the reflected direction and cannot radiate that way. With Snell's law, n₁ sin θ_B = n₂ sin(90° − θ_B) = n₂ cos θ_B, so
tan θ_B = n₂/n₁.

At θ_B the reflected light is completely s-polarised. Glare from water and roads is mostly horizontally polarised, so polarising sunglasses have vertical transmission axes; photographers use polarising filters to remove reflections and darken the sky. Laser tubes have Brewster windows that pass p-light with no loss, which makes the laser output polarised. Beyond the critical angle (from dense to less dense) both components are totally reflected but acquire different phases (the Fresnel rhomb uses this to make circular light).

Brewster's angle for glass (n = 1.52) and for water (n = 1.33)?
θ_B = arctan 1.52 = 56.7°; arctan 1.33 = 53.1°. Sunlight reflected from a lake with the Sun 37° above the horizon is almost completely polarised.
At Brewster's angle on glass, what fraction of unpolarised light is reflected?
θ_t = 90° − 56.7° = 33.3°. r_s = (cos 56.7° − 1.52 cos 33.3°)/(cos 56.7° + 1.52 cos 33.3°) = −0.396 → R_s = 15.7 %, R_p = 0. Unpolarised: (15.7 + 0)/2 = 7.8 %, all of it s-polarised.

14. Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity

Birefringence. In anisotropic crystals (calcite, quartz) the index depends on the polarisation: the ordinary ray sees n_o, the extraordinary ray n_e. Calcite (n_o = 1.658, n_e = 1.486) shows a double image (Bartholin, 1669). Stressed plastic becomes birefringent, so between crossed polarisers it shows coloured stress patterns: photoelasticity, used to test the strength of designs.

Wave plates. A plate of thickness t delays one polarisation relative to the other by the retardance Γ = 2π(n_e − n_o)t/λ.

ElementRetardanceEffect
Quarter-wave plate (λ/4)Γ = π/2linear light at 45° to its axis → circular, and back
Half-wave plate (λ/2)Γ = πrotates linear polarisation by 2α (α = angle to the axis); reverses handedness
Polariser—projects onto its axis

Jones matrix of a wave plate with fast axis at α: W = R(−α) · diag(1, e^{iΓ}) · R(α). The simulator multiplies these matrices for every element. 3D cinema glasses use circular polarisation (a λ/4 plate before a polariser), so tilting your head does not mix the left and right images.

Optical activity. Chiral molecules (sugars, amino acids) and quartz rotate the plane of polarisation by α = [α] · c · L (specific rotation × concentration × length). Saccharimeters measure sugar content this way (Biot, 1815; Pasteur discovered molecular chirality with it, 1848).

Liquid-crystal displays. A twisted-nematic cell between crossed polarisers rotates the polarisation by 90° and lets light through. A voltage untwists the molecules, the rotation stops and the pixel goes dark. That is why an LCD looks black through polarising sunglasses at certain angles.

Quartz has n_e − n_o = 0.0092 at 589 nm. Thickness of a zero-order quarter-wave plate?
Γ = π/2 → (n_e − n_o)t = λ/4 → t = 589 nm / (4 × 0.0092) = 16.0 µm. Too thin to handle, so commercial plates are often "multi-order" or cemented pairs.
Vertical polarised light passes a half-wave plate with its axis at 30° to the vertical. New polarisation direction?
Rotated by 2 × 30° = 60° from the vertical (preset "Half-wave plate rotates by 2α"; an analyser at 90° further is dark).
A sugar solution (10 g per 100 mL) fills a 20 cm tube. Sucrose has [α] = +66.5° mL g⁻¹ dm⁻¹. Rotation?
α = 66.5 × 0.10 g/mL × 2 dm = 13.3° (clockwise looking towards the source).

16. Practice Problems

Try each one first, then open it for the answer. Most can be checked in the simulator.

P1. In a Young experiment the 10th bright fringe is 18 mm from the centre on a screen 2.0 m away; d = 0.60 mm. Find λ.
y₁₀ = 10λL/d → λ = 18 mm × 0.60 mm / (10 × 2.0 m) = 540 nm.
P2. A double slit has d = 0.30 mm and a = 0.10 mm. How many bright fringes are inside the central diffraction envelope?
d/a = 3: orders ±3 are missing, so m = 0, ±1, ±2: 5 fringes.
P3. A grating has 500 lines/mm. Find the second-order angle for 600 nm and the highest observable order.
d = 2.0 µm. sin θ₂ = 2 × 0.6/2.0 = 0.6 → 36.9°. m_max = ⌊2.0/0.6⌋ = 3.
P4. How many lines must be illuminated to resolve the mercury yellow pair (577.0, 579.1 nm) in second order?
R = 578/2.1 = 275 → N = R/m = 138 lines (0.23 mm of a 600 lines/mm grating).
P5. A 0.20 mm slit is lit with 500 nm light; the screen is 2.5 m away. Width of the central maximum?
2λL/a = 2 × 500 nm × 2.5 m / 0.20 mm = 12.5 mm.
P6. What telescope diameter is needed to resolve 1 km on Mars at closest approach (5.6×10⁷ km) at 550 nm?
θ = 1 km / 5.6×10¹⁰ m = 1.79×10⁻⁸ rad. D = 1.22λ/θ = 1.22 × 550 nm / 1.79×10⁻⁸ = 37 m (and the atmosphere would still blur it).
P7. What is the thinnest soap film (n = 1.33) that looks dark in reflection at 500 nm (other than the black film)?
Dark when 2nt = mλ, m = 1: t = 500/(2 × 1.33) = 188 nm.
P8. A lens (n = 1.60) has a coating of n = 1.30. Minimum thickness for no reflection at 550 nm? Is the reflection zero?
t = λ/(4n) = 550/(4 × 1.30) = 106 nm. R = ((1.60 − 1.69)/(1.60 + 1.69))² = 0.07 %: practically zero, since √1.60 = 1.26 is close to 1.30.
P9. Polarised light passes a polariser at 40° to its direction and then another at 80°. Fraction transmitted?
cos²40° × cos²40° = 0.587² = 0.344 (34 %).
P10. Light reflected from a glass block is completely polarised when the angle of refraction is 32°. Index of the glass?
θ_B = 90° − 32° = 58°, n = tan 58° = 1.60.
P11. A Michelson interferometer is lit with the sodium doublet (589.0 and 589.6 nm). After how much mirror motion do the fringes first disappear?
The two fringe systems fall out of step after a path difference of λ²/Δλ/2 = (589.3 nm)²/(0.6 nm)/2 = 0.29 mm of path, i.e. a mirror motion of 0.145 mm.
P12. Why does a red laser through a fine mesh give a square array of spots, and what do you see if the mesh is rotated by 30°?
The mesh is two crossed gratings; the Fraunhofer pattern (the 2D Fourier transform) is a grid of spots with spacing λL/d in both directions. Rotating the mesh rotates the spot pattern by the same 30°.

18. Limits of the Models in This Module

Patterns use scalar Fraunhofer diffraction (far field) with ideal slits and apertures; the double slit assumes perfect spatial coherence, and white light uses equal energy at all wavelengths. Gratings in the zoom view use the ideal N-slit line shape with N = illuminated lines; the brightness of orders is schematic. Thin films assume a uniform, non-absorbing layer and use the exact multiple-beam (Airy) formula with Fresnel coefficients averaged over s and p; colours use the CIE 1931 observer (analytic fit) with the brightness scaled for display. Polarisers are ideal, wave plates have exact retardance at every wavelength, and Brewster reflection uses the Fresnel equations for a single clean interface.
