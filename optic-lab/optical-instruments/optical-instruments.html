Aberration presets

Practical guides for building each instrument on an optical bench (or with cardboard tubes) in a school or university lab. The numbers are a worked example you can copy; the formulas let you adapt them to the lenses you have. Use the buttons to open the same set-up in the simulator.

Safety: never look at the Sun through a lens, mirror, telescope or binoculars, not even for a moment. Permanent eye damage happens instantly. Use a distant building, tree or a lit target instead.
0. Before you start: find the focal length of your lenses ▲
You needConverging and diverging lenses, a white card (screen), a metre rule, a lens holder or optical bench
TipLens power in dioptres is often printed: f (m) = 1 / P. A +20 D lens has f = 5 cm.

Setting up

  1. Distant-object method (converging lens): stand by a window, hold the lens so it forms a sharp image of a distant building on the card. The lens–card distance is f.
  2. 2f–2f method: move an illuminated object and the screen until the image is sharp and the same size as the object. The object–screen distance is then 4f.
  3. Diverging lens: it gives no real image. Tape it against a stronger converging lens of known fc, measure the combination fcomb with the distant-object method, then use the formula below.
  4. Label every lens with its focal length (masking tape on the holder) before building anything.
1/fcomb = 1/fc + 1/fd  →  fd = 1 / (1/fcomb − 1/fc)    (fd comes out negative)

Tips and common mistakes

  • Measure f two or three times and average: judging "sharp" is the largest error.
  • Use the lens centre; hold it perpendicular to the line to the object.
1. Magnifying glass ▼
LensOne converging lens, f = 5–10 cm (+10 to +20 D)
ObjectPrinted text or a millimetre scale

Setting up

  1. Hold the lens close to your eye.
  2. Bring the object towards the lens until it looks sharp. For a relaxed eye the object sits at the focal point, so the distance is f.
  3. Moving the object slightly closer than f gives a slightly larger image at your near point (25 cm), but your eye has to work harder.
  4. To measure M, look at a millimetre scale through the lens with one eye and at a second scale 25 cm away with the other, and count how many divisions of the far scale match one magnified division.
M = 25 cm / f (relaxed eye)    M = 1 + 25 cm / f (image at the near point)    f = 5 cm → 5× to 6×

Tips and common mistakes

  • If the image is inverted or blurred the object is beyond f: move it closer.
  • Short focal lengths magnify more but have a small field and are hard to hold steady.
2. Compound microscope ▼
ObjectiveShort focal length converging lens: fo = 2.5 cm (+40 D)
EyepieceConverging lens: fe = 5 cm (+20 D)
AlsoOptical bench or a 25 cm tube, slide or millimetre scale, a lamp with a diffuser (tracing paper) under the object

Setting up

  1. Mount the objective and place the illuminated object just outside its focal point, at uo = 3.0 cm.
  2. Find the real, inverted, enlarged intermediate image with a small card: it forms at vo = 15 cm behind the objective (see formula). Mark this position.
  3. Remove the card and put the eyepiece so that this intermediate image lies at its focal point: 5 cm in front of the eyepiece, so the lenses are 20 cm apart.
  4. Look through the eyepiece with a relaxed eye. Focus by moving the object (or the objective) a few millimetres, not the eyepiece.
  5. Check the magnification: view a millimetre scale through the microscope and compare with a scale 25 cm from the other eye.
vo = fouo/(uo − fo) = 2.5·3/(0.5) = 15 cm    mo = −vo/uo = −5    Me = 25/fe = 5    M = −25× (inverted)

Tips and common mistakes

  • All lens centres must be on one straight axis: line them up by eye along a taut string or the bench rail first.
  • Strong light is essential: the image is dim because the light is spread over a large area.
  • The image is inverted and reversed; moving the slide left moves the image right.
  • Shorter fo or a longer tube gives more magnification but a smaller, dimmer, more sensitive image.
3. Keplerian (astronomical) telescope ▼
ObjectiveLong focal length converging lens: fo = 50 cm (+2 D), as wide as possible
EyepieceShort focal length converging lens: fe = 5 cm (+20 D)
AlsoTwo cardboard tubes that slide inside each other (about 60 cm total), black paint or paper, tape

Setting up

  1. Point the objective at a distant object (a building, not the Sun) and find its real image on a card: it forms fo = 50 cm behind the lens.
  2. Put the eyepiece fe = 5 cm behind that image position, so the lenses are fo + fe = 55 cm apart. Now parallel light enters and parallel light leaves (afocal).
  3. Mount the objective at the end of the outer tube and the eyepiece in the inner tube; slide the tubes to focus. Blacken the inside to stop reflections.
  4. Find the exit pupil: hold a card behind the eyepiece and look for the small bright disc. Put your eye there (about 5.5 cm behind the eyepiece).
  5. Measure M by looking at a brick wall with one eye through the telescope and the other directly, and count how many bricks fit in one magnified brick.
M = −fo/fe = −50/5 = −10× (inverted)    tube length = fo + fe = 55 cm    exit pupil = D/M

Tips and common mistakes

  • The image is upside down: fine for the Moon and stars, awkward on land.
  • If the image is coloured at the edges that is chromatic aberration; an achromatic objective or a small aperture stop helps.
  • A tripod is essential above about 10×.
4. Galilean telescope (opera glass) ▼
ObjectiveConverging lens: fo = 50 cm (+2 D)
EyepieceDiverging lens: fe = −5 cm (−20 D)
AlsoShorter tube, about 45 cm

Setting up

  1. Find the objective image position as for the Keplerian telescope (50 cm behind the lens).
  2. Put the diverging eyepiece 5 cm before that point, so the lenses are fo − |fe| = 45 cm apart. The converging light is made parallel before it focuses.
  3. Look through the eyepiece with your eye close to it and adjust the separation slightly until distant objects are sharp.
M = +fo/|fe| = +10× (upright)    tube length = fo − |fe| = 45 cm

Tips and common mistakes

  • The image is upright, which is why Galileo used this design and why opera glasses still do.
  • The field of view is small and the eye must be very close to the eyepiece; above about 4× it becomes hard to use.
5. Newtonian reflecting telescope ▼
PrimaryConcave mirror, fo = 50–120 cm (a telescope mirror; a make-up mirror is too short and poor)
SecondarySmall flat mirror, about ¼ of the primary diameter, mounted at 45°
AlsoEyepiece fe = 10–25 mm, tube, cross support for the flat

Setting up

  1. Find the focal length of the primary: point it at a distant object and catch the image on a small card in front of it. The distance is fo.
  2. Mount the primary at the bottom of the tube, facing up the tube.
  3. Place the flat mirror on the axis at 45°, a distance a little less than fo from the primary, so that the focus falls just outside the side of the tube.
  4. Cut a hole in the side of the tube at the focus and fit the eyepiece there. Focus by sliding the eyepiece in and out.
  5. Collimate: look into the focuser without an eyepiece. Tilt the flat until it shows the whole primary centred, then tilt the primary until the reflection of the flat is centred.
M = fo/fe    fo = 1200 mm, fe = 25 mm → 48×    secondary distance from primary ≈ fo − (tube radius + 3 cm)

Tips and common mistakes

  • A mirror has no chromatic aberration, so star images are colour-free.
  • The secondary and its support block a little light and cause the four "spikes" seen on bright stars.
  • Let the mirror reach outdoor temperature before observing; air currents in the tube blur the image.
6. Slide projector ▼
LightBright lamp, ideally with a condenser lens (f ≈ 5–10 cm) to collect light onto the slide
Projection lensConverging lens f = 10 cm
AlsoSlide or a drawing on transparent film, white screen or wall 2–4 m away

Setting up

  1. Place the lamp, then the condenser, then the slide just in front of the condenser so it is evenly lit.
  2. Put the slide upside down: the lens inverts the image, so it appears upright on the screen.
  3. Place the projection lens slightly more than f in front of the slide (about 10.3 cm for a screen 3 m away).
  4. Focus by moving the lens a few millimetres. Moving the screen further away makes the picture larger; refocus by bringing the lens slightly closer to the slide.
1/f = 1/do + 1/D    f = 10 cm, D = 300 cm → do = 10.34 cm    m = −D/do ≈ −29 (a 3.6 cm slide → 1.04 m)

Tips and common mistakes

  • The lamp gets hot: keep film slides away from it and switch off between measurements.
  • Darken the room; brightness falls as 1/m² because the same light covers a larger area.
7. Simple lens camera (camera obscura) ▼
BoxCardboard box or two tubes sliding inside each other
LensConverging lens f = 10–20 cm, or a pinhole
ScreenBaking paper or tracing paper at the back as a viewing screen

Setting up

  1. Fix the lens in a hole at the front of the box.
  2. Put the translucent screen at the back on a sliding tube. For a distant scene the screen must be at f behind the lens.
  3. Cover your head and the back of the box with a dark cloth and look at the screen: you see a real, inverted, colour image.
  4. For closer objects move the screen back: the image distance grows (see formula).
  5. Pinhole version: replace the lens by a small hole in aluminium foil. Everything is in focus, but the image is dim. The sharpest pinhole has the diameter given below.
v = f u /(u − f)    f = 10 cm: u = ∞ → v = 10 cm; u = 1 m → v = 11.1 cm    best pinhole d ≈ 1.9 √(f λ) ≈ 0.6 mm for f = 20 cm

Tips and common mistakes

  • A smaller lens opening (cardboard ring as an aperture) gives a deeper focus but a dimmer image: this is the f-number of a real camera.
  • Replace the screen by photographic paper in a dark room to take a real photograph.
8. Correcting a model eye ▼
Model eyeRound flask of water (eye) or a converging lens f ≈ 10 cm with a screen behind it (retina)
GlassesA set of spectacle lenses: −2 D, −1 D, +1 D, +2 D, +3 D

Setting up

  1. Set the screen so a distant object is sharp: this is a normal relaxed eye.
  2. Short sight: move the screen further back. Distant objects are now blurred; find which diverging lens in front of the "eye" makes them sharp again.
  3. Long sight: move the screen closer to the lens. Near objects are blurred; find the converging lens that fixes them.
  4. Compare the lens powers you found with the prescription formula.
Myopia: P = −1/FP    Hyperopia / reading: P = 1/0.25 m − 1/NP    (distances in metres, P in dioptres)

Tips and common mistakes

  • Put the correcting lens close to the model eye; a large gap changes the power needed.
The human eye and vision correction ▼

The cornea and lens form a real, inverted image on the retina. The lens–retina distance is fixed (about 2 cm in the reduced eye), so the eye focuses by changing its power: accommodation.

P_eye = 1/dₒ + 1/d_retina    relaxed (far point ∞): ≈ 50 D    near point 25 cm: ≈ 54 D

Myopia (short sight): the far point is finite; distant objects focus in front of the retina. Corrected with a diverging lens that images infinity at the far point: P = −1/FP.

Hyperopia (long sight) and presbyopia (loss of accommodation with age): the near point is far away; near objects focus behind the retina. Reading glasses image 25 cm at the near point: P = 1/0.25 − 1/NP.

Numbers for a normal eye

QuantityTypical value
Total power, relaxed≈ 60 D (cornea ≈ 43 D, lens ≈ 17 D); reduced-eye model 50 D
Amplitude of accommodation14 D at age 10, 7 D at 30, 2 D at 50, < 1 D at 60
Pupil diameter2 mm (bright) to 7–8 mm (dark)
Angular resolutionabout 1′ of arc (= 0.3 mm at 1 m); diffraction limit 1.22 λ/D ≈ 0.5′

Most of the eye's power is at the cornea, where light goes from air into tissue (n ≈ 1.38). Under water that surface loses almost all its power, so swimmers need goggles to see clearly.

Astigmatism

A cornea that is more curved in one direction focuses vertical and horizontal lines at different depths. It is corrected with a cylindrical lens, written as a third number on a prescription (e.g. −1.50 / −0.75 × 90°).

A myope cannot see clearly beyond 80 cm. What glasses are needed?
P = −1/FP = −1/0.80 m = −1.25 D. The lens images infinity at 80 cm, where the relaxed eye can focus.
A 55-year-old has a near point of 1.5 m. What reading glasses let them read at 30 cm?
P = 1/0.30 − 1/1.5 = 3.33 − 0.67 = +2.67 D (in practice +2.75 D).
Simple magnifier ▼

A converging lens held close to the eye with the object inside its focal length gives an upright, virtual, enlarged image. What matters is the angle the image fills, compared with the object at the near point (25 cm).

M = 25 cm / f (image at ∞, relaxed eye)    M = 1 + 25 cm / f (image at the near point)

Derivation

Without the lens the largest angle you can get is with the object at the near point D = 25 cm: θ₀ ≈ h/D. With the lens close to the eye and the object at distance dₒ the image subtends θ ≈ h/dₒ. So M = θ/θ₀ = D/dₒ.

For the image at infinity dₒ = f → M = D/f. For the image at the near point, 1/dₒ = 1/f + 1/D → M = 1 + D/f.

Magnifiers above about 10–15× are impractical: f becomes very short, the lens must be tiny and aberrations grow. That is why a compound microscope is needed for more.

What focal length gives a 4× magnifier for a relaxed eye?
f = 25 cm / 4 = 6.25 cm, a +16 D lens.
Camera ▼

A camera lens forms a real, inverted, diminished image on the sensor. The field of view depends on focal length and sensor size; the f-number N = f/D sets how much light passes.

field of view = 2 arctan(w / 2f)    EV = log₂(N²/t)    one stop = ×√2 in N = ×2 in time
Hyperfocal H = f²/(N c) + f    near = s(H−f)/(H+s−2f)    far = s(H−f)/(H−s)

c is the circle of confusion (0.03 mm full frame). Larger N, shorter f and a more distant subject all increase the depth of field.

f-number and exposure

The light reaching each point of the sensor is proportional to (D/f)² = 1/N². The standard f-stops 1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 5.6, 8, 11, 16 each differ by √2, so each step halves the light. Exposure ∝ t/N².

Focusing

From the lens equation the lens–sensor distance is v = f u/(u − f). Focusing from infinity to a subject at u moves the lens out by about f²/u. A 50 mm lens focused at 1 m moves out 2.6 mm.

Diffraction

The Airy disc diameter is 2.44 λN. At f/16 it is about 21 µm, bigger than a pixel: stopping down too far softens the picture.

A photo is correctly exposed at f/4, 1/250 s. What shutter time is needed at f/8?
f/4 → f/8 is two stops (4× less light), so t = 4/250 = 1/60 s.
Why does a phone camera have so much depth of field?
Its focal length is only about 4 mm. The hyperfocal distance H = f²/(Nc) + f is 1.8 m at f/1.8, so everything from about 0.9 m to infinity is sharp.
Projector ▼

The slide sits just outside the focal point, so the lens forms a large, real, inverted image on a distant screen. The slide is inserted upside down so the picture appears upright.

1/f = 1/dₒ + 1/D    m = −D/dₒ ≈ −D/f

Brightness

The same light is spread over an area m² times larger than the slide, so the picture brightness falls as 1/m². A condenser lens images the lamp into the projection lens so that the slide is lit evenly and no light is wasted.

A 2.4 cm LCD panel must fill a 2.4 m screen 6 m away. What focal length is needed?
|m| = 100 → dₒ = 600/100 = 6 cm. 1/f = 1/6 + 1/600 → f = 5.94 cm.
Reflecting telescope (Newtonian) ▼

A concave (parabolic) primary mirror collects the light; a small flat mirror at 45° sends the converging beam out of the side of the tube to the eyepiece. Magnification is the same as for a refractor, M = fₒ/fₑ.

Mirrors have no chromatic aberration, need only one polished surface and can be supported from behind, so all large telescopes are reflectors.

Other reflector designs

A spherical mirror suffers from spherical aberration; a paraboloid brings all parallel rays to one focus. The Hubble mirror was ground to the wrong conic constant (2.2 µm too flat at the edge) and needed corrective optics in 1993.

Resolution and the diffraction limit ▼

Light passing through a circular opening of diameter D spreads into an Airy pattern: a bright disc surrounded by faint rings. The first dark ring is at angle θ = 1.22 λ/D.

Rayleigh criterion: two points are just resolved when θ = 1.22 λ/D
InstrumentDθ (550 nm)
Eye (3 mm pupil)3 mm46″ ≈ 0.8′
Small telescope100 mm1.4″
Hubble2.4 m0.06″
Microscope, NA 1.4—d = 0.61 λ/NA = 0.24 µm

Magnification cannot beat this limit: once the instrument resolves its finest detail, more magnification only makes the blur bigger (empty magnification).

Brightness: f-number, pupils and light grasp ▼

The energy collected is proportional to the aperture area, πD²/4. What the image looks like depends on where that light goes:

  • Point sources (stars): all the light goes into one diffraction spot, so a bigger D always shows fainter stars.
  • Extended objects (Moon, nebulae, a photographed scene): the surface brightness of the image depends only on the f-number (camera) or on the exit pupil (visual). No instrument can make an extended object look brighter than with the naked eye.
image irradiance ∝ 1/N²    visual brightness ∝ (exit pupil / eye pupil)², max 1
Practice problems ▼
1. A student can see clearly only between 15 cm and 1.0 m. What glasses for distance? With them on, what is the new near point?
P = −1/1.0 = −1 D. With −1 D: an object at u must image at 15 cm: −1/u − 1 = −1/0.15 → 1/u = 5.67 → u = 17.6 cm.
2. A microscope has fₒ = 1.5 cm, fₑ = 2.5 cm and a 16 cm tube. Total magnification?
M = (16/1.5)(25/2.5) = 10.7 × 10 ≈ 107×.
3. A Keplerian telescope is 84 cm long and magnifies 20×. Find the focal lengths.
fₒ + fₑ = 84, fₒ/fₑ = 20 → fₑ = 4 cm, fₒ = 80 cm.
4. Can a 50 mm telescope separate the two headlights of a car (1.5 m apart) 30 km away?
Angle = 1.5/30000 = 5×10⁻⁵ rad = 10″. Dawes limit 116/50 = 2.3″, so yes (if the air is steady).
5. A landscape is shot with a 35 mm lens at f/11 on full frame. Hyperfocal distance?
H = 35²/(11 × 0.03) + 35 = 3747 mm ≈ 3.7 m. Focus there: sharp from 1.9 m to infinity.
6. A projector lens (f = 8 cm) puts a picture 50 times larger than the slide on the screen. How far is the screen?
m = −50 → D = 50 dₒ; 1/8 = 1/dₒ + 1/(50dₒ) = 51/(50dₒ) → dₒ = 8.16 cm, D = 4.08 m.
Thin-lens formula (recap) ▼

The fundamental formula for both mirrors and thin lenses (real-is-positive convention):

1/f = 1/dₒ + 1/dᵢ

Where f = focal length, dₒ = object distance, dᵢ = image distance. Magnification:

m = −dᵢ / dₒ = hᵢ / hₒ
  • f > 0: converging element (concave mirror, convex lens)
  • f < 0: diverging element (convex mirror, concave lens)
  • dᵢ > 0: real image (in front of mirror, or beyond lens)
  • dᵢ < 0: virtual image (behind mirror, or same side as object for lens)
  • |m| > 1: enlarged; |m| < 1: diminished; m < 0: inverted
Snell's law and refraction ▼
n₁ sinθ₁ = n₂ sinθ₂

The refractive index n = c/v (speed of light in vacuum / speed in medium). Optical density increases with n.

  • n₂ > n₁: light bends toward normal (θ₂ < θ₁)
  • n₂ < n₁: light bends away from normal (θ₂ > θ₁)

Total Internal Reflection occurs when n₁ > n₂ and θ₁ exceeds the critical angle:

θ_c = arcsin(n₂ / n₁)

All incident light is reflected; no refracted ray exists. Used in optical fibres and prisms.

Spherical aberration ▼

Spherical surfaces do not bring all parallel rays to a common focus. Marginal rays (through outer zones) focus closer to the lens than paraxial rays (near the axis).

  • Paraxial focus: theoretical ideal focus for near-axis rays
  • Marginal focus: convergence point of outer rays (closer to lens)
  • Circle of least confusion: smallest beam cross-section between foci — best image plane
  • Longitudinal SA: distance between marginal and paraxial foci
  • Transverse SA: radius of blur circle in paraxial focal plane

Remedies: aspherical lenses, aperture stops (using only paraxial zone), achromatic doublets.

Compound microscope ▼

Two converging lenses: objective (short f, near specimen) + eyepiece (magnifier on intermediate image).

M_obj = −dᵢ / dₒ (real, inverted, magnified intermediate image)
M_eye = 25 / f_eye (eyepiece as simple magnifier, near point = 25 cm)
M_total = M_obj × M_eye ≈ (L / f_obj) × (25 / f_eye)

Where L = optical tube length (distance from the objective's back focal point to the eyepiece's front focal point). Standard L = 160 mm, so an objective marked 40× has fₒ = 4 mm.

Resolution. Diffraction limits the smallest resolvable separation (Rayleigh): d = 0.61 λ / NA, with NA = n sin θ. Air limits NA to below 1; immersion oil (n ≈ 1.52) allows NA up to about 1.4.

useful magnification ≈ 500 NA … 1000 NA (beyond this: empty magnification)

Why two lenses

The objective makes a real, enlarged image (lateral magnification mₒ = −L/fₒ). The eyepiece then acts as a magnifier on that image (Mₑ = 25 cm/fₑ). The magnifications multiply.

Numerical aperture and the Abbe limit

NA = n sin θ, where θ is the half-angle of the cone of light the objective accepts. Abbe showed that a periodic detail of spacing d is resolved only if at least the first diffraction orders enter the objective: d = λ/(2 NA) with oblique light, about 0.61 λ/NA by the Rayleigh criterion.

Ways to see finer detail: higher NA (oil immersion), shorter wavelength (blue or UV light), or beyond the limit with super-resolution techniques (Nobel Prize 2014) and electron microscopes (λ ≈ 0.004 nm).

Depth of field

Roughly λn/NA²: at NA 1.25 only about 0.5 µm of the specimen is sharp at once, so you focus through a cell layer by layer.

A microscope has a 40× / 0.65 objective and a 10× eyepiece. What is the smallest resolvable detail in green light (550 nm), and is the magnification useful?
d = 0.61 × 0.55 µm / 0.65 = 0.52 µm. Total M = 400×; useful range 500·NA–1000·NA = 325–650×, so 400× is useful.
An objective of fₒ = 4 mm is used with a 160 mm tube. Where is the object?
dᵢ = fₒ + L = 164 mm. 1/dₒ = 1/4 − 1/164 → dₒ = 4.10 mm, just outside the focal point.
Refracting telescopes ▼

Two converging lenses with shared focal plane (afocal system for infinite conjugates):

  • Objective: Large aperture, long focal length — forms real image of distant object at focal plane
  • Eyepiece: Short focal length — its front focal point coincides with objective's back focal point
  • Tube length = f_obj + f_eye
M = −f_obj / f_eye (angular magnification, inverted image)
Exit pupil = D_obj / |M|  |  f-ratio = f_obj / D_obj

Galilean telescope: a diverging eyepiece placed before the focal plane (separation fₒ − |fₑ|). The image is upright and the tube is short (opera glasses), but the field of view is narrow.

Galilean: M = +fₒ / |fₑ|  |  Resolution (Dawes) ≈ 116″ / D[mm]  |  useful M ≲ 2 × D[mm]

Derivation of the angular magnification

A star at angle α forms an image of height y = fₒ tan α in the common focal plane. The eyepiece sees this image at its focal point, so the rays leave at angle αₑ with tan αₑ = y/fₑ. Hence M = αₑ/α = fₒ/fₑ.

Exit pupil and eye relief

The eyepiece forms an image of the objective: the exit pupil, of diameter D/M, at a distance about fₑ(fₒ + fₑ)/fₒ behind the eyepiece. Your eye's pupil should sit there; if the exit pupil is bigger than your pupil, light is wasted.

Light grasp and limiting magnitude

A telescope collects (D/deye)² times more light than the eye. A 100 mm telescope gathers about 200 times more than a 7 mm pupil, showing stars about 5.8 magnitudes fainter (limiting magnitude ≈ 2 + 5 log₁₀ D[mm] ≈ 12).

Resolution

Rayleigh: θ = 1.22 λ/D; Dawes (for double stars): θ ≈ 116″/D[mm]. The atmosphere ("seeing") usually limits ground telescopes to about 1″, whatever their size.

A 70 mm refractor has fₒ = 700 mm. What eyepiece gives the maximum useful magnification?
Useful limit ≈ 2 × 70 = 140×. fₑ = 700/140 = 5 mm.
Why are binoculars labelled 7×50?
Magnification 7, objective diameter 50 mm. Exit pupil = 50/7 = 7.1 mm, matching a dark-adapted eye: ideal for night use.
