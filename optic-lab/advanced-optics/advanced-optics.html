Physics Details

From Fermat's principle to real optical systems: refraction at curved surfaces, thick lenses and principal planes, the five Seidel aberrations, dispersion and achromats, ray-transfer matrices, optical fibres and the rainbow. Open the worked examples to check your understanding.

1. Fermat's Principle and the Laws of Optics

Light takes a path whose optical path length OPL = ∫ n ds is stationary. Equal OPL for all rays from an object point to its image is what makes an image. Snell's law follows from minimising t = (n₁√(a² + x²) + n₂√(b² + (d − x)²))/c.

A lens is "thicker in the middle" exactly so that the short central path through glass has the same OPL as the long outer paths through air.

2. Imaging Equation

1 / f = 1 / s + 1 / s'

f is focal length, s is object distance, and s' is image distance. In this lab the real-is-positive convention is used: real objects and real images are positive, while virtual quantities are negative.

Both mirror and thin-lens equations are special cases of refraction at a single spherical surface of radius R between indices n₁ and n₂: n₁/s + n₂/s′ = (n₂ − n₁)/R. A mirror behaves like a surface with n₂ = −n₁, which gives 1/s + 1/s′ = 2/R. Two surfaces in a row give the lensmaker's equation.

A glass sphere (n = 1.5, R = 5 cm) is a "ball lens". Where does it focus sunlight?
First surface: 1/∞ + 1.5/s′ = 0.5/5 → s′ = 15 cm (beyond the sphere). It becomes a virtual object 5 cm behind the back surface: 1.5/(−5) + 1/s″ = (1 − 1.5)/(−5) → s″ = 2.5 cm behind the back surface, i.e. f = 7.5 cm from the centre (f = nR/(2(n − 1))).

3. Magnification

m = -s' / s

The image height is h' = m h. A negative magnification means the image is inverted. A positive magnification means the image is upright.

For a refracting surface the lateral magnification is m = −(n₁ s′)/(n₂ s); for a system it is the product of the stage magnifications. The longitudinal magnification (along the axis) is m², which is why a 3D object looks stretched in depth when imaged at high magnification.

Lagrange invariant: n y θ (height × angle × index) is the same at every image plane. A smaller image is always accompanied by steeper rays, which sets the limit on how bright and how small a spot any lens can make.

4. Lensmaker Equation

Phi = (n_rel - 1) [ (1 / R1) - (1 / R2) + ((n_rel - 1) d) / (n_rel R1 R2) ]

The optical power is Phi = 1 / f, n_rel = n / n_m, and d is center thickness. For a convex lens in left-to-right light travel, R1 > 0 and R2 < 0. For a concave lens the signs reverse.

Thick lens (thickness t): 1/f = (n − 1)[1/R₁ − 1/R₂ + (n − 1)t/(n R₁ R₂)]. Distances are measured from the principal planes H and H′, which lie at −f(n − 1)t/(n R₂) from the first vertex and −f(n − 1)t/(n R₁) from the second.

Shape factor. Lenses with the same f can be bent (biconvex, plano-convex, meniscus). For a distant object the least spherical aberration comes from a nearly plano-convex lens with the curved side facing the light: this is the "best-form" lens.

A biconvex lens has R₁ = 20 cm, R₂ = −20 cm, n = 1.5, t = 2 cm. Thin- and thick-lens focal lengths?
Thin: 1/f = 0.5(0.05 + 0.05) = 0.05 → 20 cm. Thick: extra term 0.5·2/(1.5·20·(−20)) = −0.00167 → 1/f = 0.5(0.1 − 0.00167) → f = 20.3 cm.

5. Lens Refraction

n1 sin(theta1) = n2 sin(theta2)

This is Snell's law. In the aberration tab, each ray is intersected with the spherical surfaces and refracted with the full vector form of Snell's law. That lets the model show non-paraxial behavior directly.

How the ray trace works. For each ray: find where it meets the next spherical surface (a line–circle intersection), compute the surface normal at that point, and apply the vector form of Snell's law, t = r d + (r c − √(1 − r²(1 − c²))) n with r = n₁/n₂ and c = −n·d. If the square root is imaginary the ray is totally internally reflected. Repeating this surface by surface is exactly what professional optical-design programs do.

6. Spherical Aberration

Rays close to the lens edge are refracted more strongly than paraxial rays. That shifts their focus and produces a focus spread instead of a single exact image point.

The simulator reports longitudinal spherical aberration (difference in focus position) and transverse aberration (ray height error in a chosen reference plane).

Third-order estimate. For a thin lens focusing parallel light, marginal rays at height h focus short of the paraxial focus by the longitudinal spherical aberration LSA ≈ k h²/f, with k depending on n and the lens shape. The blur at the best focus (circle of least confusion) is about LSA·h/(4f), so it grows as h³: stopping down by 2 reduces the blur 8 times.

Cures: best-form bending, splitting the power over two lenses, aspheric surfaces, or a doublet whose aberrations cancel.

8. Dispersion and Chromatic Aberration

n(λ) = A + B/λ² (Cauchy). Short wavelengths are bent more. The Abbe number V = (n_d − 1)/(n_F − n_C) measures dispersion: crown glass V ≈ 64, flint ≈ 36.

A singlet lens has a focal length that depends on colour; the longitudinal colour error is about f/V. An achromatic doublet uses f₁V₁ + f₂V₂ = 0 so that red and blue focus together.

Lateral colour (different magnification for each colour) affects off-axis images; longitudinal colour (different focus) affects the whole field. An achromat cancels the second for two wavelengths; an apochromat uses special low-dispersion glass (fluorite, ED glass) to bring three colours together.

Fraunhofer lines used to define glass: F = 486.1 nm (blue hydrogen), d = 587.6 nm (yellow helium), C = 656.3 nm (red hydrogen).

A crown/flint achromat must have f = 100 cm. V₁ = 64, V₂ = 36. Find f₁ and f₂.
1/f₁ + 1/f₂ = 1/100 and 1/(f₁V₁) + 1/(f₂V₂) = 0 → f₁ = f(V₁ − V₂)/V₁ = 43.8 cm, f₂ = −f(V₁ − V₂)/V₂ = −77.8 cm.

9. Ray-Transfer (ABCD) Matrices

A paraxial ray is a vector (y, θ). A thin lens is [1 0; −1/f 1], a gap is [1 d; 0 1]; a system is the product, applied right to left.

For M = [A B; C D]: f = −1/C, back focal distance −A/C, front focal distance −D/C, H′ at (1 − A)/C from the last element. C = 0 means afocal (telescope). Two thin lenses: 1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂ − d/(f₁f₂).

ElementMatrix
Free space, length d[1 d; 0 1]
Thin lens, focal length f[1 0; −1/f 1]
Flat interface n₁ → n₂[1 0; 0 n₁/n₂]
Curved interface, radius R[1 0; (n₁ − n₂)/(n₂R) n₁/n₂]
Spherical mirror, radius R[1 0; −2/R 1]

The determinant AD − BC equals n₁/n₂ (1 in air) for every system: a quick check of your algebra. The same matrices describe laser resonators and Gaussian beams, where a cavity is stable when |A + D| ≤ 2 for one round trip.

Show that two lenses in contact add powers.
[1 0; −1/f₂ 1][1 0; −1/f₁ 1] = [1 0; −(1/f₁ + 1/f₂) 1], so 1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂.

10. Optical Fibres

Total internal reflection at the core–cladding wall traps light. NA = n₀ sin θ_max = √(n₁² − n₂²).

Rays at different angles travel different paths, so a pulse spreads: Δt/L ≈ (n₁/c)(n₁/n₂ − 1). A fibre is single-mode when V = 2πa·NA/λ < 2.405, which removes this modal dispersion.

Graded-index fibre. If n falls smoothly from the axis outwards (parabolic profile), rays follow sinusoidal paths and the steep rays travel through more of the faster low-index glass. All rays then take almost the same time, cutting modal dispersion from about 50 ns/km to about 0.5 ns/km.

Losses. Modern silica fibre loses only about 0.2 dB/km at 1550 nm (light falls to half after about 15 km). Losses come from Rayleigh scattering (∝ 1/λ⁴) at short wavelengths and absorption by the glass at long wavelengths, which is why telecom uses 1310 and 1550 nm.

A step-index fibre has n₁ = 1.50, n₂ = 1.48. Find NA, acceptance angle and pulse spread over 10 km.
NA = √(1.50² − 1.48²) = 0.244, θ = 14.1°. Δt = (L n₁/c)(n₁/n₂ − 1) = (10⁴ × 1.5/3×10⁸)(0.0135) = 0.68 µs.

11. The Rainbow

For a ray with impact parameter b in a drop: D = 2(i − r) + k(π − 2r), with sin i = b/R, sin i = n sin r and k internal reflections.

D has a stationary value where cos² i = (n² − 1)/(k(k + 2)). Many rays leave near this angle, so light concentrates there: 42° for k = 1, 51° for k = 2. Because n depends on λ, each colour has its own angle (Descartes, Newton).

Derivation. With sin i = b/R and sin i = n sin r, the deviation D = 2(i − r) + k(π − 2r). Setting dD/di = 0 and using dr/di = cos i/(n cos r) gives cos i = √((n² − 1)/(k(k + 2))). For water (n = 1.333) and k = 1: i = 59.4°, r = 40.2°, D = 137.9°, so the bow is at 180° − 137.9° = 42.1° from the antisolar point.

Polarisation and supernumeraries. Rainbow light is strongly polarised (the internal reflection is close to Brewster's angle). Faint pastel bands just inside the primary bow (supernumerary arcs) come from interference between the two rays that leave at the same angle; geometric optics cannot explain them, but wave optics (Airy, 1838) can.

12. Practice Problems

A plano-convex lens (n = 1.52) has f = 50 cm. What is the radius of the curved face?
1/f = (n − 1)/R → R = 0.52 × 50 = 26 cm.
The same lens is put in water (n = 1.33). New focal length?
1/f = (1.52/1.33 − 1)/26 → f = 182 cm (3.6 times longer).
Two lenses, f₁ = 30 cm and f₂ = −20 cm, are 15 cm apart. Effective focal length and back focal distance?
1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂ − d/(f₁f₂) = 1/30 − 1/20 + 15/600 = 0.00833 → f = 120 cm. BFL = f(f₁ − d)/f₁ = 120 × 15/30 = 60 cm.
Is a fibre with core 9 µm, NA = 0.12 single-mode at 1310 nm?
V = 2π(4.5 µm)(0.12)/1.31 µm = 2.59 > 2.405: slightly multimode at 1310 nm, single-mode above λ = 1.41 µm.
A glass prism (n = 1.5, A = 60°) is used at minimum deviation. Find D_min.
n = sin((A + D)/2)/sin(A/2) → sin((60 + D)/2) = 0.75 → D = 37.2°.

13. Limits of the Model

Mirrors and the imaging tabs use paraxial thin-optics equations, so they are most accurate for small angles and moderate apertures. The aberration tab is more physical because it uses direct refraction at spherical surfaces, but it still assumes a 2D meridional slice and ideal homogeneous materials.

Manual

How to Use the Lab

Pick a tab, move the sliders, and watch the numbers and diagrams update together. The left panel controls the system. The right side shows the calculated quantities and the 2D/3D visualizations.

Suggested Lens Experiments

Increase the refractive index or reduce the curvature radii to strengthen the lens and shorten the focal length. Compare convex and concave lenses with the same absolute curvature values and note the sign change in focal length and image distance.

Suggested Aberration Experiments

Keep the material fixed and increase the aperture. Marginal rays should shift farther from the paraxial focus. Then try a plano-convex or meniscus preset to compare how lens shape changes aberration behavior.

New Tabs

Dispersion: choose a material and a prism, a single lens or an achromatic doublet; the focal length against wavelength is plotted underneath. Lens systems: two thin lenses (set f = 0 to remove one); read the system matrix and cardinal points, and watch the intermediate image. Optical fibre: change the indices and launch angle; rays outside the acceptance cone leak out. Rainbow: move the impact parameter and see the exit angle reach its 42° limit.

Reading the Diagrams

Solid rays are physical light paths. Dashed rays are backward extensions used to locate virtual images. In the 3D views, the wireframe surfaces are perspective schematics that help you see the optic shape and beam direction.

Practical Notes

When the object approaches the focal point, the image can move very far away and the magnification can become very large. That is expected behavior from the imaging equation, not a bug.
