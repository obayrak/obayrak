Lasers, Light Forces and Relativity — Theory

This chapter starts inside the laser: which transverse field patterns a two-mirror cavity supports, which frequencies it can oscillate at, and how locking those frequencies together makes ultrashort pulses and optical frequency combs. It then turns to the momentum of light and the optical tweezers built on it. The last part treats light under special and general relativity: the relativistic Doppler effect, aberration, Fizeau's moving-water experiment and gravitational lensing. Practice problems, history and the limits of the models close the chapter.

1. Optical Resonators and Stability

A laser needs feedback: two mirrors facing each other so that light passes many times through the gain medium. Flat mirrors are very sensitive to tilt, and any ray not exactly on axis walks out. Curved mirrors can refocus the light so that rays stay trapped. Whether they do is decided by the two g-parameters

g₁ = 1 − L/R₁, g₂ = 1 − L/R₂, stable if 0 ≤ g₁g₂ ≤ 1

where L is the mirror separation and R the radii of curvature (positive for concave mirrors facing the cavity). In the g₁–g₂ plane the stable region lies between the axes and the hyperbola g₁g₂ = 1 (the Resonator modes tab draws it).

Why this condition. A round trip is the ray-transfer (ABCD) matrix M of propagation, mirror 2, propagation, mirror 1. Rays stay bounded after many round trips only if the eigenvalues of M lie on the unit circle, which requires |(A + D)/2| ≤ 1. Multiplying out the matrices gives (A + D)/2 = 2g₁g₂ − 1, so −1 ≤ 2g₁g₂ − 1 ≤ 1, i.e. 0 ≤ g₁g₂ ≤ 1.

2. Gaussian Beams in a Cavity

The lowest mode of a stable cavity is a Gaussian beam whose wavefront curvature matches each mirror. It has a waist w₀ somewhere between the mirrors and expands as

w(z) = w₀ √(1 + (z/z_R)²), z_R = π w₀²/λ, R(z) = z (1 + (z_R/z)²)

Requiring R(z) to equal the mirror radii at the two mirror positions fixes w₀ and the waist position. For the spot sizes on the mirrors:

w₁² = (λL/π) √( g₂ / (g₁(1 − g₁g₂)) ), w₂² = (λL/π) √( g₁ / (g₂(1 − g₁g₂)) )

Both diverge at the edges of the stability region, where the mode either fills the mirrors or collapses to a point. A symmetric confocal cavity (g₁ = g₂ = 0) has w₀ = √(λL/2π): for a 30 cm He–Ne cavity, 0.17 mm.

On each round trip a Gaussian beam picks up the ordinary propagation phase 2kL plus an extra Gouy phase. For a mode of order N (N = m + n for Hermite–Gauss, 2p + |l| for Laguerre–Gauss) the one-way Gouy phase is (N + 1) arccos(±√(g₁g₂)). This makes the resonance frequencies depend on the transverse order (section 4).

Worked example: a He–Ne cavity has a flat mirror and a concave mirror with R = 1 m, 30 cm apart. Find the spot sizes at 633 nm.
g₁ = 1, g₂ = 0.7, g₁g₂ = 0.7. λL/π = 6.04 × 10⁻⁸ m². w₁² = 6.04 × 10⁻⁸ × √(0.7/(1 × 0.3)) = 9.23 × 10⁻⁸ → w₁ = 0.30 mm (the waist, on the flat mirror). w₂² = 6.04 × 10⁻⁸ × √(1/(0.7 × 0.3)) = 1.32 × 10⁻⁷ → w₂ = 0.36 mm.

3. Higher-Order Transverse Modes

The Gaussian is the lowest of a complete family of beams that keep their shape as they propagate, apart from scaling with w(z). In Cartesian coordinates they are the Hermite–Gauss modes TEMₘₙ:

E ∝ Hₘ(√2 x/w) Hₙ(√2 y/w) exp(−(x² + y²)/w²) × phase

TEMₘₙ has m nodal lines across x and n across y. In cylindrical coordinates the equivalent family is the Laguerre–Gauss modes LGₚₗ:

E ∝ (√2 r/w)^|l| L_p^|l|(2r²/w²) exp(−r²/w²) e^{ilφ}

LGₚₗ has p dark rings and a phase that winds by 2πl around the axis: an optical vortex with a dark core, carrying orbital angular momentum lħ per photon. Each set is complete; an LG mode is a superposition of HG modes of the same order N (for example LG₀₁ = (TEM₁₀ + i TEM₀₁)/√2). Lasers oscillate on TEM₀₀ when an aperture inside the cavity adds enough loss to the wider higher-order modes.

Higher-order modes are larger: the rms radius grows as √(N + 1). The beam quality factor, which measures how much faster than an ideal Gaussian a beam diverges at a given waist, is M² = 2m + 1 along x for TEMₘₙ and 2p + |l| + 1 for LGₚₗ.

4. Longitudinal Modes and Gain

A mode resonates when the round-trip phase is a multiple of 2π. For the fundamental mode, ignoring the small Gouy term, this gives frequencies separated by the free spectral range

Δν_FSR = c/(2nL)

A 30 cm cavity has Δν = 500 MHz; a 1.5 m one, 100 MHz. Including the Gouy phase, the full set of resonances is

ν_qmn = Δν_FSR [ q + (m + n + 1) arccos(±√(g₁g₂))/π ]

so transverse modes of different orders are shifted from each other. In a confocal cavity (arccos 0 = π/2) they fall halfway between the longitudinal modes or on top of them: the mode spectrum becomes degenerate, which is why confocal cavities make good scanning spectrum analysers.

The laser oscillates only on modes whose gain exceeds the loss. The gain curve has a width set by the medium: about 1.5 GHz (Doppler) for He–Ne, about 120 GHz for Nd:YAG, 100 THz for Ti:sapphire. Dividing the gain width above threshold by Δν_FSR gives the number of oscillating modes: a He–Ne laser runs on 2–3 modes, a Ti:sapphire laser can support a million. A single-frequency laser needs either a short cavity, so that only one mode fits under the gain, or an extra filter (an etalon) inside the cavity.

Linewidth. A passive cavity with round-trip loss fraction ℓ has finesse F ≈ 2π/ℓ and resonance width δν = Δν_FSR/F. An active laser is narrower still: the Schawlow–Townes limit δν_L = πhν(δν)²/P shrinks with output power P. For a 1 mW He–Ne laser with δν ≈ 1 MHz, δν_L is below 1 mHz; in practice mirror vibrations and temperature drifts give kHz–MHz.

5. Mode Locking and Ultrashort Pulses

If N longitudinal modes oscillate with random phases, their sum is a noisy field whose intensity fluctuates randomly around N times the single-mode value. If the phases are locked (all equal, or varying linearly with mode number), the modes interfere constructively once per round trip and destructively elsewhere:

I(t) ∝ sin²(Nπ Δν t) / sin²(π Δν t)

This is a train of pulses with spacing T = 1/Δν = 2L/c (the round-trip time), peak power N times the average, and duration

τ ≈ 1/(N Δν) ≈ 1/Δν_gain

The broader the gain, the shorter the pulse. Ti:sapphire (≈ 100 THz) supports pulses below 10 fs, only a few optical cycles. The Longitudinal modes tab adds up to 301 modes with either random or locked phases to show the difference.

Locking is enforced by something in the cavity that favours high peak power: an acousto-optic modulator (active), a saturable absorber that becomes transparent at high intensity, or Kerr-lens mode locking, where the intense pulse self-focuses through an aperture (passive). The time–bandwidth product of a transform-limited Gaussian pulse is Δν·τ = 0.441 (FWHM), and 0.315 for a sech² pulse.

6. Optical Frequency Combs

The spectrum of a mode-locked pulse train is a set of perfectly evenly spaced lines: a frequency comb. Because the pulse envelope travels at the group velocity and the carrier at the phase velocity, the carrier slips by a phase Δφ relative to the envelope on each round trip. This shifts the whole comb by an offset frequency:

ν_n = n f_rep + f_ceo, f_ceo = (Δφ/2π) f_rep

Both f_rep (typically 100 MHz – 1 GHz) and f_ceo are radio frequencies and can be counted electronically. Once they are known, every optical line ν_n is known to the same accuracy: the comb is a ruler that connects optical frequencies (hundreds of THz) to a microwave atomic clock.

f–2f self-referencing. If the comb spans an octave, take a line n from the red end and double its frequency: 2ν_n = 2n f_rep + 2f_ceo. Beat it against the line 2n from the blue end, ν_2n = 2n f_rep + f_ceo. The beat note is 2ν_n − ν_2n = f_ceo, measured directly. Spectra this broad are produced by sending the pulses through a nonlinear photonic-crystal fibre (supercontinuum generation, Module 8).

Combs earned Hall and Hänsch the 2005 Nobel Prize. They are used in optical atomic clocks (fractional uncertainties near 10⁻¹⁸), calibration of astronomical spectrographs for exoplanet searches (cm/s precision in Doppler velocity), molecular spectroscopy with thousands of lines at once (dual-comb spectroscopy) and precision distance measurement.

Worked example: a comb has f_rep = 250 MHz and f_ceo = 20 MHz. A laser near 282 THz beats at 63 MHz with the nearest comb line, which has n = 1 128 000. What is its frequency?
ν_n = 1 128 000 × 250 MHz + 20 MHz = 282.000 020 THz. The laser is at ν_n ± 63 MHz, i.e. 282.000 083 THz or 281.999 957 THz. Changing f_rep slightly and seeing which way the beat moves resolves the sign.

7. The Momentum of Light

Light carries momentum p = E/c (per photon ħk = h/λ). A beam of power P absorbed by a surface pushes it with force P/c; if it is reflected straight back, 2P/c. These are tiny: 1 W gives 3.3 nN. The radiation pressure of sunlight at Earth (1.36 kW/m²) is 4.5 µPa, enough to steer spacecraft over months and to drive solar sails.

In a medium of index n the momentum per unit power is n/c (the Minkowski form; the competing Abraham form gives 1/(nc), and the "Abraham–Minkowski" controversy is resolved by accounting for the momentum carried by the medium itself). For optical forces on particles in water, n_m P/c is the natural unit, and force is written as

F = Q n_m P / c

where the dimensionless trapping efficiency Q (at most 2) tells what fraction of the beam's momentum is transferred. For P = 20 mW in water, n_m P/c = 89 pN, so Q = 0.1 gives about 9 pN: comparable to the forces that molecular motors and DNA polymerases produce.

8. Optical Tweezers: Ray Optics

An optical tweezer is a single laser beam focused by a high-NA microscope objective. A transparent bead with a higher index than the surrounding water is pulled towards the focus and held there in three dimensions (Ashkin, 1986).

For beads larger than the wavelength (a ≳ λ) the force follows from ray optics. Each ray refracts in and out of the sphere, and the change in its direction is a change in momentum, transferred to the bead. Splitting each ray's force into a scattering part along the ray and a gradient part perpendicular to it (Ashkin 1992):

F_s = (n₁P/c) [1 + R cos 2θ − T² (cos(2θ − 2r) + R cos 2θ) / (1 + R² + 2R cos 2r)]
F_g = (n₁P/c) [R sin 2θ − T² (sin(2θ − 2r) + R sin 2θ) / (1 + R² + 2R cos 2r)]

with θ the angle of incidence on the sphere, r the refraction angle, R and T the Fresnel reflectance and transmittance (averaged over s and p), and the series summing all internal reflections. The Optical tweezers tab sums these over about 900 rays filling the objective's aperture, each weighted by sin θ cos θ (uniform illumination of the pupil).

Why a bead is pulled into the light. Consider a bead to the right of the beam axis. The rays passing through its left side are the bright ones. Each is bent towards the right as it passes through the higher-index sphere (the sphere acts as a lens). The light gains momentum to the right, so the bead recoils to the left: towards the brighter region. A bead with lower index than the medium (an air bubble) bends rays the other way and is pushed out of the beam.

Axially, rays converging steeply from the side pull the bead back towards the focus (gradient force), while the axial momentum of the beam pushes it forwards (scattering force). A stable trap needs the backward gradient force to win somewhere, which is why a high-NA objective (NA ≥ 1.2, oil or water immersion) is used. The equilibrium sits slightly downstream of the focus. In the ray-optics regime the maximum force (the efficiency Q) does not depend on bead size, so the stiffness falls roughly as 1/a: a large bead is held as firmly but on a softer spring. Stiffness peaks for beads comparable in size to the focal spot.

9. Optical Tweezers: Small Particles

For a ≪ λ the bead acts as an induced dipole with polarisability α = 4πε₀ n_m² a³ (m² − 1)/(m² + 2), with m = n_bead/n_m. The forces separate cleanly:

F_grad = (2π n_m a³/c) ((m² − 1)/(m² + 2)) ∇I
F_scat = (n_m/c) σ I, σ = (128π⁵ a⁶ / 3λ_m⁴) ((m² − 1)/(m² + 2))²

The gradient force scales as a³ and the scattering force as a⁶, so very small particles are trapped easily but weakly. For a 100 nm polystyrene bead in a tightly focused beam the gradient force exceeds the scattering force by a large factor; for gold nanoparticles the polarisability is much larger and absorption adds to the scattering force. Between the two regimes (a ≈ λ, the most common case in biology) neither model is exact; full Mie or T-matrix calculations are needed, and both simple models are good to within a factor of about two.

The same gradient force, with light tuned slightly below an atomic resonance, makes the dipole traps that hold cold atoms; with the laser tuned above resonance, atoms are pushed out of the light. Laser cooling itself uses the scattering force.

10. Stiffness, Calibration and Uses

Near equilibrium the trap is a spring: F = −k x. Typical stiffnesses are 0.01–1 pN/nm. The bead jiggles thermally with rms amplitude √(k_BT/k) (20 nm for k = 0.01 pN/nm) and its motion in water is overdamped, with a corner frequency f_c = k/(2πγ), γ = 6πηa.

Trap stiffness is calibrated from that motion: the power spectrum of the bead position (measured with a quadrant photodiode) is a Lorentzian with corner frequency f_c; the equipartition theorem ⟨x²⟩ = k_BT/k gives k directly; or the stage is moved at known speed v and the bead displacement measured against the Stokes force γv. Displacement is then a force sensor with sub-pN resolution and nm position resolution.

11. The Relativistic Doppler Effect

A source moving with speed v = βc emits light of frequency ν₀ in its own frame. An observer who sees the light arrive at an angle θ from the direction of motion measures

ν = D ν₀, D = 1 / (γ (1 − β cos θ)), γ = 1/√(1 − β²)
Derivation. The source emits wave crests at intervals T₀ in its own frame, which the observer sees stretched to γT₀ by time dilation. Between crests the source moves vγT₀ and the component of this along the line of sight, vγT₀ cos θ, shortens the path of the next crest. The arrival interval is γT₀(1 − β cos θ), so ν = 1/(γ(1 − β cos θ)) · ν₀. The classical formula has the same (1 − β cos θ) factor but no γ.

Head-on (θ = 0), D = √((1 + β)/(1 − β)); straight away (θ = 180°), the inverse. At θ = 90° the classical shift vanishes but the relativistic one does not: D = 1/γ, a pure time-dilation redshift. Ives and Stilwell measured this transverse Doppler effect with fast hydrogen ions in 1938; today it is confirmed to parts in 10⁹ with stored ions and in the GPS system, whose satellite clocks run slow by 7 µs per day from their speed (and fast by 45 µs per day from the weaker gravity).

The redshift z = λ_obs/λ_emit − 1 is used throughout astronomy. For a source receding radially, 1 + z = √((1 + β)/(1 − β)). Cosmological redshifts of distant galaxies are caused by the expansion of space rather than by motion through space, and should not be converted to speeds with this formula for z ≳ 0.1.

12. Aberration and Relativistic Beaming

The direction from which light appears to come depends on the observer's velocity. A star at angle θ from the direction of motion (in the rest frame of the stars) is seen at θ′ with

cos θ′ = (cos θ + β) / (1 + β cos θ)

Everything shifts forwards. A star at θ = 90° appears at cos θ′ = β, so at β = 0.9 the whole forward hemisphere is squeezed into a cone of 25.8°. For Earth orbiting the Sun (β = 10⁻⁴) the effect is the annual aberration of up to 20.5″, discovered by Bradley in 1727 and the first direct evidence that light has a finite speed and the Earth moves.

At the same time, light arriving from ahead is Doppler-shifted to the blue by D (now with the observer moving: D = γ(1 + β cos θ) in terms of the rest-frame angle), and photons arrive faster and from a smaller solid angle. The specific intensity transforms as I_ν/ν³ = invariant, so the total brightness from a direction scales as D⁴: relativistic beaming. A thermal source at temperature T looks like a blackbody at DT. Ahead, the sky becomes a brilliant blue disc; behind, it fades to a dim red. (Science fiction's "stars streaking past" is wrong: the stars crowd forward.)

Beaming explains why relativistic jets from quasars look one-sided (the approaching jet is brightened by D⁴ or more, the receding one dimmed) and why synchrotron light from electron storage rings is emitted in a narrow cone of half-angle 1/γ.

13. Fizeau's Experiment

Does flowing water carry light with it? Fresnel proposed in 1818 that a moving medium drags light only partly, by a drag coefficient f:

u = c/n + f v, f = 1 − 1/n²

In 1851 Fizeau split a beam, sent the two halves in opposite directions through two tubes of flowing water, and recombined them. One beam travelled with the flow in both tubes, the other against it, so the fringe shift is

N = 4 L v n² f / (λ c)

With L = 1.49 m, v = 7.06 m/s, λ = 526 nm, n = 1.333 this predicts 0.20 fringe; Fizeau saw about 0.23, and Michelson and Morley (1886) confirmed Fresnel's coefficient to about 1%. Full drag (f = 1) would have given 0.47.

The relativistic explanation. In the water's rest frame light moves at c/n. Adding the water's speed with Einstein's velocity-addition rule: u = (c/n + v)/(1 + v/(nc)) ≈ (c/n + v)(1 − v/(nc)) ≈ c/n + v(1 − 1/n²). Fresnel's coefficient, introduced as a property of the ether, is simply relativistic velocity addition to first order in v/c. Including dispersion adds a small Lorentz term −(λ/n)(dn/dλ) to f, which Zeeman confirmed in 1915.

The same physics underlies the Sagnac effect used in fibre-optic and ring-laser gyroscopes: light going round a rotating loop in opposite directions acquires a phase difference proportional to the rotation rate and the enclosed area.

14. Light Bending by Gravity

In general relativity a light ray passing a mass M at impact parameter b is deflected by

α = 4GM / (c² b)

twice the value obtained by treating light as a Newtonian particle with speed c. The extra factor 2 comes from the curvature of space, which contributes as much as the "pull" of time dilation. For a ray grazing the Sun (b = R☉), α = 1.75″.

An optical picture. In a weak static field with Newtonian potential Φ (negative), light behaves as if it moved through a medium of refractive index n = 1 − 2Φ/c², larger near the mass. A ray bends towards higher index (Module 5's gradient-index optics), and integrating ∇n across the path of a ray passing a point mass gives exactly 4GM/(c²b). The same effective index slows light near the mass: the Shapiro delay, about 200 µs for radar echoes from Venus passing close to the Sun.

Eddington's 1919 eclipse expedition measured deflections near the solar limb consistent with 1.75″ rather than 0.87″. Modern radio interferometry and the Gaia satellite confirm the general-relativity value to better than 10⁻⁴; Gaia must correct star positions for the Sun's deflection even 90° away from it (4 mas).

15. Gravitational Lenses

A lens of mass M at distance D_L deflects light from a source at D_S. With all angles measured from the observer, the lens equation relates the true source position β to the image positions θ:

β = θ − θ_E² / θ (point mass), θ_E = √( (4GM/c²) · D_LS / (D_L D_S) )

θ_E is the Einstein radius. A source exactly behind the lens (β = 0) is seen as a ring of radius θ_E. Otherwise there are two images at θ± = (β ± √(β² + 4θ_E²))/2, one outside the ring on the source's side and one inside it on the opposite side. Lensing conserves surface brightness, so images are magnified in proportion to their stretched area; with u = β/θ_E the total magnification is

A(u) = (u² + 2) / (u √(u² + 4))

A source passing at u = 1 is brightened by 1.34; at u = 0.1, by 10. The two images also arrive at different times: the light paths differ in length and in Shapiro delay.

Lensθ_EWhat is seen
Star (0.3 M☉) in the Milky Way, 4 kpc≈ 0.5 masmicrolensing: unresolved images, a symmetric brightening lasting weeks

Galaxies are better modelled as singular isothermal spheres (mass growing linearly with radius, flat rotation curve), for which the deflection is constant: β = θ − θ_E θ/|θ|. Two images form only if β < θ_E, at β ± θ_E. Real lens modelling adds ellipticity and external shear, which produce the four-image "Einstein crosses".

Microlensing surveys (OGLE, MOA, KMTNet) monitor hundreds of millions of bulge stars. A planet orbiting the lens star adds a short spike to the light curve, a method that has found over 200 exoplanets, including cold planets and free-floating ones invisible to other techniques. Time delays between quasar images give an independent measurement of the Hubble constant.

17. Practice Problems

1. Is a cavity with R₁ = 0.5 m, R₂ = 2 m and L = 1 m stable?
g₁ = 1 − 2 = −1, g₂ = 0.5: g₁g₂ = −0.5 < 0. Unstable.
2. How many longitudinal modes of a 25 cm He–Ne laser fit under a 1.5 GHz gain width?
Δν = c/(2L) = 600 MHz; 1.5/0.6 = 2.5, so two or three modes oscillate depending on where they fall.
3. A Ti:sapphire laser with a 1.8 m cavity supports 60 THz of bandwidth. Estimate the repetition rate and the shortest pulse.
f_rep = c/(2L) = 83 MHz. τ ≈ 0.44/60 THz ≈ 7 fs (Gaussian, transform-limited).
4. What is the peak power of 10 fs pulses at 80 MHz with 1 W average power?
Energy per pulse 1/80 × 10⁶ = 12.5 nJ; P_peak ≈ 12.5 nJ/10 fs ≈ 1.25 MW.
5. A 50 mW beam is reflected straight back by a mirror. What force acts on the mirror?
F = 2P/c = 0.1/3 × 10⁸ = 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ N = 0.33 nN.
6. A trap has k = 0.05 pN/nm and holds a 1 µm diameter bead in water. Find the rms position fluctuation and the corner frequency.
√(4.1 × 10⁻²¹/5 × 10⁻⁵) = 9.1 nm. γ = 6π × 10⁻³ × 0.5 × 10⁻⁶ = 9.4 × 10⁻⁹ N s/m; f_c = 5 × 10⁻⁵/(2π × 9.4 × 10⁻⁹) ≈ 850 Hz.
7. How fast must a car approach a red light (650 nm) for it to look green (530 nm)?
√((1 + β)/(1 − β)) = 650/530 = 1.226 → (1 + β)/(1 − β) = 1.504 → β = 0.201, about 60 000 km/s.
8. A spaceship moves at β = 0.6. At what apparent angle does a star that is perpendicular to the motion (in the stars' frame) appear?
cos θ′ = β = 0.6 → θ′ = 53.1° from the direction of travel.
9. Find the Einstein radius of a 0.5 M☉ star at 5 kpc lensing a bulge star at 8 kpc.
4GM/c² = 2.95 km = 2950 m; D_LS/(D_L D_S) = 3/(5 × 8 kpc) = 0.075 kpc⁻¹ = 2.43 × 10⁻²¹ m⁻¹. θ_E = √(2950 × 2.43 × 10⁻²¹) = 2.68 × 10⁻⁹ rad = 0.55 mas.
10. A microlensing event peaks at a magnification of 5. What is the impact parameter u₀?
For large A, A ≈ 1/u, so u ≈ 0.2. Check: A(0.2) = (0.04 + 2)/(0.2 × √4.04) = 2.04/0.402 = 5.07. So u₀ ≈ 0.2.

19. Limits of the Models in This Module

Resonator modes: paraxial, scalar, ideal spherical mirrors of infinite size; no gain guiding, thermal lensing, astigmatism of tilted mirrors, or diffraction losses at finite apertures.

Longitudinal modes: a fixed Gaussian gain curve with each mode's amplitude set by the gain above threshold; no spatial hole burning, mode competition dynamics, dispersion or nonlinear pulse shaping. Locked phases are set exactly equal, so pulses are transform-limited.

Tweezers: the ray-optics model ignores diffraction (so it is only accurate for a ≳ 5λ) and treats the focus as a point; the Rayleigh model uses a paraxial Gaussian focus, which underestimates the axial gradient of a real high-NA focus. Spherical aberration at a glass–water interface, which weakens real traps with depth, is omitted. Neither model is correct at a ≈ λ, where Mie theory is needed.

Relativistic optics: uniform motion only (no acceleration); the star field is random with blackbody colours and bolometric D⁴ brightening, without the band-limited response of a real eye or camera. Fizeau's calculation omits dispersion.

Lensing: thin, axially symmetric point-mass and singular isothermal lenses with Euclidean distances (D_LS = D_S − D_L); cosmological angular-diameter distances, ellipticity, external shear, finite-source effects in microlensing and the lens galaxy's own light are not modelled.
