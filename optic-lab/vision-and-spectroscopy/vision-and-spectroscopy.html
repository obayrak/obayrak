Vision and Spectroscopy — Theory

Two of the oldest uses of optics: seeing and analysing light. The first half treats the eye as an optical instrument (its refractive errors, aberrations and sampling limits), colour vision and its deficiencies, and contrast sensitivity. The second half explains where spectral lines come from, why they have a width, how magnetic fields split them, and how a grating spectrometer measures them. Practice problems, history and the limits of the models close the chapter.

1. The Eye as an Optical System

The eye focuses light with two elements: the cornea (about 43 D, two thirds of the total, because the jump from air to tissue is largest there) and the crystalline lens (about 20 D, variable). Together they give about 60 D, focusing distant objects on the retina 24 mm behind the cornea (inside the eye, where n ≈ 1.336). The iris sets the pupil diameter between about 2 mm in bright light and 7–8 mm in darkness.

To focus near objects the ciliary muscle relaxes the zonules and the lens becomes rounder: accommodation. An object at distance s requires an extra 1/s dioptres (4 D at 25 cm). The lens stiffens with age; when accommodation falls below about 2–3 D (mid-forties) reading glasses are needed (presbyopia).

2. Refractive Errors and Their Correction

If the eye's power does not match its length, distant objects focus in front of the retina (myopia, eye too long or too strong) or behind it (hyperopia). The refractive error R in dioptres is the power of the spectacle lens (at the eye) that corrects it: R = −1/(far point distance). A −2 D myope sees sharply only out to 50 cm.

If the cornea is curved more in one meridian than in the other, the eye has two focal lengths: astigmatism. Vertical and horizontal lines blur differently, and a point is imaged as a line or an ellipse. A prescription has three numbers, e.g. −1.25 / −0.75 × 180: sphere, cylinder and the cylinder axis. The spherical equivalent S + C/2 places the circle of least confusion on the retina.

The angular blur of a defocused eye is the pupil diameter times the refractive error:

blur angle β ≈ d · |ΔD| (radians, with d in metres and ΔD in dioptres)

A 3 mm pupil with 1 D error gives β = 3 mrad ≈ 10′, the height of a 20/40 letter: the reason a −1 D myope reads only the top lines without glasses.

3. Aberrations of the Eye

Beyond defocus and astigmatism, real eyes have higher-order aberrations, measured clinically with a Shack–Hartmann aberrometer (Module 8) and expressed as Zernike polynomials over the pupil (normalised radius ρ, angle θ):

Zernike termFormTypical rms, 6 mm pupil
Defocus Z₂⁰√3 (2ρ² − 1)corrected by glasses
Astigmatism Z₂^±2√6 ρ² cos 2θ, sin 2θcorrected by glasses
Coma Z₃^±1√8 (3ρ³ − 2ρ) cos θ≈ 0.1–0.2 µm
Spherical aberration Z₄⁰√5 (6ρ⁴ − 6ρ² + 1)≈ +0.1 µm (more after LASIK)

The point-spread function is |FT{P(x, y) e^(2πiW/λ)}|², with P the pupil and W the wavefront error, exactly as for a telescope. The Strehl ratio compares its peak with the aberration-free peak; by the Maréchal criterion an eye is "diffraction-limited" when the rms wavefront error is below λ/14 ≈ 0.04 µm, which most eyes achieve only with pupils below about 3 mm.

Spherical aberration grows with the fourth power of the pupil radius, so at night (large pupils) it shifts best focus forwards and makes many people slightly myopic: night myopia of 0.5–1 D.

4. Pupil Size: Diffraction vs Aberration

A small pupil is limited by diffraction (Airy disc angular radius 1.22λ/d: 1.2′ at 2 mm), a large pupil by aberrations (which grow as d to d⁴). The best image quality is reached at about 2.5–3 mm, roughly the pupil in daylight. Very small pupils also increase depth of focus, because the geometric blur d·|ΔD| shrinks: the pinhole effect. Looking through a 1 mm hole lets an uncorrected myope read much better; optometrists use a pinhole to tell refractive blur from disease.

The retina itself helps: cones accept light best when it arrives along their axis, so rays through the pupil edge count less (the Stiles–Crawford effect), reducing the effective aberrations of large pupils.

5. Retinal Sampling and Visual Acuity

Foveal cones are spaced about 2.5 µm apart, i.e. 0.5′ of arc. By the Nyquist theorem the cone mosaic can represent gratings up to about 60 cycles per degree. With a 3 mm pupil the optics cut off at d/λ ≈ 95 c/deg, so the eye is nearly matched: optics and sampling limit fine detail together.

6. Colour Vision

Daylight vision uses three types of cones with overlapping sensitivities, peaking near 440 nm (S, "blue"), 535 nm (M, "green") and 565 nm (L, "red"). Each cone only counts absorbed photons; it cannot tell wavelength from intensity (the principle of univariance). Colour is therefore the ratio of three signals: trichromacy, the reason three primaries (RGB) can match most colours (Module 6). Lights with different spectra but the same three cone signals look identical: metamers.

The retina and brain recombine the signals into an achromatic channel (L + M) and two opponent channels, L − M (red–green) and S − (L + M) (blue–yellow). This explains why there is no "reddish green" or "yellowish blue", and why colour after-images are complementary. Rods, with a peak at 498 nm, take over at night (scotopic vision): they are colour-blind, which is why the world looks grey in moonlight, and blue objects look relatively brighter at dusk (the Purkinje shift).

7. Colour Vision Deficiencies

The genes for the L and M opsins lie on the X chromosome, so red–green defects are much more common in men.

A dichromat (two cone types) confuses all colours that differ only in the signal of the missing cone. In the CIE chromaticity diagram these colours lie on confusion lines that meet at a copunctal point. Pseudo-isochromatic plates (Ishihara, 1917) hide a figure in dots whose colours lie on such lines, with random lightness so that brightness gives no clue. The anomaloscope (Rayleigh match of red + green against yellow) measures the cone shift precisely.

The simulator uses the method of Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009): convert to linear RGB, apply a 3 × 3 matrix that projects onto the colours a dichromat can distinguish (or the shifted cone sensitivity for anomalous trichromats), and convert back. Designers use such simulations to choose colour-blind-safe palettes: avoid coding information by red versus green alone, and add lightness differences, symbols or patterns.

8. Contrast Sensitivity

For a sinusoidal grating of luminance L(x) = L̄[1 + C sin(2πfx)], the threshold contrast C_t is the smallest C at which the bars are just visible. The contrast sensitivity function S(f) = 1/C_t describes the whole visual system (optics, retina, brain) as a filter. In daylight it peaks at about 3–6 c/deg with S ≈ 200–300 (threshold 0.3–0.5%), and falls to S = 1 at about 40–60 c/deg, the grating acuity.

The fall at high frequencies comes from the optics (MTF) and cone sampling; the fall at low frequencies from lateral inhibition in the retina. The Campbell–Robson chart shows the function directly: frequency increases to the right, contrast decreases upwards, and the bars fade out along an inverted-U boundary.

In dim light the sensitivity drops and the peak moves to lower frequencies: at low luminance thresholds follow the de Vries–Rose law (C_t ∝ 1/√L, set by photon noise), at high luminance Weber's law (C_t constant). Blur from refractive error removes high frequencies first. Clinically, the CSF reveals early cataract, glaucoma and the effect of refractive surgery even when Snellen acuity is normal. The simulator's CSF uses the Mannos–Sakrison shape, scaled with luminance for illustration.

9. Atomic Energy Levels and Spectra

Electrons in atoms occupy discrete energy levels. A transition between levels E₂ > E₁ emits or absorbs a photon of energy hν = E₂ − E₁. For hydrogen, Bohr's model and quantum mechanics give

Eₙ = −13.6 eV / n², 1/λ = R_H (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), R_H = 1.0968 × 10⁷ m⁻¹
SeriesLower level n₁RegionFirst line, series limit
Lyman1ultraviolet121.6 nm, 91.2 nm
Balmer2visible656.3 nm (Hα), 364.6 nm
Paschen3infrared1875 nm, 820 nm

Multi-electron atoms have more complex level structures, but each element still has its own fingerprint of lines: sodium's yellow D doublet (589.0 and 589.6 nm, split by spin–orbit coupling), mercury's blue and green lines, neon's many red lines. Electric-dipole transitions obey selection rules (Δl = ±1, ΔJ = 0, ±1), which is why some levels are long-lived (metastable).

Kirchhoff's laws (1859): a hot dense body gives a continuous spectrum; a hot thin gas gives bright emission lines; a cooler gas in front of a hot continuum gives dark absorption lines at the same wavelengths.

10. Line Broadening

Spectral lines are not infinitely sharp. Three mechanisms dominate:

MechanismShapeWidth (FWHM)Typical value
Natural (lifetime τ of the upper level)LorentzianΔν_N = 1/(2πτ)10 MHz for τ = 16 ns (Na D)
Doppler (thermal motion)GaussianΔν_D = ν₀ √(8kT ln 2/(mc²))1.7 GHz (Na, 500 K); 10 GHz (H, 1000 K)
Pressure (collisions interrupt the emission)Lorentzian∝ collision rate ∝ P/√T≈ 10 GHz per atmosphere
Doppler width. Atoms moving with velocity component v along the line of sight emit at ν = ν₀(1 + v/c). The Maxwell distribution gives v a Gaussian with variance kT/m, so the line is Gaussian with standard deviation σ_ν = ν₀√(kT/(mc²)) and FWHM 2√(2 ln 2) σ_ν = ν₀√(8kT ln 2/(mc²)).

When Gaussian and Lorentzian broadening act together the line is their convolution, the Voigt profile: Gaussian in the core, Lorentzian in the far wings. In low-pressure gases Doppler broadening dominates; in high-pressure lamps, stellar atmospheres and the Earth's atmosphere, pressure broadening matters. Laser-cooled atoms (µK) and Doppler-free techniques (saturated absorption, two-photon spectroscopy) reveal the natural width, which is how hydrogen's levels are measured to 15 digits.

11. The Zeeman Effect

In a magnetic field B, a level with magnetic quantum number m_l shifts by ΔE = μ_B B m_l (μ_B = 9.27 × 10⁻²⁴ J/T). For a transition with Δm_l = 0, ±1 the line splits into three components (the normal Zeeman effect):

Δν = μ_B B/h = 14.0 GHz per tesla, Δλ = λ² μ_B B/(hc)

Viewed across the field, the central π component is polarised parallel to B and the two σ components perpendicular; viewed along the field, only the σ components appear, circularly polarised in opposite senses. Because electron spin also has a magnetic moment (with g ≈ 2), most lines show the anomalous Zeeman effect with more components, described by the Landé g-factor.

At 1 T the splitting at 546 nm is 0.02 nm, smaller than the Doppler width of a hot lamp: it needs a cold source or a high-resolution instrument. Hale discovered strong magnetic fields (0.1–0.3 T) in sunspots in 1908 this way; today solar magnetographs map the Sun's fields daily, and Zeeman splitting underlies atomic clocks, magnetometers and magneto-optical atom traps.

12. Fraunhofer Lines and Astronomical Spectroscopy

In 1814 Fraunhofer mapped hundreds of dark lines in sunlight and labelled the strongest with letters still used today: A and B (oxygen in the Earth's atmosphere), C (Hα), D (sodium), E (iron), F (Hβ), H and K (ionised calcium). Kirchhoff and Bunsen showed in 1859 that the D lines coincide with sodium's emission: the Sun's atmosphere contains sodium. Helium was discovered in the solar spectrum in 1868, 27 years before it was found on Earth.

A star's spectrum reveals its composition, temperature (from which lines are strong: the OBAFGKM sequence), pressure and surface gravity (from line widths), rotation (Doppler broadening), magnetic fields (Zeeman effect) and motion (Doppler shift). Measuring tiny periodic Doppler shifts of a few m/s, with spectrometers calibrated by frequency combs (Module 10), revealed the first planets around Sun-like stars (1995); redshifts of galaxies revealed the expansion of the Universe.

13. The Grating Spectrometer

A grating with groove spacing d diffracts light of wavelength λ in order m at angle β given by the grating equation:

m λ = d (sin α + sin β)

In a Czerny–Turner spectrometer a concave mirror collimates light from the entrance slit, the grating disperses it, and a second mirror (focal length f) images the slit onto a detector, one image per wavelength. Rotating the grating selects the centre wavelength.

QuantityFormula
Angular dispersiondβ/dλ = m/(d cos β)
Reciprocal linear dispersiondλ/dx = d cos β/(m f)
Slit-limited bandpassΔλ_s ≈ w · dλ/dx (slit width w, unit magnification)
Diffraction-limited resolutionλ/Δλ = mN (N grooves illuminated)
Free spectral rangeΔλ_FSR = λ/m

The resolving power mN is the ultimate limit, reached only with a narrow slit; usually the slit image sets the resolution, and opening the slit trades resolution for light. Because mλ is what matters, a line at λ/2 in second order lands at the same place as λ in first order: order overlap, removed with order-sorting filters. Blazed gratings, with grooves tilted like tiny mirrors, send most light into one chosen order. Échelle spectrographs use coarse gratings at high orders (m ≈ 50–100) with a second disperser to separate the orders, reaching λ/Δλ ≈ 10⁵ in a compact instrument.

Worked example: can a 1200 /mm grating, 30 mm wide, resolve the sodium doublet in first order? What slit is needed with f = 0.3 m?
N = 36 000, so λ/Δλ = 36 000 and Δλ = 589/36 000 = 0.016 nm ≪ 0.597 nm: easily, in principle. Dispersion: dλ/dx = d cos β/(mf) ≈ 833 nm × 0.99/300 mm ≈ 2.7 nm/mm. To keep the slit bandpass below 0.3 nm needs w < 0.3/2.7 mm ≈ 110 µm.

15. Practice Problems

1. A myope's far point is 40 cm. What lens corrects her distance vision?
R = −1/0.40 m = −2.5 D.
2. A presbyope with 1 D of accommodation wants to read at 33 cm. What reading addition is needed?
Required 1/0.33 = 3 D; add 3 − 1 = +2 D.
3. Estimate the blur angle for a 5 mm pupil and 0.75 D of uncorrected myopia.
β = 0.005 × 0.75 = 3.75 mrad = 12.9′, more than a 20/40 letter.
4. What is the Airy-disc radius for a 2.5 mm pupil at 555 nm, in arcminutes?
1.22 × 555 × 10⁻⁹/0.0025 = 2.71 × 10⁻⁴ rad = 0.93′.
5. Calculate the wavelength of Hβ (n = 4 → 2).
1/λ = 1.0968 × 10⁷ (1/4 − 1/16) = 2.0565 × 10⁶ m⁻¹ → λ = 486.3 nm (486.1 nm in air).
6. Find the Doppler width (FWHM) of the Hg 546 nm line at 1000 K.
Δν = ν₀√(8kT ln 2/(mc²)) with m = 200.6 u: √(8 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 1000 × 0.693/(3.33 × 10⁻²⁵ × 9 × 10¹⁶)) = 1.6 × 10⁻⁶; ν₀ = 5.49 × 10¹⁴ Hz → 880 MHz ≈ 0.87 pm.
7. What magnetic field makes the normal Zeeman splitting of a 600 nm line equal to 0.01 nm?
Δν = cΔλ/λ² = 3 × 10⁸ × 10⁻¹¹/(3.6 × 10⁻¹³) = 8.3 GHz → B = 8.3/14.0 = 0.6 T.
8. A 600 /mm grating has a 50 mm illuminated width. What is its resolving power in second order?
N = 30 000, R = 2 × 30 000 = 60 000: Δλ = 0.01 nm at 600 nm.
9. In first order, which second-order line falls at the same position as 810 nm?
Any line at 405 nm (e.g. the mercury 404.7 nm line). A long-pass filter above ≈ 450 nm removes it.
10. Why do protanopes see red traffic lights as dim?
Without L cones, long-wavelength light is absorbed only by M cones, whose sensitivity is low above 620 nm. Red lights look darker and brownish; they are recognised by position (top) rather than colour.

17. Limits of the Models in This Module

Eye: monochromatic (555 nm) Fourier optics with a circular pupil, defocus, astigmatism and primary spherical aberration only; no chromatic aberration, scatter, Stiles–Crawford weighting, retinal sampling or neural processing; the acuity estimate is a rule of thumb from the encircled energy. Colour: simulation matrices for an average observer on an sRGB display; cone curves are Gaussian approximations. Contrast sensitivity: illustrative model, not fitted to data. Spectra: selected visible lines with approximate relative intensities; generic pressure-broadening coefficient; normal Zeeman triplet only. Spectrometer: ideal aberration-free optics with a fixed 24° included angle; the instrument profile is approximated by a Gaussian of combined width; grating efficiency of other orders set to 30%.
