Physics Details

This simulator combines thin-optics image equations, the lensmaker equation, Snell's law, and basic instrument formulas for microscopes and telescopes.

1. Imaging Equation

1 / f = 1 / s + 1 / s'

f is focal length, s is object distance, and s' is image distance. In this lab the real-is-positive convention is used: real objects and real images are positive, while virtual quantities are negative.

2. Magnification

m = -s' / s

The image height is h' = m h. A negative magnification means the image is inverted. A positive magnification means the image is upright.

3. Mirror Geometry

R = 2 f

Concave mirrors are converging mirrors and have positive focal length. Convex mirrors are diverging mirrors and have negative focal length. The center of curvature lies at twice the focal distance.

4. Lensmaker Equation

Phi = (n_rel - 1) [ (1 / R1) - (1 / R2) + ((n_rel - 1) d) / (n_rel R1 R2) ]

The optical power is Phi = 1 / f, n_rel = n / n_m, and d is center thickness. For a convex lens in left-to-right light travel, R1 > 0 and R2 < 0. For a concave lens the signs reverse.

5. Lens Refraction

n1 sin(theta1) = n2 sin(theta2)

This is Snell's law. In the aberration tab, each ray is intersected with the spherical surfaces and refracted with the full vector form of Snell's law. That lets the model show non-paraxial behavior directly.

6. Spherical Aberration

Rays close to the lens edge are refracted more strongly than paraxial rays. That shifts their focus and produces a focus spread instead of a single exact image point.

The simulator reports longitudinal spherical aberration (difference in focus position) and transverse aberration (ray height error in a chosen reference plane).

7. Microscope Physics

The objective creates a real, enlarged intermediate image. The eyepiece then acts as a magnifier for that intermediate image.

Approximate total angular magnification: M_total approx m_obj (D / u_e), where D = 25 cm is the standard near-point reference and u_e is the intermediate image distance from the eyepiece.

8. Microscope Resolution

NA = n sin(alpha)

delta approx 0.61 lambda / NA

A larger numerical aperture and a shorter wavelength improve resolution. The app estimates the Rayleigh limit in micrometers.

9. Telescope Physics

M_theta approx -f_obj / f_eye

A Keplerian telescope uses a positive eyepiece and produces an inverted view. A Galilean telescope uses a negative eyepiece and produces an upright view. The normal adjustment length is f_obj + f_eye, where the eyepiece focal length keeps its algebraic sign.

10. Limits of the Model

Mirrors and the imaging tabs use paraxial thin-optics equations, so they are most accurate for small angles and moderate apertures. The aberration tab is more physical because it uses direct refraction at spherical surfaces, but it still assumes a 2D meridional slice and ideal homogeneous materials.

Manual

How to Use the Lab

Pick a tab, move the sliders, and watch the numbers and diagrams update together. The left panel controls the system. The right side shows the calculated quantities and the 2D/3D visualizations.

Suggested Mirror Experiments

Set the concave mirror object at 2f to get an equal-size inverted image. Move it inside f to see the real image disappear and a virtual upright image appear behind the mirror. Switch to the convex mirror to compare the always-virtual case.

Suggested Lens Experiments

Increase the refractive index or reduce the curvature radii to strengthen the lens and shorten the focal length. Compare convex and concave lenses with the same absolute curvature values and note the sign change in focal length and image distance.

Suggested Aberration Experiments

Keep the material fixed and increase the aperture. Marginal rays should shift farther from the paraxial focus. Then try a plano-convex or meniscus preset to compare how lens shape changes aberration behavior.

Microscope Guide

Keep the object just outside the objective focal length so the objective forms a large real intermediate image. Then tune the eyepiece focal length and separation to place the final image comfortably at infinity or at a finite virtual distance.

Telescope Guide

For a Keplerian telescope, set the separation near f_obj + f_eye for normal adjustment. For a Galilean telescope, the correct separation is shorter because the eyepiece focal length is negative.

Reading the Diagrams

Solid rays are physical light paths. Dashed rays are backward extensions used to locate virtual images. In the 3D views, the wireframe surfaces are perspective schematics that help you see the optic shape and beam direction.

Practical Notes

When the object approaches the focal point, the image can move very far away and the magnification can become very large. That is expected behavior from the imaging equation, not a bug.
