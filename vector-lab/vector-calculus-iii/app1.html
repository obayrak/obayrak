Gradient  ∇f

The gradient generalises the one-dimensional derivative to scalar fields in any number of dimensions. At every point P, it produces a vector that points in the direction of steepest ascent of f, with magnitude equal to the rate of change in that direction.

∇f = (∂f/∂x) î + (∂f/∂y) ĵ + (∂f/∂z) k̂
|∇f| = √[ (∂f/∂x)² + (∂f/∂y)² + (∂f/∂z)² ]
Directional derivative:  D_û f = ∇f · û
Geometric interpretation

∇f is always perpendicular to level surfaces f = constant. Moving along ∇f gives maximum ascent; −∇f gives steepest descent. The directional derivative in any unit direction û is simply the dot product ∇f · û — so ∇f compactly encodes all directional derivatives simultaneously.

Coordinate systems
Cartesian:  ∇f = ∂f/∂x î + ∂f/∂y ĵ + ∂f/∂z k̂
Cylindrical:  ∇f = ∂f/∂r r̂ + (1/r)∂f/∂φ φ̂ + ∂f/∂z ẑ
Spherical:  ∇f = ∂f/∂r r̂ + (1/r)∂f/∂θ θ̂ + (1/r sinθ)∂f/∂φ φ̂

Divergence  ∇·F

The divergence measures how much a vector field F spreads out from or converges toward a point. It is a scalar — the rate of outward flux per unit volume in the limit of vanishing volume.

∇·F = ∂Fₓ/∂x + ∂F_y/∂y + ∂F_z/∂z

Integral definition:  ∇·F(P) = lim_{V→0} (1/|V|) ∯_{∂V} F·dA
(outward flux per unit volume at point P)
Physical intuition — the fluid picture

Imagine F as the velocity field of a compressible fluid. Then:

∇·F > 0
Source — fluid is being created here. Field lines diverge outward.
∇·F < 0
Sink — fluid is being removed here. Field lines converge inward.
∇·F = 0
Solenoidal / incompressible. No sources or sinks. Conservation holds locally.
∇·F = 0 everywhere
F = ∇×A for some vector potential A (de Rham's theorem).
Maxwell's equations — Gauss laws
∇·E = ρ/ε₀     (Gauss's law — electric sources are charges)
∇·B = 0        (no magnetic monopoles exist)
∇·g = −4πGρ (Gauss's law for gravity)
∇·J = −∂ρ_e/∂t (charge continuity equation)
Gauss's Divergence Theorem
∯_{∂V} F · dA = ∫∫∫_V (∇·F) dV

Total outward flux through closed surface S = ∂V
equals total divergence integrated over enclosed volume V.

This theorem bridges the local divergence to global flux measurements — the core tool for deriving all conservation laws in classical and quantum field theory.

Curl  ∇×F  (Rotation)

The curl measures the local rotation or vorticity of a vector field at each point. The resulting vector's direction is the instantaneous axis of rotation (by the right-hand rule), and its magnitude is twice the local angular velocity.

∇×F = | î    ĵ    k̂ |
         | ∂/∂x  ∂/∂y  ∂/∂z |
         | Fₓ    F_y   F_z |

x-component: ∂F_z/∂y − ∂F_y/∂z
y-component: ∂Fₓ/∂z − ∂F_z/∂x
z-component: ∂F_y/∂x − ∂Fₓ/∂y
The paddle-wheel test

Place a tiny frictionless paddle wheel in the field F with its axis pointing in direction n̂. The wheel spins if and only if the component of ∇×F along n̂ is nonzero, with angular velocity ω = (∇×F · n̂)/2. The wheel spins fastest when n̂ ∥ ∇×F.

Conservative vs rotational fields
∇×F = 0 everywhere in simply-connected domain
   ⟺ F = ∇φ for some scalar potential φ (F is conservative)
   ⟺ ∮_C F·dl = 0 for every closed curve C (path independence)
   ⟺ ∫_A^B F·dl depends only on A and B, not the path
Maxwell's equations — Faraday & Ampère
∇×E = −∂B/∂t           (Faraday induction)
∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t  (Ampère–Maxwell)
∇×v = 2ω                (fluid vorticity)
∇×A = B                 (magnetic vector potential)
Stokes' theorem
∮_{∂S} F · dl = ∫∫_S (∇×F) · dA

Circulation around closed curve C = ∂S
equals flux of curl through any surface S bounded by C.

Vector Identities
Fundamental null identities
∇×(∇f) = 0       — curl of any gradient is identically zero
∇·(∇×F) = 0      — divergence of any curl is identically zero

These are consequences of the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives (Schwarz's theorem).
They are the foundation of gauge theory and electromagnetic potentials.
First-order product rules
∇(fg) = f∇g + g∇f
∇·(fF) = f(∇·F) + F·(∇f)
∇×(fF) = f(∇×F) + (∇f)×F
∇·(F×G) = G·(∇×F) − F·(∇×G)
∇×(F×G) = F(∇·G) − G(∇·F) + (G·∇)F − (F·∇)G
∇(F·G) = F×(∇×G) + G×(∇×F) + (F·∇)G + (G·∇)F
Second-order operators
∇²f = ∇·(∇f) = ∂²f/∂x² + ∂²f/∂y² + ∂²f/∂z²    (scalar Laplacian)
∇²F = ∇(∇·F) − ∇×(∇×F)                   (vector Laplacian)
∇×(∇×F) = ∇(∇·F) − ∇²F                   (curl-curl identity)
∇²(fg) = f∇²g + 2∇f·∇g + g∇²f             (Leibniz rule for ∇²)
Green's identities
Green's first:
∫_V f∇²g dV = ∮_{∂V} f(∇g)·dA − ∫_V ∇f·∇g dV

Green's second (reciprocity):
∫_V (f∇²g − g∇²f) dV = ∮_{∂V} (f∇g − g∇f)·dA

Derived by applying the divergence theorem to fF = f∇g. Green's second identity
is the basis for boundary element methods and Green's function solutions to PDEs.
Helmholtz decomposition
Any smooth, rapidly decaying vector field F can be written as:

F = −∇φ + ∇×A

where φ is the scalar potential (irrotational part)
and A is the vector potential (solenoidal part).

This is the vector analogue of Fourier decomposition. In electrostatics:
E = −∇V (irrotational), B = ∇×A (solenoidal).
Fundamental Theorems

All three major integral theorems share a single deep structure: the integral of a differential operator over a domain equals the integral of a related quantity over the domain's boundary. This is captured by the generalised Stokes theorem: ∫_M dω = ∮_{∂M} ω, where ω is a differential form on an oriented manifold M.

1 — Gradient theorem
∫_A^B ∇f · dl = f(B) − f(A)

1D analogue: ∫_a^b f'(x)dx = f(b) − f(a)  (Fundamental Theorem of Calculus)
Boundary operator: endpoints — 0-dimensional boundary of a curve.

Path independence: the integral of a conservative field depends only on the start and end points. Physical application — work done by gravity, electrostatic force, or any conservative force depends only on displacement, not trajectory.

2 — Gauss's Divergence Theorem
∯_{∂V} F · dA = ∫∫∫_V (∇·F) dV

Boundary operator: ∂V is the closed surface bounding volume V.
Dimension jump: 3D volume → 2D closed surface.

Applications: derives Maxwell's Gauss laws from field equations, proves the continuity equation ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·J = 0, underpins the finite volume method in CFD. The flux through any closed surface tells you the total source strength inside.

3 — Stokes' Theorem
∮_{∂S} F · dl = ∫∫_S (∇×F) · dA

Boundary operator: ∂S is the closed curve bounding surface S.
Dimension jump: 2D surface → 1D closed curve.

Applications: derives Faraday's induction law, Ampère's law. The classic proof of the magnetic Gauss law ∇·B = 0 uses Stokes' theorem together with ∇·(∇×A) = 0.

de Rham cohomology connection

A form ω is closed if dω = 0; exact if ω = dα for some α. Every exact form is closed (d² = 0), but in a non-simply-connected domain, closed forms may not be exact. The de Rham cohomology groups H^k(M) = ker(d)/im(d) classify the topological obstructions — the "holes" that prevent closed forms from being exact. This is the deep reason why ∮ B·dl ≠ 0 around a solenoid even though ∇×B = 0 outside it.

Getting started

This simulator computes and renders gradient, divergence, and curl numerically using central finite differences (O(h²) accuracy, h = 10⁻⁴), displayed as 3D arrow fields via Three.js. All mathematical expressions are parsed live by math.js.

Quick steps
1  Choose operation
Select ∇f, ∇·F, or ∇×F using the three buttons at the top of the sidebar.
2  Enter function(s)
Type your scalar f(x,y,z) or vector components Fₓ, F_y, F_z in the input fields.
3  Adjust parameters
Set resolution, domain, arrow scale, opacity, and magnitude clip to taste.
4  Compute & Render
Press the teal Compute button. Explore the field by dragging, scrolling, and right-dragging.

Expressions like 1/r blow up at the origin. Add a small regularisation constant:

r = sqrt(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + 0.01)  ← regularised
1/r² → 1/(x^2+y^2+z^2+0.01)
