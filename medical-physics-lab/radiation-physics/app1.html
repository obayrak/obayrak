Radiation Sources

The origin of ionizing radiation: how it is produced, its typical energy range, and the physical process responsible for its emission.

01

X-rays

High-energy electromagnetic radiation produced by electron transitions in the inner shells of atoms or by bremsstrahlung when fast electrons are decelerated. Used in imaging, security screening, and crystallography.

Bremsstrahlung (continuous spectrum)

Electrons accelerated through a tube voltage V strike a target and decelerate in the Coulomb field of target nuclei, radiating photons of any energy up to a sharp maximum set by the electron's kinetic energy. This produces the continuous part of the spectrum, cut off at a minimum wavelength known as the Duane–Hunt limit:

$$\lambda_{\min}\,[\text{pm}] = \dfrac{1239.84}{V\,[\text{kV}]}$$

A higher tube voltage pushes the cutoff to shorter wavelength (harder X-rays) and increases the total emitted intensity.

Characteristic X-rays (line spectrum)

If an incident electron carries enough energy to eject an inner K-shell electron, an outer electron falls into the vacancy and emits a photon at an energy fixed by the target element. Moseley's law gives the approximate scaling with atomic number Z:

$$E_{K\alpha} \approx 10.2\ \text{eV} \times (Z-1)^2$$

These sharp Kα and Kβ lines sit on top of the bremsstrahlung continuum and are unique to the target material — the basis of X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis. They only appear once the tube voltage exceeds the corresponding excitation energy.

Attenuation in matter

Passing through a material, X-ray intensity falls off exponentially with the linear attenuation coefficient μ, which depends on photon energy and the material's composition and density:

$$I(x) = I_0\, e^{-\mu x}$$

02

Gamma rays

Electromagnetic radiation of nuclear origin, emitted when an unstable nucleus relaxes from an excited state. Typically the highest-energy, shortest-wavelength photons in the radiation spectrum.

Nuclear transitions

Unlike X-rays, which come from atomic electron processes, gamma rays originate inside the nucleus. After alpha or beta decay (or fission, or a nuclear reaction), the daughter nucleus is often left in an excited state and relaxes to a lower energy level by emitting a photon whose energy is fixed by the gap between nuclear levels:

$$E_\gamma = E_i - E_f$$

Because nuclear energy levels are discrete, gamma spectra are sharp lines rather than a continuum — and those line energies are a fingerprint of the specific nuclide. Cobalt-60, for example, always emits a 1.17 MeV photon immediately followed by a 1.33 MeV photon as it cascades down to stable nickel-60.

Radioactive decay law

The rate at which a gamma-emitting source decays — and therefore how its activity falls over time — follows simple exponential decay:

$$N(t) = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad \lambda = \dfrac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}}$$

where λ is the decay constant and T1/2 is the half-life. Activity is A = λN, measured in becquerels (decays per second).

Annihilation radiation

A related 511 keV gamma line appears whenever a positron meets an electron: the pair annihilates into two photons emitted back-to-back to conserve momentum, each carrying the electron rest-mass energy. This is the physical basis of PET imaging.

$$e^{+} + e^{-} \rightarrow 2\gamma, \qquad E_\gamma = m_e c^2 = 511\ \text{keV each}$$

03

Alpha particles

Helium-4 nuclei (two protons, two neutrons) emitted by heavy unstable nuclei. Highly ionizing but stopped by a sheet of paper or a few centimeters of air due to their short range.

Alpha decay

An alpha particle — a bare helium-4 nucleus — is emitted when a heavy nucleus tunnels through the Coulomb barrier that would classically confine it. The decay conserves baryon number and charge, reducing the parent by 4 in mass number and 2 in atomic number:

$${}^A_Z X \;\longrightarrow\; {}^{A-4}_{Z-2} Y \;+\; {}^4_2\text{He}$$

The energy released (Q-value) is shared between the alpha and the recoiling daughter nucleus by conservation of momentum. Because the daughter is always much heavier, the alpha carries away almost all of the kinetic energy and its energy is essentially fixed — alpha spectra are discrete lines, not a continuum.

$$Q = \bigl[M(X) - M(Y) - M(\alpha)\bigr]c^2, \qquad T_\alpha \approx Q\,\frac{M_Y}{M_Y + M_\alpha}$$

Quantum tunneling and the Geiger–Nuttall law

Inside the nucleus the alpha is bound by the strong force; outside it is repelled by the Coulomb potential. Classical mechanics forbids escape, but quantum mechanics permits tunneling through the barrier with an exponentially small probability that falls steeply with barrier width — and therefore with alpha energy. The empirical Geiger–Nuttall relation captures this extreme sensitivity:

$$\log_{10} t_{1/2} \;\approx\; \frac{a\,Z}{\sqrt{E_\alpha}} + b$$

where a and b are empirical constants for each decay series. A factor-of-two change in alpha energy can shift the half-life by more than twenty orders of magnitude — from microseconds (²²Po, 8.78 MeV) to 4.5 billion years (²³⁸U, 4.27 MeV) — an astonishing consequence of quantum tunneling.

Energy loss in matter — Bethe–Bloch and the Bragg peak

Because alpha particles carry charge +2 and are relatively slow for their mass, they interact intensely with atomic electrons through continuous Coulomb collisions. The mean stopping power is described by the Bethe–Bloch formula:

$$-\frac{dE}{dx} \;\propto\; \frac{z^2}{\beta^2}\,Z_t\;\ln\!\left(\frac{2m_e c^2\beta^2}{I(1-\beta^2)}\right)$$

where z = 2 is the projectile charge, β = v/c its speed, and I the mean excitation energy of the target. The 1/β² factor means the stopping power rises as the particle slows, culminating in a sharp spike — the Bragg peak — just before it comes to rest. Beyond the peak the particle captures electrons, the effective charge falls to zero, and the dose drops abruptly. This is the physics exploited by hadron therapy to concentrate dose at tumour depth while sparing surrounding tissue.

04

Beta particles

High-speed electrons (β⁻) or positrons (β⁺) emitted during nuclear beta decay, when a neutron converts to a proton or vice versa. More penetrating than alpha particles, stopped by a few millimeters of aluminum.

β⁻ and β⁺ decay

Beta decay converts a neutron into a proton (β⁻) or a proton into a neutron (β⁺) inside the nucleus. In both cases a neutrino or antineutrino is emitted alongside the beta particle to conserve lepton number:

$$\beta^-:\quad {}^A_Z X \;\longrightarrow\; {}^{\;A}_{Z+1} Y \;+\; e^- \;+\; \bar{\nu}_e$$
$$\beta^+:\quad {}^A_Z X \;\longrightarrow\; {}^{\;A}_{Z-1} Y \;+\; e^+ \;+\; \nu_e$$

A competing process for β⁺ is electron capture (EC): an inner-shell atomic electron is absorbed by a proton, producing only a neutrino and characteristic X-rays as the vacancy refills. EC becomes increasingly probable for proton-rich heavy nuclei where the Q-value is insufficient to support positron emission (requires Q > 1.022 MeV).

Continuous energy spectrum — Fermi theory

Unlike alpha decay, which is a two-body process with a unique alpha energy, beta decay distributes Q among three products: the daughter nucleus, the β particle, and the (anti)neutrino. Energy is shared continuously, so the β spectrum is a broad distribution from zero up to the endpoint energy Emax:

$$\frac{dN}{dE} \;\propto\; F(Z',E)\;p\;(E + m_e c^2)\;(E_{\max} - E)^2$$

Here $p = c^{-1}\!\sqrt{(E+m_ec^2)^2-(m_ec^2)^2}$ is the relativistic electron momentum and $F(Z', E)$ is the Fermi function, a Coulomb correction that enhances the spectrum at low energies for β⁻ (nuclear attraction) and suppresses it for β⁺ (nuclear repulsion). The mean energy of the emitted electron is roughly $\langle E\rangle \approx E_{\max}/3$; the exact endpoint equals the Q-value of the transition minus any excitation energy left in the daughter nucleus.

Range in matter — Katz–Penfold formula

Electrons are 7300× lighter than protons, so they undergo violent large-angle scattering and follow tortuous, branching paths through matter. Range is therefore a statistical quantity with large straggling. The empirical Katz–Penfold formula gives the practical (CSDA) range as an areal density independent of material:

$$R\;[\text{g\,cm}^{-2}] \approx 0.412\;E_{\max}^{\;n}, \qquad n = 1.265 - 0.0954\ln E_{\max} \quad (E_{\max}\text{ in MeV})$$

Dividing by the material density ρ gives the linear range in cm. Unlike heavy charged particles, there is no Bragg peak for electrons: the dose–depth profile falls off roughly monotonically. At high energies an increasingly important fraction of the kinetic energy is also lost via bremsstrahlung — the ratio of radiative to collisional loss is approximately $E_{\max}\,[\text{MeV}] \times Z / 800$.

Neutrons

Uncharged nucleons released by fission, fusion, or spallation reactions. Lacking charge, they interact mainly through nuclear collisions rather than electromagnetic forces, giving them very different shielding requirements.

Nuclear fission — the dominant large-scale neutron source

When a fissile nucleus absorbs a neutron (induced fission) or splits spontaneously (spontaneous fission), it releases an average of two to four prompt neutrons within 10⁻¹⁴ s of the split, plus a small delayed fraction from excited fission products:

$$n \;+\; {}^{235}_{92}\text{U} \;\longrightarrow\; {}^{A_1}_{Z_1}X \;+\; {}^{A_2}_{Z_2}Y \;+\; \bar{\nu}\,n \;+\; {\sim}200\ \text{MeV}$$

The mean prompt multiplicity is $\bar{\nu} \approx 2.43$ for ²³⁵U thermal fission, 2.88 for ²³⁸U fast fission, and 3.76 for ²⁵²Cf spontaneous fission. The resulting neutron energy spectrum is described by the Watt fission spectrum, which peaks near 0.7 MeV and has a mean energy of roughly 2 MeV:

$$\chi(E) = C\,e^{-E/a}\sinh\!\sqrt{b\,E} \qquad a \approx 0.965\ \text{MeV},\quad b \approx 2.29\ \text{MeV}^{-1}\ ({}^{235}\text{U})$$

Sealed ²⁵²Cf sources (microgram quantities, activity ~10⁶–10⁹ n/s) are the most widely used portable laboratory neutron sources precisely because spontaneous fission requires no accelerator infrastructure.

(α, n) reactions — compact sealed sources

When alpha particles emitted by a radioactive nuclide impinge on a low-Z target material, they can overcome the Coulomb barrier and trigger neutron emission. The classic combination is ²⁴¹Am (or ²¹⁰Po, ²²⁶Ra) intimately mixed with beryllium powder — the lowest-Z practical target:

$$\alpha \;+\; {}^9_4\text{Be} \;\longrightarrow\; {}^{12}_6\text{C}^* \;\longrightarrow\; {}^{12}_6\text{C} \;+\; n \;+\; 5.71\ \text{MeV}$$

An Am-Be source converts roughly one alpha in every 10⁴ decays into a neutron, yielding a broad 0–12 MeV spectrum and about $2.2 \times 10^6\ \text{n s}^{-1}\ \text{GBq}^{-1}$ of ²⁴¹Am. Other (α,n) targets include ⁷Li and ¹¹B in compact accelerator-driven neutron generators. The reaction cross-section grows steeply with alpha energy and falls with increasing target Z (higher Coulomb barrier), so light elements are always preferred.

Fusion reactions and spallation — highest-intensity sources

Fusion of hydrogen isotopes is the energetically efficient route to a single fast neutron. The D-T reaction has the largest cross-section of any thermonuclear reaction near keV energies and is used in compact sealed-tube neutron generators, tokamaks, and inertial confinement targets:

$$\text{D} + \text{T} \;\longrightarrow\; {}^4\text{He}\;(3.52\ \text{MeV}) \;+\; n\;(14.1\ \text{MeV})$$
$$\text{D} + \text{D} \;\longrightarrow\; {}^3\text{He}\;(0.82\ \text{MeV}) \;+\; n\;(2.45\ \text{MeV}) \quad [50\%\ \text{branch}]$$

At the very highest intensities, GeV-class protons strike heavy-metal targets (liquid Hg at SNS/ORNL; W or Pb elsewhere) triggering an intranuclear cascade that evaporates 20–30 neutrons per incident proton — roughly ten times more efficient per watt than fission. Spallation facilities (SNS, ISIS, ESS) deliver the world's brightest pulsed neutron beams for condensed-matter science and fundamental physics.

Moderation and neutron energy classification

Fast neutrons are slowed through repeated elastic collisions with moderator nuclei. The key transport quantity is the mean logarithmic energy decrement per collision:

$$\xi = 1 + \frac{(A-1)^2}{2A}\ln\!\frac{A-1}{A+1}$$

For hydrogen ($A=1$), $\xi = 1$ — a neutron can lose all its kinetic energy in a single head-on collision, which is why water and polyethylene are such effective fast-neutron shields. The average number of collisions to slow from fission energies down to thermal ($\sim\!25\ \text{meV}$) is $n_\text{coll} = \ln(E_0/E_\text{th})/\xi \approx 19$ for water but $\approx 115$ for graphite. Neutrons are conventionally grouped by kinetic energy:

  • Thermal (< 0.5 eV) — in near-equilibrium with the moderator; $E_\text{th} = k_BT \approx 25.3\ \text{meV}$ at 20 °C; cross-sections follow the $1/v$ law
  • Epithermal (0.5 eV – 100 keV) — dominated by narrow resonance absorption in ²³⁸U, ¹¹³Cd, ¹¹⁵In and many other heavy nuclides
  • Fast (> 100 keV) — produced directly by fission or fusion; primarily elastic and inelastic nuclear scattering; require moderation before efficient capture

Radiation Interaction

How radiation exchanges energy with matter once it reaches a target — the physics that governs attenuation, shielding, and dose deposition.

01

Photoelectric Effect

A photon is completely absorbed by a bound atomic electron, which is then ejected. Dominant at low photon energies and in high-atomic-number materials, scaling roughly as Z⁴–Z⁵.

Einstein's photoelectric equation — the atomic case

In the atomic photoelectric effect, an incident photon is completely absorbed by a single bound electron in a particular atomic shell. If that shell's binding energy is EB, the ejected photoelectron carries away the remainder as kinetic energy:

$$T_e = h\nu - E_B$$

Absorption from a given shell is only possible once hν ≥ EB; below that threshold the photon can still ionize a more loosely bound outer shell instead. Because the photoelectron's energy is offset below hν by a fixed binding energy, monoenergetic photons produce a discrete photoelectron spectrum — one line per accessible shell — rather than a continuum.

Once the inner-shell vacancy is created, an outer electron falls in to fill it, releasing the energy difference either as a characteristic X-ray photon or as an Auger electron. The probability of X-ray emission — the fluorescence yield ω — rises steeply with atomic number (see the reference table below).

Z and energy scaling — why high-Z, low-energy absorbers dominate

Away from absorption edges, the atomic photoelectric cross-section is well approximated by a power law in atomic number and photon energy:

$$\tau \;\approx\; k\,\frac{Z^{\,n}}{E_\gamma^{\,m}} \qquad (n \approx 4\text{–}5,\;\; m \approx 3\text{–}3.5)$$

The steep Zn dependence is why high-atomic-number materials — lead, tungsten, barium, iodine — are so effective at photoelectric absorption, and why they are chosen for shielding and as radiographic contrast agents. The steep 1/Em falloff means the effect dominates total attenuation only at comparatively low photon energies — below roughly 100 keV for high-Z absorbers, and considerably lower still for low-Z materials.

Because the K shell holds only two electrons but they are by far the most tightly bound, K-shell photoionization accounts for roughly 80% of the total photoelectric cross-section whenever hν exceeds EK. Crossing each shell's binding energy from below produces a sharp discontinuity — an absorption edge — where the cross-section jumps upward as that shell's electrons suddenly become accessible, before resuming its decline with increasing energy.

Angular distribution of the photoelectron

At low photon energies, the non-relativistic dipole approximation predicts photoelectrons emitted preferentially perpendicular to the incident photon direction:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \propto \sin^2\theta$$

As photon energy — and with it the photoelectron speed β = v/c — increases, relativistic retardation tilts the emission lobe forward, toward the photon's direction of travel. The leading correction to the dipole result is:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \;\propto\; \frac{\sin^2\theta}{(1-\beta\cos\theta)^4}$$

where θ is measured from the photon's propagation direction. This forward-peaking is explored interactively in the Visualizations tab below.

02

Compton Scattering

A photon scatters off a loosely bound (effectively free) electron, transferring part of its energy and changing direction. The dominant interaction for intermediate photon energies in low-to-medium-Z materials.

Compton kinematics — the wavelength (and energy) shift

Compton scattering treats the photon as colliding elastically with a free (or loosely bound, outer-shell) electron. Applying relativistic energy and momentum conservation to the two-body collision gives the wavelength shift:

$$\lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_ec}(1-\cos\theta)$$

where h/mec = 2.426 pm is the Compton wavelength of the electron and θ is the photon's scattering angle. Equivalently, in terms of photon energy:

$$E' = \frac{E}{1+\alpha(1-\cos\theta)}, \qquad \alpha \equiv \frac{E}{m_ec^2}$$

Unlike the photoelectric effect, this is purely kinematic — it requires no atomic binding energy at all, which is why Compton scattering is so well described by treating the target electron as free.

Electron recoil — energy, angle, and the Compton edge

By energy conservation, the recoil electron carries off whatever the photon loses:

$$T_e = E - E' = E\,\frac{\alpha(1-\cos\theta)}{1+\alpha(1-\cos\theta)}$$

Momentum conservation in the transverse direction links the electron's recoil angle φ (measured from the incident photon direction) to the photon's scattering angle θ:

$$\cot\phi = (1+\alpha)\tan\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$$

The recoil energy is maximized for photon backscatter (θ = 180°), where the electron recoils straight forward (φ = 0°):

$$T_{e,\max} = E\,\frac{2\alpha}{1+2\alpha}$$

This maximum is the Compton edge — the sharp cutoff seen in real detector spectra at the top of the Compton continuum (explored in the Visualizations tab below).

The Klein-Nishina differential cross-section

The exact quantum-electrodynamic (lowest-order) cross-section per electron, due to Klein and Nishina (1929):

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{r_e^2}{2}\left(\frac{E'}{E}\right)^2\left(\frac{E}{E'}+\frac{E'}{E}-\sin^2\theta\right)$$

where re = 2.818 fm is the classical electron radius. At low energies (E ≪ mec²), E′→E and this reduces to the classical Thomson result, dσ/dΩ → (re²/2)(1+cos²θ) — symmetric between forward and backward scattering. As energy increases, the distribution becomes progressively forward-peaked (explored interactively below).

Total cross-section and Z-independence

Integrating the Klein-Nishina formula over solid angle gives the total cross-section per electron, σKN(E). A closed form exists:

$$\sigma_{KN} = 2\pi r_e^2\left[\frac{1+\alpha}{\alpha^2}\left(\frac{2(1+\alpha)}{1+2\alpha}-\frac{\ln(1+2\alpha)}{\alpha}\right)+\frac{\ln(1+2\alpha)}{2\alpha}-\frac{1+3\alpha}{(1+2\alpha)^2}\right]$$

As α→0, this correctly recovers the Thomson cross-section σT = (8π/3)re² = 0.6652 barn; at high energy it falls off roughly as 1/E. The simulator below computes σKN both ways — by direct numerical integration of the differential formula above, and from this closed form — as a cross-check.

Because the cross-section is defined per electron, the atomic Compton cross-section simply scales as σatom = Z·σKN — linear in Z, not Z⁴–⁵ like the photoelectric effect. That weaker, energy-independent-of-Z scaling is exactly why Compton scattering dominates total attenuation across a broad intermediate-energy band in low-to-medium-Z materials such as tissue, water, and aluminum.

03

Pair Production

A high-energy photon converts into an electron–positron pair in the field of a nucleus. Requires a photon energy above the 1.022 MeV threshold (twice the electron rest mass) and grows in importance at high energies.

Threshold condition — why a nucleus is required

Pair production converts a photon entirely into a matter–antimatter pair (electron + positron) in the Coulomb field of a nearby nucleus. The photon must carry at least the combined rest-mass energy of both particles:

$$h\nu \;\geq\; 2\,m_e c^2 = 1.022\;\text{MeV}$$

Below this hard threshold the process is kinematically forbidden — regardless of material, intensity, or any other condition. The nucleus acts as a momentum sink: a free photon cannot produce a pair in vacuum because no pair of four-momenta for the products can simultaneously satisfy energy and momentum conservation without a third body. The nuclear recoil absorbs the residual momentum with negligible energy transfer, leaving the nucleus essentially unchanged:

$$\gamma \;+\; {}^A_Z X \;\longrightarrow\; e^- \;+\; e^+ \;+\; {}^A_Z X$$

Kinematics — energy sharing and opening angle

Above threshold, the surplus kinetic energy is shared between the electron and positron. By conservation of energy and momentum:

$$T_{e^-} + T_{e^+} = h\nu - 2\,m_e c^2$$

The distribution of this surplus is broad and roughly symmetric, shaped by the Bethe–Heitler differential cross section (see the Interactive Simulations tab). Because both particles are highly relativistic, their momenta are nearly collinear with the incident photon — the mean emission half-angle for each track scales inversely with energy:

$$\langle\theta_\pm\rangle \;\approx\; \frac{m_e c^2}{h\nu} \;=\; \frac{0.511\;\text{MeV}}{h\nu}$$

At a few MeV this is already less than 10°; at tens of MeV the tracks are nearly parallel to the beam direction. Pair-tracking calorimeters in gamma-ray telescopes exploit this forward collimation to reconstruct the original photon direction.

Bethe–Heitler cross section — Z² scaling and energy dependence

The pair production cross section per atom was derived quantum-electrodynamically by Bethe and Heitler. In the high-energy, complete-screening limit (atomic electrons fully shield the nuclear charge — valid when $h\nu \gg 137\,m_e c^2 / Z^{1/3}$):

$$\sigma_{\text{pair}} \;\approx\; 4\,\alpha\,r_e^2\,Z^2\!\left[\frac{7}{9}\ln\frac{183}{Z^{1/3}} - f(Z) - \frac{1}{54}\right]$$

where $\alpha \approx 1/137$ is the fine-structure constant, $r_e = 2.818\,\text{fm}$ the classical electron radius, and $f(Z)$ a small Coulomb correction (tabulated; of order $(\alpha Z)^2$). In the no-screening, high-energy limit the formula simplifies to:

$$\sigma_{\text{pair}} \;\approx\; 4\,\alpha\,r_e^2\,Z^2\!\left[\frac{7}{9}\ln\!\left(\frac{2h\nu}{m_e c^2}\right) - \frac{109}{42}\right]$$

Two consequences dominate practical shielding and detector design: (1) the Z² scaling — lead (Z=82) has a per-atom pair-production cross section roughly $(82/6)^2 \approx 187\times$ larger than carbon, dwarfing the linear-Z Compton term; (2) logarithmic growth with energy above threshold — unlike the photoelectric cross section which falls steeply as $1/E^3$, pair production keeps growing, so it becomes the dominant attenuation mechanism above a few MeV in high-Z materials and above tens of MeV even in tissue.

Triplet production and the positron's fate

When the Coulomb field belongs to an atomic electron rather than a nucleus, the process is called triplet production: the result is three energetic charged particles (e⁻, e⁺, plus the struck atomic electron). The threshold doubles to $4\,m_e c^2 = 2.044\,\text{MeV}$, and the cross section scales as Z rather than Z², so it contributes at most a few percent except in very low-Z materials at high energies.

The positron's fate is universal: once it slows to near rest through ionization losses it annihilates with a surrounding electron, converting the two rest masses into exactly two 511 keV photons emitted back-to-back (to conserve the near-zero momentum of the pair at rest). This annihilation line at 511 keV is the diagnostic signature used in PET imaging:

$$e^+ + e^- \;\longrightarrow\; 2\gamma, \quad E_\gamma = m_e c^2 = 511\;\text{keV each}$$

In a radiation detector, pair production followed by positron annihilation produces two characteristic escape peaks in the measured spectrum: a single-escape peak at $h\nu - 511\,\text{keV}$ (one 511 keV photon escapes the detector) and a double-escape peak at $h\nu - 1022\,\text{keV}$ (both escape), alongside the full-energy photopeak where both annihilation photons are reabsorbed.

Coherent (Rayleigh) Scattering

A photon scatters elastically off an atom as a whole, with no energy loss and only a small change in direction. Most significant at low photon energies and high atomic number.

Thomson scattering and the origin of Z² coherence

Coherent (Rayleigh) scattering is an elastic interaction: a photon scatters off an entire atom and emerges with its frequency unchanged. No energy is deposited in the medium, and the atom returns to its ground state undisturbed. The classical foundation is Thomson scattering off a single free electron, for which the differential cross-section is:

$$\frac{d\sigma_T}{d\Omega} = \frac{r_e^2}{2}(1+\cos^2\theta)$$

where $r_e = 2.818\,\text{fm}$ is the classical electron radius and $\theta$ is the photon's scattering angle measured from its incident direction. Integrating over all solid angles gives the total Thomson cross-section: $\sigma_T = (8\pi/3)\,r_e^2 = 0.6652$ barn per electron. For Z electrons scattering independently the expectation would be $Z\sigma_T$. What coherence produces instead is a factor of $Z^2$ in the forward limit — the electrons' scattered wavelets add in phase, not amplitude-by-amplitude.

The atomic form factor F(q, Z) — encoding the charge distribution

Whether the Z electrons scatter in phase (coherently) or out of phase (incoherently) depends on the momentum transfer $q = |\boldsymbol{k}_f - \boldsymbol{k}_i|$ relative to the reciprocal of the atomic radius. For a photon of energy $E$ scattered through angle $\theta$:

$$q = \frac{2E}{\hbar c}\sin\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$$

The atomic form factor $F(q,Z)$ is the Fourier transform of the ground-state electron density $\rho(\boldsymbol{r})$:

$$F(q,Z) = \int \rho(\boldsymbol{r})\,e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}\,d^3r \qquad \Longrightarrow \quad F(0,Z)=Z,\quad F(\infty,Z)=0$$

When $q \to 0$ (small angle or low energy), the phase factor $e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}\approx 1$ across the entire atom, all electrons scatter in phase, and $F\to Z$: full coherence. When $q$ is large (large angle or high energy), the exponential oscillates rapidly across the charge cloud and the integral vanishes: the electrons scatter incoherently. The Rayleigh differential cross-section is therefore the Thomson result weighted by the squared form factor:

$$\frac{d\sigma_R}{d\Omega} = \frac{r_e^2}{2}(1+\cos^2\theta)\,|F(q,Z)|^2$$

Integrating over angles gives $\sigma_R(E,Z)$. A useful total cross-section approximation, using the Thomas–Fermi atomic radius $a \approx a_0 Z^{-1/3}$ and recovering the well-established high-energy $Z^{4/3}/E^2$ scaling, is:

$$\sigma_R(E,Z) \;\approx\; Z^2\,\sigma_T\,\left(\frac{E_c}{E_c + E\,Z^{1/3}}\right)^{\!2} \qquad E_c \approx 14\ \text{keV}$$

At $E \ll E_c/Z^{1/3}$: $\sigma_R \to Z^2\sigma_T$ (full coherence). At $E \gg E_c/Z^{1/3}$: $\sigma_R \approx Z^{4/3}\sigma_T E_c^2/E^2$ — the $Z^{4/3}/E^2$ form reported in NIST XCOM tables for the coherent contribution.

Practical significance — when does Rayleigh scattering matter?

Coherent scattering contributes to the total linear attenuation coefficient $\mu/\rho$ alongside photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering, and pair production. It is significant when:

  • Photon energy is below ~100 keV — before Compton dominates for most elements
  • The absorber has high atomic number — the $Z^{4/3}$ factor boosts $\sigma_R$ relative to the linear-$Z$ Compton term
  • Angular precision matters — Rayleigh deflects photons out of narrow beams without producing secondary electrons or depositing dose

Unlike Compton scattering, coherent scattering produces no energy transfer to the medium. A 100 keV photon remains at exactly 100 keV after Rayleigh scattering, so it contributes no Compton continuum to detector spectra. However, it does redirect photons, broadening beams and introducing a small correction in dose calculations and CT reconstruction algorithms.

Energy Loss

Charged particles lose energy continuously through many small collisions with atomic electrons, described by the Bethe-Bloch formula, plus radiative losses (bremsstrahlung) at high energies.

Bethe–Bloch formula — collisional stopping power for heavy charged particles

A charged particle traversing matter loses energy continuously through many small Coulomb collisions with atomic electrons. The mean rate of energy loss per unit areal density — the mass stopping power — is described by the Bethe–Bloch formula:

$$-\!\left\langle\frac{dE}{dx}\right\rangle_{\!\text{col}} = K\,z^2\,\frac{Z}{A}\,\frac{1}{\beta^2} \left[\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2T_{\max}}{I^2} - \beta^2 - \frac{\delta(\beta\gamma)}{2}\right]$$

where $K = 4\pi N_A r_e^2 m_e c^2 = 0.307\;\text{MeV cm}^2\text{/g}$, $z$ is the projectile charge number, $Z/A$ is the absorber's atomic-to-mass-number ratio, $I$ is its mean excitation energy, and the density effect correction $\delta$ accounts for polarisation screening of distant collisions. The maximum kinetic energy transferable to a single electron in one collision is:

$$T_{\max} = \frac{2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2}{1 + 2\gamma m_e/M + (m_e/M)^2}$$

Three distinct velocity regions follow directly from the formula. At low $\beta$ the $1/\beta^2$ prefactor dominates and the stopping power rises steeply as the particle slows. A broad minimum — the minimum ionising point — occurs near $\beta\gamma\approx3\text{–}4$ ($\approx 3.5\,M$ in kinetic energy for any particle of mass $M$). Above the minimum, the logarithmic Bethe term produces a gentle relativistic rise that is eventually clamped by the density effect to the Fermi plateau. Minimum-ionising particles (MIPs) are the fundamental calibration signal in almost every tracking detector.

Modified Bethe formula for electrons and positrons

For electrons, the projectile is identical to the target electron, so exchange effects halve the maximum energy transfer ($T_\max = T/2$). The Möller formula gives:

$$-\!\left\langle\frac{dE}{dx}\right\rangle_{\!\text{col}} = K\,\frac{Z}{A}\,\frac{1}{\beta^2} \left[\ln\frac{\tau^2(\tau+2)}{2(I/m_ec^2)^2} + F^-(\tau) - \delta\right]$$

where $\tau = T/m_ec^2$ and the exchange correction is:

$$F^-(\tau) = 1-\beta^2 + \frac{\tau^2/8 - (2\tau+1)\ln 2}{(\tau+1)^2}$$

For positrons a slightly different correction $F^+(\tau)$ applies (Bhabha scattering), giving a marginally higher stopping power than for electrons at the same energy; the two curves converge above a few MeV. Because the electron mass is 1836 times smaller than the proton mass, electrons reach the MIP region at kinetic energies of only a few MeV rather than a few GeV.

Radiative losses, radiation length, and critical energy

In the nuclear Coulomb field, charged particles also lose energy by bremsstrahlung — electromagnetic radiation emitted during the deceleration. The radiative mass stopping power grows linearly with total energy:

$$-\!\left\langle\frac{dE}{dx}\right\rangle_{\!\text{rad}} \approx \frac{E}{X_0}$$

where $X_0$ is the radiation length — the mean distance over which a high-energy electron loses all but $1/e$ of its energy by radiation. Radiative loss scales as $1/M^2$ relative to collisional loss, making it entirely negligible for protons and heavier particles below the TeV range, but dominant for electrons above a few tens of MeV. The critical energy $E_c$ is where the two contributions are equal:

$$E_c \approx \frac{800\;\text{MeV}}{Z + 1.2} \quad \text{(solids and liquids)}$$

Above $E_c$, bremsstrahlung photons immediately produce pairs, which in turn radiate more photons — the alternating steps of an electromagnetic shower — and the transverse shower profile scales with $X_0$. This is the physics of calorimeter design in high-energy particle physics.

CSDA range, straggling, and the Bragg peak

Integrating the reciprocal stopping power over all kinetic energies down to rest gives the continuous-slowing-down approximation (CSDA) range:

$$R_{\text{CSDA}}(T_0) = \int_0^{T_0}\!\left(-\frac{dE}{dx}\right)^{-1}\!dE$$

For heavy charged particles (proton, alpha, ...) the CSDA range accurately predicts the actual range because large-angle scattering is rare and paths are nearly straight. The $1/\beta^2$ rise in stopping power as the particle slows produces the Bragg peak — a sharp dose spike just before the particle stops — with essentially zero dose beyond. Range straggling (particle-to-particle fluctuations) is small: typically 1–2% of the mean range. For electrons, 7000× lighter than the proton, large-angle Möller and Mott scattering is frequent; paths branch and are tortuous. There is no Bragg peak — depth-dose falls off roughly monotonically — and straggling is much larger. The practical (extrapolated) range is considerably shorter than $R_\text{CSDA}$.

Ionization Chamber

A gas-filled detector that collects the charge produced when incoming radiation ionizes the fill gas, operated in the saturation region for stable, proportional current measurement.

Basic operating principle — ion pairs, charge, and the W-value

An ionization chamber is simply a gas-filled volume between two electrodes held at a potential difference V. As ionizing radiation passes through the gas, it transfers energy to gas atoms via Coulomb collisions, creating electron–ion pairs along its track. The applied field sweeps electrons (or negative ions, in electronegative gases) to the anode and positive ions to the cathode before they can recombine, producing a measurable charge or current at the electrodes.

The mean number of ion pairs produced by an absorbed energy E is fixed by the gas's W-value — the average energy expended per ion pair formed. W is somewhat larger than the gas's first ionization potential because some absorbed energy excites atoms without ionizing them, and is nearly independent of the type and energy of the incident radiation over a wide range:

$$n = \frac{E}{W}, \qquad Q = n\,e = \frac{E}{W}\,e$$

Saturation and recombination — why operating voltage matters

Region II is itself a plateau bounded by an onset voltage below which recombination losses are non-negligible. Two recombination mechanisms compete with charge collection: initial (columnar) recombination, between an electron and the positive ion left behind by the very same track, and general (volume) recombination, between charges from different tracks that build up in the gas at high dose rates. Boag's theory of continuous irradiation gives the fraction of charge actually collected — the collection efficiency f — as a function of applied voltage, gap, and ionization rate:

$$f(V) \;\approx\; \frac{1}{1 + k\,d^{4}\dot{Q}\,/\,V^{2}}$$

where d is the electrode spacing, Q̇ is the rate of ion-pair production per unit volume (proportional to dose rate), and k is a constant set by the gas's ion mobility and recombination coefficient. The strong d⁴/V² dependence has two practical consequences: doubling the electrode gap requires four times the voltage to maintain the same collection efficiency, and a chamber that saturates comfortably at low dose rates may show significant recombination loss at high dose rates — exactly the regime explored in the simulator below.

Exposure, dose, and the Bragg–Gray cavity

Historically, the ionization chamber's signature application is the definition of exposure X — the charge of one sign produced per unit mass of air:

$$X = \frac{dQ}{dm}\ \left[\text{C/kg}\right], \qquad 1\ \text{R} = 2.58\times10^{-4}\ \text{C/kg}$$

The absorbed dose to the air itself follows directly from the W-value: Dair = X·(W/e)air = X × 33.97 J/C. To get the dose in some other medium — tissue, water, a chamber's wall material — Bragg–Gray cavity theory treats the gas as a small, non-perturbing "sample" of the charged-particle field that would exist in the surrounding medium, related by the ratio of mass collision stopping powers:

$$D_{\text{medium}} = D_{\text{gas}}\,\frac{(\bar S/\rho)_{\text{medium}}}{(\bar S/\rho)_{\text{gas}}}$$

This is the working principle behind clinical reference dosimeters such as the Farmer chamber: a small gas-filled cavity surrounded by a wall of known material measures Dgas directly through collected charge, and a tabulated stopping-power ratio converts that reading into the dose delivered to water or tissue.

Geiger Counter

A gas detector operated in the Geiger-Müller region, where a single ionizing event triggers a full avalanche discharge — giving a large, easily counted pulse but no energy information.

Townsend ionization and gas multiplication

Primary radiation creates n₀ electron–ion pairs along its track. Electrons drift toward the central anode wire, gaining kinetic energy in the applied electric field. Once their energy exceeds the first ionization potential of the fill gas, each electron triggers further ionizations — an exponentially growing Townsend avalanche. The gas multiplication factor M is the ratio of collected charge to primary ionization:

$$M = \exp\!\left(\int_{r_a}^{r_c} \alpha(r)\,dr\right)$$

where α(r) is the first Townsend ionization coefficient and the integral runs from the anode wire radius ra to the cathode radius rc. In the cylindrical geometry of a GM tube the electric field at radius r is:

$$E(r) = \frac{V}{r\,\ln(r_c/r_a)}$$

The field grows steeply as r → ra, so avalanche multiplication is confined to a thin cylindrical shell surrounding the wire, while ions produced at larger radii simply drift without multiplying. In the proportional region M ≈ 10³–10⁴; in the Geiger–Müller region M ≈ 10⁷–10⁹.

The self-propagating Geiger–Müller discharge

In the proportional region each avalanche is spatially confined. As voltage increases further, the avalanche becomes so intense that excited gas atoms emit UV photons energetic enough to eject photoelectrons from the cathode or ionize gas at remote locations — spreading new avalanches along the entire anode wire within microseconds. The discharge engulfs the whole tube regardless of where the original ionization occurred.

Consequently, every detected event produces an identical, large output pulse. The total charge released per discharge is approximately:

$$Q \approx C\,\Delta V \;\approx\; C\,\frac{q\,n_0\,M}{C} \qquad \Rightarrow \quad \Delta V \approx \frac{q\,n_0\,M}{C}$$

where C is the tube capacitance (~10 pF) and ΔV is the voltage drop across C during the discharge. Because M is the same for every event, all pulses are the same height — the GM tube carries no energy information. This is its key limitation compared with proportional or scintillation detectors, but the enormous gain means even a single ion pair produces a pulse easily detected by simple electronics.

The discharge is terminated when the growing sheath of slow-moving positive ions near the anode wire reduces the local field below the threshold for further avalanche. The pulse then decays with a characteristic time constant set by the RC product of the tube circuit.

Quenching — controlling the discharge

Without quenching, positive ions drifting to the cathode would release secondary electrons by ion bombardment or photoemission, restarting the discharge indefinitely. A quench gas suppresses this by preferentially accepting the charge transfer from the fill gas ions during their drift, then dissipating the energy harmlessly:

  • Organic quenching: polyatomic vapours (ethanol, isobutane, ethyl formate) added at 5–10%. Ions transfer charge to the organic molecules, which then dissociate rather than emit a photon. Drawback: the quench gas is consumed irreversibly, giving tube lifetimes of ~10⁸–10⁹ counts.
  • Halogen quenching: Cl₂ or Br₂ at 0.1–1%. After dissociation, halogen atoms recombine thermally — the quench gas is continuously regenerated and tube lifetime is essentially unlimited. Plateau slopes are slightly steeper and operating voltages higher than organic tubes.
  • External quenching: an electronic circuit rapidly reduces the anode voltage below the threshold after each pulse. Used in some early tubes; now largely superseded by self-quenching fill gases.

The quenching process sets the detector's dead time τ — the period after a detected pulse during which no new event can produce a fresh discharge. For typical GM tubes τ ≈ 50–300 µs. A secondary, longer recovery time (~100–500 µs) follows, during which pulses are detectable but reduced in height.

Dead time, count-rate correction, and the plateau

At high count rates, uncorrected readings underestimate the true flux. Two idealised dead-time models bracket real detector behaviour:

$$\textbf{Non-paralyzable:}\quad m = \frac{n}{1+n\tau} \;\Longrightarrow\; n = \frac{m}{1-m\tau}$$
$$\textbf{Paralyzable:}\quad m = n\,e^{-n\tau}$$

In the non-paralyzable (extending) model — which GM tubes most closely follow — the observed rate m saturates at 1/τ regardless of how high n climbs. In the paralyzable (non-extending) model, very high true rates can actually decrease the observed rate as events pile up into the dead window — the detector can be "blinded" by an intense source. GM tubes avoid true paralysis because the discharge itself terminates the dead period, but they do approach the 1/τ ceiling at high rates.

The Geiger plateau is the range of operating voltage over which the count rate from a fixed source is nearly constant. A low slope (≪ 3%/100 V) indicates a healthy tube; it arises because the threshold ionisation for the discharge stays well below the primary ionisation over the plateau range. The recommended operating point is the centre of the plateau, typically 50–100 V above the threshold (starting voltage):

Scintillation Detector

Uses a scintillator material that emits light when radiation deposits energy in it, with that light converted to an electrical pulse by a photomultiplier tube or photodiode.

Scintillation mechanism — converting deposited energy to light

Ionizing radiation deposits energy in the scintillator, exciting and ionizing the host medium. In inorganic crystals this energy migrates to activator centers (e.g. Tl⁺ in NaI(Tl)) which relax radiatively; in organic scintillators it populates molecular excited states that fluoresce. The number of optical photons produced is set by the material's light yield Y (photons per unit energy):

$$N_{ph} = Y \cdot E_{dep}$$

Only a fraction of this light reaches the photodetector — set by the light-collection efficiency εcoll (geometry, reflective wrapping, optical coupling) — and only a fraction of those photons liberate a photoelectron, set by the photocathode's quantum efficiency QE. The number of photoelectrons that ultimately carries the signal is:

$$N_{pe} = Y \cdot E_{dep} \cdot \varepsilon_{coll} \cdot \mathrm{QE}$$

High-Z inorganic crystals (NaI(Tl), CsI(Tl), LaBr₃(Ce), BGO) favour photoelectric absorption and high light yield — ideal for gamma-ray spectroscopy. Low-Z organic scintillators (plastic, liquid, stilbene) are fast and cheap, favoured for timing, charged-particle counting, and fast-neutron detection.

Statistical energy resolution — the photoelectron-counting limit

Because Npe is a finite, randomly sampled number, the pulse height fluctuates event-to-event even for a fixed deposited energy. Counting (Poisson) statistics, broadened further by the multiplicative variance of the PMT's dynode gain (captured by an excess noise factor ε), set a fundamental floor on the fractional FWHM resolution:

$$R \equiv \frac{\mathrm{FWHM}}{E} \;\approx\; 2.35\sqrt{\frac{1+\varepsilon}{N_{pe}}}$$

Since Npe ∝ E, this statistical term falls as 1/√E — the familiar improvement in resolution at higher energy seen in every scintillator spectrum. Two further contributions are added in quadrature: non-proportionality (the light yield itself varies slightly with deposited energy density) and transfer/collection non-uniformity (position-dependent light collection across the crystal):

$$R_{tot}^2 = R_{stat}^2 + R_{nonprop}^2 + R_{transfer}^2$$

Photomultiplier gain and the dynode chain

A photoelectron ejected from the photocathode is accelerated onto the first of a chain of n dynodes, each releasing δ secondary electrons per incident electron (secondary-emission gain). The cascade multiplies the original single electron into a measurable charge pulse at the anode:

$$M = \delta^{\,n}$$

Typical PMTs use δ ≈ 3–6 across n ≈ 8–12 dynode stages, giving total gain M ≈ 10⁶–10⁷ from only ~1000–2000 V distributed across the chain. Silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) — dense arrays of Geiger-mode avalanche photodiode microcells — offer a compact, magnetic-field-immune alternative with gain ≈ 10⁵–10⁶, at the cost of dark counts and microcell saturation at high photon flux.

Decay time and pulse shape discrimination (PSD)

Scintillation light output is rarely a single exponential — most materials show a fast and a slow component:

$$L(t) = N_0\left[\frac{f_{fast}}{\tau_{fast}}\,e^{-t/\tau_{fast}} + \frac{1-f_{fast}}{\tau_{slow}}\,e^{-t/\tau_{slow}}\right], \quad t \ge 0$$

In certain organic scintillators, the relative weight of the slow (triplet-mediated) component depends on how densely the exciting particle ionizes. Recoil protons from fast-neutron elastic scattering ionize far more densely than the Compton electrons produced by gamma rays, populating more long-lived triplet states and producing a longer tail. This shape difference is the basis of pulse shape discrimination, typically quantified by a tail-to-total charge ratio measured against a gate time tgate:

$$\text{PSD ratio} = \frac{\displaystyle\int_{t_{gate}}^{\infty} L(t)\,dt}{\displaystyle\int_0^{\infty} L(t)\,dt}$$

Semiconductor Detector

A solid-state device (e.g. silicon or germanium) where radiation creates electron-hole pairs in a depletion region, giving excellent energy resolution for spectroscopy.

Carrier generation — the W-value advantage

Ionizing radiation deposits energy in the crystal lattice, promoting electrons across the band gap and leaving behind holes. The mean energy needed to create one electron–hole pair is the W-value, substantially larger than the band gap Eg because some energy dissipates as phonons:

$$N_{eh} = \frac{E_{dep}}{W}, \qquad W \approx 2.67\,E_g + 0.87\;\text{eV}\quad\text{(Fahrenbruch–Bube approximation)}$$

The resulting signal charge is Q = Neh·e. The key spectroscopic advantage is that W is 5–10 eV — roughly five to ten times smaller than the W-value of gas detectors (~26–34 eV per ion pair) — so an equivalent deposited energy produces 5–10× more charge carriers, dramatically suppressing Poisson statistical noise and delivering superior energy resolution.

The depletion region — where detection happens

A semiconductor detector is a reverse-biased p–n junction. The applied voltage sweeps away mobile carriers from the junction, creating a depletion region of width Wd that acts as a sensitive volume. Radiation-generated electron–hole pairs within this region are separated by the built-in electric field before they can recombine:

$$W_d = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0(V_{\text{bias}}+V_{bi})}{eN_D}}, \qquad V_{fd} = \frac{eN_D\,d^2}{2\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0}$$

where εr is the relative permittivity, Vbi the built-in potential (~0.5–0.8 V), ND the net dopant concentration, d the detector thickness, and Vfd the full-depletion voltage — the bias at which Wd equals the physical thickness d. Operating at or above Vfd maximises the active volume and thus the efficiency.

Carrier transport — drift velocity and the Hecht equation

Electrons and holes drift under the applied field E with mobilities µe and µh respectively, and simultaneously recombine with trapping centres characterised by carrier lifetimes τe and τh. The fraction of generated charge actually collected — the charge collection efficiency (CCE) — is given by the Hecht equation:

$$\text{CCE} = \frac{\mu_e\tau_e}{d^2/E}\!\left(1-e^{-d/\mu_e\tau_e E}\right) + \frac{\mu_h\tau_h}{d^2/E}\!\left(1-e^{-d/\mu_h\tau_h E}\right)$$

The product μτ (mu-tau) is the mobility–lifetime product — the fundamental material quality figure. High-purity germanium (HPGe) achieves μτ values of 10⁻² cm²/V or better; compound semiconductors like CdTe or CdZnTe (CZT) are 10–100× lower, making charge trapping a significant limitation in room-temperature detectors.

$$v_{\text{drift}} = \mu\,E\,\left(1 + \frac{E}{E_{\text{sat}}}\right)^{-1}\qquad\text{(saturation approximation)}$$

At fields typically used in HPGe detectors (∼100 V/cm) both carriers travel well below the saturation velocity (~10⁷ cm/s); in CZT, where electric fields exceed 1 kV/cm, saturation effects become noticeable for holes.

Energy resolution — Fano factor and noise

Because the Neh electron–hole pairs created per event are not independent Poisson samples — they are constrained by energy conservation — the actual variance falls below the Poisson expectation by the Fano factor F < 1:

$$\sigma_E^2 = F\,W\,E_{dep} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \text{FWHM}_{\text{stat}} = 2.355\sqrt{F\,W\,E_{dep}}$$

For HPGe, F ≈ 0.13 and W = 2.96 eV, giving a statistical limit of ~0.5 keV FWHM at 1 MeV — roughly 0.05%. Electronic noise (equivalent noise charge ENC, in electrons r.m.s.) adds in quadrature:

$$\text{FWHM}_{\text{total}}^2 = \text{FWHM}_{\text{stat}}^2 + (2.355\,W\,\text{ENC})^2$$

Cooling to 77 K (liquid nitrogen) in HPGe suppresses both thermal carrier generation (leakage current) and phonon scattering of carriers (improving μτ), making cryogenic operation necessary.

Dosimeters

Devices that record cumulative radiation dose over time for personnel or environmental monitoring, including film badges, TLDs, and electronic personal dosimeters.

Radiation protection quantities — absorbed dose, equivalent dose, effective dose

Three hierarchical quantities underpin all dosimetry. Absorbed dose D is the energy deposited per unit mass of any material — the most fundamental and measurable quantity:

$$D = \frac{dE}{dm} \quad \left[\text{Gy} = \frac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}\right] \qquad \text{(old unit: rad; 1 Gy = 100 rad)}$$

Because equal absorbed doses from different radiation types produce different biological effects, absorbed dose is multiplied by the dimensionless radiation weighting factor wR to give equivalent dose HT in a tissue T:

$$H_T = \sum_R w_R \cdot D_{T,R} \quad [\text{Sv}] \qquad \text{(old unit: rem; 1 Sv = 100 rem)}$$

Since different organs and tissues show different radiosensitivity, equivalent doses are further weighted by tissue weighting factors wT and summed to give the effective dose E — the central quantity in radiation protection:

$$E = \sum_T w_T \cdot H_T \quad [\text{Sv}]$$

Effective dose enables comparison of radiation risks from partial-body exposures of very different types. Annual natural background is ≈ 2–3 mSv; the occupational limit (ICRP) is 20 mSv/year averaged over five years.

Thermoluminescent and optically stimulated luminescence — the band model

TLDs and OSL dosimeters exploit defect states — traps — within the forbidden band gap of an insulating crystal. Ionizing radiation promotes valence-band electrons into the conduction band; most recombine promptly, but a small fraction fall into traps and remain there at room temperature, encoding the radiation dose in the form of a frozen population of trapped charge.

In a TLD reader the crystal is heated at a controlled rate β (°C/s). When the temperature supplies enough thermal energy to lift trapped electrons back to the conduction band, they migrate to luminescence centres (activator ions such as Mg²⁺, Mn²⁺, Ce³⁺) and recombine with hole centres, emitting a visible photon. The resulting glow curve I(T) follows first-order Randall–Wilkins kinetics:

$$\frac{dI}{dT} = \frac{n\,s}{\beta}\exp\!\left(-\frac{E}{kT}\right), \qquad \frac{dn}{dT} = -\frac{n\,s}{\beta}\exp\!\left(-\frac{E}{kT}\right)$$

where n is the trapped-electron concentration, s the attempt-to-escape frequency factor (s⁻¹), E the trap depth (activation energy, eV), and k Boltzmann's constant. The peak temperature Tm satisfies:

$$\frac{s}{\beta}\exp\!\left(-\frac{E}{kT_m}\right) = \frac{E}{kT_m^2}$$

Deeper traps (larger E) need higher temperatures to release. Since the total light output ∫I(T) dT = n₀ ∝ dose, integrating the glow curve gives a linear measure of absorbed dose. In OSL dosimeters the same trapped electrons are released by an optical beam (green or IR laser) rather than heat; the stimulated luminescence is then detected in the blue/UV band by a PMT separated from the stimulation wavelength by an optical filter.
