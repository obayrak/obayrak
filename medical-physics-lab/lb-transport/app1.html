Step-by-Step Derivation
Step 1 — Particle Balance on a Phase-Space Volume ▶

Consider a small 6-D phase-space element d³r·dΩ·dE centred at (r,Ω,E,t). The rate of change of particles in this element equals the sum of all gains minus all losses:

d/dt [n dV] = Gains − Losses

where n(r,Ω,E,t) = Ψ/v is the angular particle density (particles per unit volume of phase space).

Step 2 — Streaming Term ∇·(ΩΨ) ▶

Particles streaming with direction Ω and speed v carry a net outward current through the surface of dV. Applying the divergence theorem to the angular current J=ΩΨ:

Streaming loss = Ω·∇Ψ · dV dΩ dE

This term is purely geometric — no interactions, just free flight along straight lines.

Step 3 — Collision (Loss) Term Σ_t Ψ ▶

A particle collides at rate v·Σ_t per unit time. Every collision removes the particle from state (r,Ω,E):

Collision loss = v Σ_t(r,E) Ψ(r,Ω,E,t)

Σ_t = Σ_a + Σ_s includes all absorption and scattering reactions.

Step 4 — In-Scatter Source ∫∫Σ_s Ψ dΩ'dE' ▶

Particles colliding elsewhere at (Ω',E') are re-born at (Ω,E) according to the differential scattering kernel:

Q_scat = ∫₄π ∫₀^∞ Σ_s(r, E'→E, Ω'·Ω) Ψ(r,Ω',E') dE' dΩ'

For elastic scattering in a heavy medium the energy transfer per collision is small; for hydrogen it can be up to 100%.

Step 5 — Fission Source χ(E)·νΣ_f·φ / 4π ▶

Fission neutrons are born isotropically (1/4π factor) with energy distribution χ(E):

Q_fiss = χ(E)/4π · ∫₀^∞ ν Σ_f(r,E') φ(r,E') dE'

Delayed neutrons require separate precursor groups; the prompt fraction is ~99.35% of ν.

Step 6 — Assemble the Full BTE ▶

Combining all terms and dividing by v gives the integro-differential Boltzmann equation in its standard form:

1/v · ∂Ψ/∂t + Ω·∇Ψ + Σ_t Ψ = ∫∫Σ_s Ψ' dΩ'dE' + χ/(4π)·∫νΣ_f φ dE' + S

In steady state, ∂Ψ/∂t = 0, simplifying the left side to streaming + collisions.

Historical Background

Ludwig Boltzmann (1844–1906) formulated the kinetic transport equation in 1872 for ideal gases. The linear form — where the transported particles do not interact with each other — was adapted for neutron transport in the 1930s by Eugene Wigner, Enrico Fermi, and colleagues at the Manhattan Project.

The equation underpins every modern reactor physics code: MCNP, Serpent, OpenMC (Monte Carlo), CASMO, HELIOS (deterministic SN/MOC), and PARTISN.

Why "Linear"? The equation is linear in the angular flux Ψ because neutron–neutron collisions are negligible (neutron density ≪ atom density). This linearity enables powerful mathematical tools: superposition, adjoint methods, and Green's functions.
Physical Interpretation

The BTE is a particle continuity equation in 7-dimensional phase space (x,y,z,Ω,E,t). It states:

  1. Rate of change of Ψ in a phase-space element
  2. = Net streaming in
  3. + Scattering into (Ω, E) from all other (Ω', E')
  4. + Fission source at energy E
  5. + External/volumetric source S
  6. − Removal by all collisions (absorption + out-scatter)
Key Assumptions
  • ✓ Particles travel in straight lines between collisions
  • ✓ Particle–particle interactions negligible
  • ✓ Scattering is instantaneous
  • ✓ Medium properties (cross sections) are deterministic
  • ✗ Does NOT include quantum effects or relativistic corrections
  • ✗ Does NOT resolve individual nuclei positions (continuum)
Dimensional Analysis

Every term must have units of n·cm⁻³·s⁻¹·sr⁻¹·MeV⁻¹ (source density in phase space):

[1/v · ∂Ψ/∂t] = (s/cm)·(n·cm⁻²·s⁻¹·sr⁻¹·MeV⁻¹)/s = n·cm⁻³·sr⁻¹·MeV⁻¹·s⁻¹ ✓
[Ω·∇Ψ] = (1)·(cm⁻¹)·(n·cm⁻²·s⁻¹·sr⁻¹·MeV⁻¹) = n·cm⁻³·s⁻¹·sr⁻¹·MeV⁻¹ ✓

Process Descriptions
🔵 Streaming▶

Between collisions, particles travel in straight lines at constant speed v in direction Ω. The streaming term Ω·∇Ψ represents this free flight — it has no material dependence and is exact in vacuum.

🟡 Elastic Scattering▶

The neutron interacts with a nucleus and rebounds with a new direction Ω and energy E. Total kinetic energy is conserved. For light nuclei (H, D) large energy loss per collision is possible; for heavy nuclei (U, Pu) the energy loss is small.

E' = E · (A²+2Aμ+1)/(A+1)²

where A is mass number and μ is the cosine of the CM scattering angle.

🟠 Inelastic Scattering▶

The nucleus is left in an excited state after the collision. The neutron loses more energy than in elastic scattering. Important for fast reactor spectrum shaping in materials like iron and nickel.

🔴 Absorption▶

The neutron is absorbed by the nucleus, which may then undergo radiative capture (n,γ), (n,p), (n,α), or fission. The particle is removed from the transport problem.

🟣 Fission▶

A neutron is absorbed by a heavy nucleus (²³⁵U, ²³⁹Pu) causing it to split into two fission fragments plus ν=2–3 prompt neutrons at energies described by the Watt fission spectrum:

χ(E) = 0.4527 e^(-E/0.965) sinh(√(2.29E))
🟢 External Source▶

A neutron or photon source independent of the flux — e.g., a californium (²⁵²Cf) spontaneous fission source, a (α,n) source, or an accelerator-based spallation source. Appears on the right-hand side of the BTE.

📐 Angular Redistribution▶

After scattering, the new direction Ω is drawn from the differential scattering cross section Σ_s(E'→E, Ω'·Ω). For isotropic scattering in the CM frame, the lab-frame distribution is forward-peaked, especially for light nuclei.

Angular Flux vs Scalar Flux
φ(r,E,t) = ∫4π Ψ(r,Ω,E,t) dΩ

The scalar flux φ is the speed-weighted particle density integrated over all directions — the quantity most directly related to reaction rates:

Rx(r,E,t) = Σx(r,E) · φ(r,E,t)

The angular flux Ψ carries directional information needed for accurate streaming, anisotropic scattering, and boundary conditions.

Boundary Condition Type
Vacuum boundary: No particles enter from outside — Ψ(r_s, Ω, E) = 0 for Ω·n̂ < 0. All particles reaching the boundary escape. Used for the outer surface of a shielding calculation or an isolated reactor.
Mathematical Forms
Vacuum BC▶
Ψ(r_s, Ω, E, t) = 0 for all Ω·n̂ < 0

No incoming particles at the surface. The inward hemisphere of directions has zero flux. Physical: particles leave and never return.

Reflective / Mirror BC▶
Ψ(r_s, Ω, E, t) = Ψ(r_s, Ω_r, E, t)

where Ω_r = Ω − 2(Ω·n̂)n̂ is the mirror-reflected direction. Used to exploit symmetry — half or quarter-core calculations reduce to full core solutions.

Periodic BC▶
Ψ(r_L, Ω, E, t) = Ψ(r_R, Ω, E, t)

Particles leaving one face reappear at the opposite face with identical angle and energy. Used for infinite lattice calculations and Fourier analysis of reactor cores.

Albedo (Partial Return) BC▶
Ψ_in(r_s, Ω, E) = α · Ψ_out(r_s, Ω_r, E)

A fraction α (0≤α≤1) of the outgoing flux is returned. α=0 is vacuum; α=1 is reflective. Useful for approximating a thick reflector without computing its interior.

Incoming Flux BC▶
Ψ(r_s, Ω, E) = f(Ω, E) for Ω·n̂ < 0

A prescribed flux enters the domain — e.g., a collimated beam Ψ = I₀·δ(Ω−Ω₀)·δ(E−E₀). Used in shielding calculations and detector response.

Operator Notation

The BTE can be written compactly using the transport operator L and scattering operator S:

LΨ = SΨ + F·φ + Q

where:

L = v⁻¹∂/∂t + Ω·∇ + Σ_t (loss operator)
S = ∫∫ Σ_s(·) dΩ'dE' (scattering operator)
F = χ/(4π)·∫νΣ_f(·) dE' (fission operator)
All operators are linear — the fundamental property enabling superposition, adjoint methods, and perturbation theory.
Eigenvalue (k-effective) Problem
LΨ − SΨ = (1/k)·F·φ

In a critical reactor, the multiplication factor k_eff is the eigenvalue such that the steady-state equation is satisfied. Physical interpretation:

  • k < 1 → subcritical (absorption dominates)
  • k = 1 → critical (self-sustaining chain reaction)
  • k > 1 → supercritical (growing reaction)

Adjoint Transport Equation

The adjoint (importance) function Ψ⁺ satisfies:

L⁺Ψ⁺ = S⁺Ψ⁺ + Q⁺

Ψ⁺(r,Ω,E) represents the importance of a particle at (r,Ω,E) to a detector response R:

R = ∫ Q Ψ⁺ d³r dΩ dE = ∫ Ψ Q⁺ d³r dΩ dE
Applications: Perturbation theory, variance reduction in Monte Carlo (importance sampling, weight windows), sensitivity analysis, shutdown dose calculations.
Diffusion Approximation

When scattering is nearly isotropic and the medium is weakly absorbing, the BTE reduces to the neutron diffusion equation:

−∇·D(r,E)∇φ + Σ_a φ = Q

D = 1/(3Σ_tr) = 1/(3(Σ_t − μ̄Σ_s))

where D is the diffusion coefficient and μ̄ = ⟨cos θ_scat⟩ is the average scattering cosine.

Diffusion fails near: Boundaries, strong absorbers (control rods), voids, and highly anisotropic scattering media. The full transport equation is required in these regions.
Conservation Laws
Particle Conservation▶

Integrating the BTE over all phase space (r,Ω,E) gives the global particle balance. In steady state: Total production = Total absorption + leakage.

Reciprocity Relation▶
⟨Ψ₁, Q₂⟩ = ⟨Ψ₂⁺, Q₁⟩

The response to source Q₁ due to detector Q₂ equals the response to source Q₂ due to adjoint detector Q₁. Used in detector design to replace expensive forward calculations with adjoint ones.

Green's Functions▶

The transport Green's function G(r,Ω,E | r',Ω',E') gives the flux at (r,Ω,E) due to a point source at (r',Ω',E'). By linearity:

Ψ(r,Ω,E) = ∫ G(r,Ω,E|r',Ω',E') S(r',Ω',E') dV' dΩ' dE'
Legendre Expansion of Scattering Kernel

For azimuthally symmetric scattering, the kernel depends only on μ₀ = Ω'·Ω:

Σ_s(E'→E, Ω'→Ω) = Σ_l Σ_s,l(E'→E) · (2l+1)/4π · P_l(μ₀)

where P_l are Legendre polynomials. Truncating at l=L gives the P_L approximation (l=0: isotropic, l=1: linearly anisotropic used in diffusion theory).

Common Misconceptions
❌ "Flux is particle density." — No, φ = n·v has units of cm⁻²·s⁻¹, not cm⁻³.
❌ "Diffusion theory is always accurate." — It fails near boundaries, voids, and strong absorbers.
❌ "k_eff > 1 means an explosion." — Prompt criticality (k_prompt ≥ 1) is dangerous; delayed-supercriticality is the actual concern in reactors.
❌ "The BTE is Boltzmann's original equation." — The original 1872 equation describes ideal gases; the nuclear transport version is the linear (non-colliding particles) specialization.
