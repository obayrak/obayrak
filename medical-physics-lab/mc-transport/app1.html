📖 Complete Radiation Transport Theory

This section covers the linear Boltzmann equation, photon/neutron interactions, Monte Carlo and deterministic solution methods, cross‑section handling, variance reduction, and validation.

1. The Linear Boltzmann Transport Equation

The steady‑state transport equation for neutral particles (photons, neutrons) in a medium is:

Ω·∇ψ(r,Ω,E) + Σt(r,E) ψ(r,Ω,E) = Q(r,Ω,E) + ∫ dE' ∫ dΩ' Σs(r,E'→E, Ω'·Ω) ψ(r,Ω',E')

where ψ is the angular flux (particles/cm²/sr/MeV), Σt the total macroscopic cross section, and the right side includes the fixed source Q and the scattering source. For photons, Σs includes Compton, Rayleigh, and pair production contributions.

Integral Form

Integrating along a characteristic line gives the integral transport equation for the collision density Ψ = Σt ψ:

Ψ(P) = ∫ Ψ(P') C(P' → P) dP' + Qc(P)

where C is the collision kernel, describing the probability that a particle emerging from a collision at P' will next collide at P. This Fredholm equation is the foundation of Monte Carlo simulation.

2. Photon Interactions

2.1 Photoelectric Effect

Photon is absorbed by a bound electron; the electron is ejected with kinetic energy Ee = hν – Be. Cross section scaling: σpe ∝ Zn/E3.5 (n ≈ 4–5). In our simulation, full photon energy is deposited locally.

2.2 Compton Scattering

Incoherent scattering from a free electron. Scattered photon energy: E' = E / [1 + (E/mec²)(1 – cosθ)]. The differential cross section is given by the Klein–Nishina formula. Sampling uses Kahn's rejection method to select E' and then the scattering angle.

2.3 Pair Production

Threshold 2mec² = 1.022 MeV. In the nuclear field, the photon converts to an electron‑positron pair. Excess energy is shared as kinetic energy. The simulation deposits the kinetic energy and creates two 511 keV annihilation photons (one tracked).

2.4 Rayleigh Scattering

Coherent elastic scattering from bound electrons; no energy loss. Angular distribution is sharply forward‑peaked. We use a small‑angle approximation (deflection ∝ 1/θ²).

3. Neutron Transport

For the mixed γ+n source (Cf‑252), neutrons are generated with energies up to ~10 MeV. Interactions include elastic scattering (energy loss depends on target mass), inelastic scattering, radiative capture, and fission. The current implementation simplifies to elastic scattering and capture using energy‑dependent cross sections. Neutron tracks are shown in blue.

4. Charged Particle Transport (Conceptual)

While not tracked explicitly in this simulation, the underlying theory for electrons/positrons includes:

  • Continuous slowing‑down (CSDA): Bethe‑Bloch formula for collisional stopping power: dE/dx = K·(Z/A)·(1/β²)[½ ln(2mec²β²γ²Tmax/I²) – β² – δ/2].
  • Multiple Coulomb scattering: Molière or Highland theory for angular deflections.
  • Bremsstrahlung: Radiation loss proportional to Z² and energy, dominant at high energies.

In our photon simulation, secondary electrons are not transported; their energy is deposited locally (kerma approximation).

5. Monte Carlo Solution Algorithm

The analog Monte Carlo method solves the integral transport equation by simulating particle histories:

  1. Source sampling: Position, direction, energy from user‑defined distributions.
  2. Free flight: Distance to next collision L = –ln(ξ) / Σt(E).
  3. Geometry tracking: Ray‑box intersection; move to the nearest boundary or collision point.
  4. Interaction selection: Random number selects channel based on partial cross sections.
  5. Post‑collision update: New energy and direction according to physics model.
  6. Scoring: Energy deposition grid, detector spectrum tally.
  7. Russian roulette / splitting: Weight‑based population control.
  8. Termination: Particle killed if escaped, absorbed, or weight below cutoff.

6. Deterministic Methods

  • SN (Discrete Ordinates): Angular domain discretised into M directions; spatial differencing (diamond, step) and source iteration. Acceleration by diffusion synthetic acceleration (DSA).
  • PN (Spherical Harmonics): Angular flux expanded in spherical harmonics; P1 gives diffusion equation.
  • Method of Characteristics (MOC): Integrates along tracks; used in reactor physics for complex geometries.

7. Cross‑Section Libraries & Scaling

Macroscopic cross sections (cm⁻¹) are built from reference values at 662 keV. Energy dependence is approximated by power laws: Σ(E) = Σref·(E/662 keV)n. Exponents: photoelectric ≈ –3.2, Compton ≈ –0.45, pair ≈ +1.8 (above threshold). This avoids large data tables while preserving qualitative behaviour.

8. Variance Reduction Techniques

  • Source biasing: Directional probability density adjusted toward detector; weight corrected by w = punbiased/pbiased.
  • Russian roulette: If weight < 0.01, survival probability 0.5; survivors have weight doubled.
  • Forced collision: In shield, interaction is forced with weight multiplied by (1 – e–Σd).

9. Validation & Benchmarks

  • Attenuation: Simulation reproduces exponential transmission exp(–μx) for narrow‑beam geometry.
  • Compton edge: Spectrum shows edge at E/(1+2E/mec²).
  • XCOM comparison: Relative photoelectric/Compton fractions match NIST data within ~20%.
  • Deterministic benchmarks: Simple slab problems can be compared with SN solutions.

10. Output Interpretation

Energy deposition map: Spatial distribution of deposited energy (keV per history).
Detector spectrum: Histogram of photon energies reaching the detector, including partial energy depositions.
Dose rate (µSv/hr): Converted from energy deposited in detector mass using 1 Gy ≈ 1 Sv for photons.
Figure of Merit (FOM): 1/(σ²·T), where σ is relative uncertainty of detected counts and T is CPU time.

📖 User Manual – How to Use the Simulation

1. Overview

This interactive tool simulates photon (and optionally neutron) transport through a 2D geometry using the Monte Carlo method. You can adjust source, shielding, detector, and physics parameters and observe real‑time particle tracks and tallies.

2. Parameters

  • Source Type: Choose between monoenergetic gamma sources (Cs‑137, Co‑60, Am‑241, I‑131, Na‑22), an X‑ray source, custom monoenergetic options, or a mixed gamma‑neutron source (Cf‑252).
  • Intensity: Number of particles to simulate per batch (affects statistical precision).
  • Shielding Material & Thickness: Material and thickness (cm) of the shielding block placed between source and detector. Expanded library now includes lead, iron, tungsten, depleted uranium, aluminum, concrete, borated polyethylene, and water.
  • Detector Type & Width: NaI, HPGe, or plastic scintillator; width controls the sensitive volume.
  • Photon Interactions: Checkboxes to enable/disable each interaction. Disable all but one to isolate its effect (e.g., pure Compton scattering).
  • Variance Reduction: Source biasing (preferential emission toward detector), Russian roulette (kill low‑weight particles), forced collision (always interact in shield).

3. Controls

Buttons: Run/Stop continuous simulation, Single Shot (one particle), Burst ×10, Reset (clear tallies).
Keyboard: Space = Run/Stop, R = Reset, S = Single Shot, B = Burst ×10, 0 = Reset view (zoom/pan).
Mouse: Scroll to zoom, drag to pan, hover to inspect material and cross section.

4. Display

  • Canvas: 2D geometry with source (yellow dot), shield (grey block), detector (green block). Particle tracks are drawn as colored lines. Energy deposition is shown as a semi‑transparent heatmap.
  • Signal Analyzer: A live spectrum bar chart appears directly above the detector, showing the detected energy distribution.
  • Legend: Colors for particle tracks and materials.
  • Status Bar: Total histories, detected counts, absorbed, escaped, estimated dose rate, figure of merit (FOM), and computing time.

5. Interpretation

Dose Rate: Approximate dose behind the detector; increases with poor shielding or high source intensity.
FOM: Higher means better computational efficiency; decreases if many particles miss the detector.
Spectrum: Look for the photopeak (full energy), Compton continuum, and backscatter peak.

6. Tips

  • To see pure Compton scattering, disable PE, Pair, and Rayleigh; the spectrum will show only the Compton continuum and edge.
  • For shielding studies, increase thickness and run many histories to reduce statistical noise.
  • Use Single Shot to trace a single particle and understand the interactions step‑by‑step.
  • Try the new materials: depleted uranium offers excellent gamma attenuation; borated polyethylene is effective for neutron shielding (in mixed mode).
  • If the simulation is too slow, reduce the number of particles or disable some interactions.
