Deterministic chaos

This section explains the theory behind the four simulators: what chaos is, how it is measured (Lyapunov exponents, Poincaré sections, bifurcation diagrams), and how each model system becomes chaotic. The Try it buttons open the matching preset.

1. What is chaos?

A system is deterministic if its present state fixes its whole future: there is no randomness in the equations. Even so, deterministic systems can behave in a way that is unpredictable in practice. Chaos is long-term aperiodic behaviour in a bounded deterministic system that shows sensitive dependence on initial conditions (Strogatz). Three ingredients are needed:

  • Aperiodicity: the trajectory never settles onto a fixed point, a periodic orbit or a quasi-periodic orbit.
  • Boundedness: the motion stays in a finite region of phase space, so it is not simply escaping to infinity.
  • Sensitivity: nearby trajectories separate exponentially fast on average. This is measured by a positive Lyapunov exponent.

Boundedness and exponential separation together force the flow to stretch and fold phase space repeatedly, like kneading dough. Folding keeps trajectories bounded; stretching separates them. Repeated folding gives strange attractors their fractal, layered structure.

Dimension requirement. By the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem, an autonomous flow \(\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf F(\mathbf x)\) in two dimensions cannot be chaotic. Chaos needs at least three dimensions: the Lorenz system has 3, the driven pendulum has 2 plus the drive phase, and the double pendulum has 4. A one-dimensional map such as the logistic map can be chaotic because it is not invertible: two points can map to the same image.

2. Lyapunov exponents

Let two trajectories start a small distance \(\delta_0\) apart. In a chaotic system the separation grows on average as

\[|\delta(t)|\approx|\delta_0|\,e^{\lambda t},\qquad \lambda=\lim_{t\to\infty}\lim_{\delta_0\to0}\frac1t\ln\frac{|\delta(t)|}{|\delta_0|}.\]
\(\lambda>0\): chaos. \(\lambda=0\): marginal (limit cycles, quasi-periodicity, onset of chaos). \(\lambda<0\): convergence to a stable fixed point or periodic orbit.

For a one-dimensional map \(x_{n+1}=f(x_n)\), the chain rule gives an exact formula:

\[\lambda=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\ln|f'(x_n)| .\]

How the simulator computes λ (Benettin's method)

A raw separation cannot grow forever because the attractor is bounded, so \(\ln|\delta(t)|\) eventually saturates (look at the plateau in the separation plots). The simulators therefore run a shadow trajectory at distance \(d_0\). After each step they add \(\ln(d/d_0)\) to a running sum and pull the shadow back to distance \(d_0\) along the current separation direction:

\[\lambda\approx\frac{1}{t}\sum_k\ln\frac{d_k}{d_0}.\]
This is the largest Lyapunov exponent \(\lambda_1\). Separately, the orange twin trajectory is never renormalised: it shows the butterfly effect directly. Its initial growth follows the green line of slope \(\lambda_1\).

The predictability horizon

If the initial state is known to accuracy \(\delta_0\) and the prediction is useless once the error reaches \(\Delta\), then

\[t_{\text{p}}\approx\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\frac{\Delta}{\delta_0}.\]
The horizon grows only logarithmically with the measurement accuracy. Measuring the initial state 1000 times more precisely adds just \(\ln(1000)/\lambda\approx6.9/\lambda\). This is why weather forecasts are limited to about two weeks.

3. The logistic map

\[x_{n+1}=f(x_n)=r\,x_n(1-x_n),\qquad 0\le x\le1,\ 0\le r\le4 .\]

The map models a population with limited resources: growth at rate \(r\), with crowding suppressing growth. It is the simplest system that shows the complete period-doubling route to chaos.

Fixed points and their stability

A fixed point satisfies \(x^*=f(x^*)\), which gives \(x^*=0\) or \(x^*=1-1/r\). It is stable if \(|f'(x^*)|<1\). Since \(f'(x)=r(1-2x)\):

\[f'(0)=r,\qquad f'(1-1/r)=2-r\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ x^*=1-\tfrac1r\ \text{is stable for } 1<r<3 .\]
At \(r=3\), \(f'(x^*)=-1\): a flip (period-doubling) bifurcation. A stable 2-cycle is born, made of two fixed points of \(f\circ f\). Set the cobweb order to k = 2 to see them.

Windows, intermittency and fully developed chaos

  • Periodic windows: the chaotic band is interrupted by stable windows. The largest is the period-3 window, which starts at \(r=1+\sqrt8\approx3.8284\) through a tangent (saddle-node) bifurcation. By the Li–Yorke theorem, “period three implies chaos”.
  • Intermittency: just below the window, the orbit spends long laminar stretches that look almost period-3, interrupted by irregular bursts. The mean laminar length scales as \((r_c-r)^{-1/2}\) (Pomeau–Manneville).
  • r = 4: the substitution \(x_n=\sin^2(\pi\theta_n)\) turns the map into the doubling map \(\theta_{n+1}=2\theta_n \bmod 1\), so \(\lambda=\ln2\approx0.693\) exactly. Each iteration loses one bit of information about the initial condition.

4. The Lorenz system

\[\dot x=\sigma(y-x),\qquad \dot y=x(\rho-z)-y,\qquad \dot z=xy-\beta z .\]
Edward Lorenz (1963) obtained these equations by truncating the equations of Rayleigh–Bénard convection. \(x\) is the speed of the convection roll; \(y\) and \(z\) describe the temperature distribution. \(\sigma\) is the Prandtl number, \(\rho\) the reduced Rayleigh number (the heating), and \(\beta\) a geometric factor.

Structure

  • Symmetry: \((x,y,z)\to(-x,-y,z)\) leaves the equations unchanged, which is why the attractor has two symmetric wings.
  • Dissipation: \(\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf F=-(\sigma+1+\beta)\). Every volume of phase space shrinks as \(V(t)=V_0e^{-(\sigma+1+\beta)t}\); with the classic parameters, by a factor of about \(10^{-6}\) per unit time. The attractor therefore has zero volume. Its fractal dimension is about 2.06.
  • Lyapunov spectrum (classic parameters): \((\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)\approx(0.906,\ 0,\ -14.57)\). The sum equals the divergence, −13.67.

The Lorenz map

Lorenz noticed that the successive maxima \(z_n\) of \(z(t)\) are almost exactly related by a one-dimensional curve \(z_{n+1}=g(z_n)\), shaped like a tent with a cusp. Its slope satisfies \(|g'|>1\) everywhere, so small differences grow at every step. This shows that the three-dimensional flow is chaotic in essentially the same way as a one-dimensional map, and links tabs 1 and 2.

5. The double pendulum: Hamiltonian chaos

The double pendulum consists of two point masses on massless rigid rods, with no friction and no small-angle approximation. Its Lagrangian is

\[L=\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2+\tfrac12m_2L_2^2\dot\theta_2^2+m_2L_1L_2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)+(m_1+m_2)gL_1\cos\theta_1+m_2gL_2\cos\theta_2 .\]
The Euler–Lagrange equations give \(\ddot\theta_1\) and \(\ddot\theta_2\), with \(\Delta=\theta_1-\theta_2\) and \(D=2m_1+m_2-m_2\cos2\Delta\):
\[\ddot\theta_1=\frac{-g(2m_1+m_2)\sin\theta_1-m_2g\sin(\theta_1-2\theta_2)-2m_2\sin\Delta\,(\dot\theta_2^2L_2+\dot\theta_1^2L_1\cos\Delta)}{L_1D}\] \[\ddot\theta_2=\frac{2\sin\Delta\,\big(\dot\theta_1^2L_1(m_1+m_2)+g(m_1+m_2)\cos\theta_1+\dot\theta_2^2L_2m_2\cos\Delta\big)}{L_2D}\]

The energy \(E=T+V\) is conserved. The simulator reports the relative drift \(|E-E_0|/|E_0|\) as a check on the numerical accuracy.

No attractors: Liouville's theorem

A Hamiltonian flow preserves phase-space volume (\(\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf F=0\)), so there are no attractors. A chaotic orbit does not settle onto a fractal set; it wanders through a chaotic sea on its energy surface. Energy conservation does not prevent chaos.

KAM theory and the Poincaré section

At low energy the motion is close to the two linear normal modes. For \(L_1=L_2\), \(m_1=m_2\), these have \(\theta_2/\theta_1=\pm\sqrt2\) and frequencies \(\omega_\pm^2=(2\mp\sqrt2)\,g/L\). The motion lies on invariant tori, which appear in the Poincaré section as smooth closed curves. The Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser (KAM) theorem says that most tori survive small nonlinear perturbations. As the energy increases they break up one by one, so the section shows regular islands embedded in a chaotic sea. Above a certain energy the arms can flip over the top and the motion becomes strongly chaotic.

6. The driven damped pendulum

\[\ddot\theta+\beta\dot\theta+\sin\theta=A\cos(\omega t)\]
This is the dimensionless form: time is measured in units of \(\sqrt{L/g}\), and \(\beta=1/q\) is the damping. The phase space is three-dimensional, \((\theta,\omega,\varphi=\omega t\bmod2\pi)\), so chaos is possible. The same equation describes a Josephson junction and a charge-density wave.

The stroboscopic Poincaré map

Sampling \((\theta,\dot\theta)\) once per drive period \(T=2\pi/\omega\) turns the flow into a two-dimensional map. A period-\(n\) orbit gives \(n\) points. A chaotic orbit fills a fractal set: a slice through the strange attractor that shows the folded layers. The simulator discards a number of transient periods first, so that the points lie on the attractor.

8. Numerical considerations

  • Integration: all flows use the classical 4th-order Runge–Kutta method, with local error \(O(\Delta t^5)\) and global error \(O(\Delta t^4)\). The Lorenz system uses \(\Delta t=0.005\), the double pendulum \(\Delta t=0.002\) s, and the driven pendulum 400 steps per drive period, so that the Poincaré samples fall exactly at \(t=nT\).
  • Round-off is amplified: in a chaotic system any numerical error grows like \(e^{\lambda t}\), so no simulation follows the “true” trajectory for long. The shadowing lemma guarantees, for many systems, that the computed orbit stays close to some true orbit. Statistical properties such as λ, the shape of the attractor and the Poincaré section are therefore reliable.
  • RK4 is not symplectic: for the double pendulum the energy drifts slowly. Keep the relative drift below about 10⁻⁵; if it grows, reduce the speed.

9. Exercises

1. Show that the fixed point \(x^*=1-1/r\) of the logistic map is stable exactly when \(1<r<3\).

Answer

\(f'(x^*)=r(1-2x^*)=r(1-2+2/r)=2-r\). Stability requires \(|2-r|<1\), i.e. \(1<r<3\). At \(r=3\), \(f'=-1\) and a period-2 cycle is born.

2. Estimate Feigenbaum's δ from \(r_1=3\), \(r_2=3.449490\), \(r_3=3.544090\) and \(r_4=3.564407\).

Answer

\(\delta_2=0.449490/0.094600=4.751\) and \(\delta_3=0.094600/0.020317=4.656\). These converge toward 4.6692.

3. For the Lorenz system (\(\lambda_1=0.906\)), how long can you predict the state if \(\delta_0=10^{-8}\) and the tolerance is \(\Delta=1\)? How much longer if \(\delta_0\) is 1000 times smaller?

Answer

\(t_p=\ln(10^8)/0.906=18.42/0.906\approx20.3\) time units. Improving \(\delta_0\) by \(10^3\) adds only \(\ln(10^3)/0.906\approx7.6\) units. Check it with the δ₀ slider and the separation plot.

4. By what factor does a small volume of phase space shrink in one time unit of the Lorenz flow (σ = 10, β = 8/3)?

Answer

\(e^{-(\sigma+1+\beta)}=e^{-13.67}\approx1.2\times10^{-6}\).

5. Compute \(\rho_H\) for σ = 10, β = 8/3 and confirm with the simulator that ρ = 24 converges to C± from a nearby start but ρ = 26 does not.

Answer

\(\rho_H=10(10+8/3+3)/(10-8/3-1)=10\cdot15.667/6.333=24.74\). Near ρ = 24 the attractor may still coexist with C± (24.06 < ρ < 24.74), so the result depends on the initial state.

6. Why can the Lyapunov exponent of the double pendulum be positive even though its energy is constant?

Answer

Energy conservation restricts the motion to a 3-dimensional energy surface but does not prevent stretching within it. Liouville's theorem forces the Lyapunov exponents to come in pairs \(\pm\lambda\) summing to zero, so a positive \(\lambda\) is balanced by a contracting direction. There is no attractor, but the motion is still chaotic.
