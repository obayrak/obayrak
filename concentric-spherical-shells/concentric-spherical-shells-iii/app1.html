Understanding how electric fields and potentials interact across multiple charged layers

Understanding how electric fields and potentials interact across multiple charged layers is a classic and fundamental physics problem. Because the shells are insulators, the charge does not flow or redistribute; it remains fixed in place.

To solve this, we rely on two powerful principles: Gauss's Law (to find the electric field) and the Principle of Superposition (to find the electric potential).

Here is the step-by-step physical breakdown of how we obtain the Electric Field E(r), the Electric Potential V(r), and the Potential Energy U(r) for a test charge qₚ moving in from infinity.

1. Electric Field E(r) via Gauss's Law

Gauss's Law states that the electric flux through a closed spherical Gaussian surface of radius r is proportional to the enclosed charge:

∮ E · dA = qenc / ε₀

Because of spherical symmetry, this simplifies to:

E(r) = k qenc / r²

where k = 1 / (4π ε₀). In this simulator we use k = 1 for visualization scaling.

Assuming we have a solid inner insulating core (radius R₁, charge q₁), Shell K (radius R₂, charge q₂), and Shell L (radius R₃, charge q₃), the field is divided into four distinct regions:

  • Inside the Core (r < R₁):
    E(r) = k q₁ r / R₁³
  • Between Core and Shell K (R₁ ≤ r < R₂):
    E(r) = k q₁ / r²
  • Between Shell K and Shell L (R₂ ≤ r < R₃):
    E(r) = k (q₁ + q₂) / r²
  • Outside Shell L (r ≥ R₃):
    E(r) = k (q₁ + q₂ + q₃) / r²

2. Electric Potential V(r) via Superposition

The electric potential at a distance r is defined as the work done per unit charge to bring a positive test charge from infinity to r:

V(r) = -∫∞→r E · dr

Instead of integrating the piecewise electric field directly, it is mathematically much easier to use the Principle of Superposition. We calculate the potential created by each object individually as if the others were not there, and simply add them together:

V(r) = Vcore + VK + VL

For a thin charged shell of radius R and charge Q:

  • Outside the shell (r ≥ R):
    V(r) = kQ / r
  • Inside the shell (r < R):
    V(r) = kQ / R

For the solid uniformly charged insulating core of radius R₁:

  • Outside (r ≥ R₁):
    V(r) = kq₁ / r
  • Inside (r < R₁):
    V(r) = (kq₁ / 2R₁) (3 - r²/R₁²)

To find the total potential V(r) at any distance, you simply calculate the value for each of the three components at that specific r and sum them up.

3. Potential Energy U(r)

Electric potential V(r) is a property of the space created by the shells. When you bring a specific particle with charge qₚ into this space from infinity, it gains or loses potential energy based on its interaction with that potential.

U(r) = qₚ V(r)

Important: this means the potential energy depends on the projectile charge, but the potential does not. So if you change the projectile charge slider, the V(r) graph remains unchanged while the U(r) graph changes.

How to read the simulator

  • 3D shells: show the physical geometry of the core and two insulating shells.
  • Cross-section cut: reveals the inside layers clearly.
  • Blue field arrows: show the direction of the electric field at sample points in space.
  • Animated streamlines: indicate how field direction changes across space.
  • Red projectile: is the test charge moving radially from the left toward the center.
  • Yellow force vector: shows the force on the projectile:
    F = qₚ E
  • E(r) graph: shows the electric field magnitude/sign versus radius.
  • V(r) graph: shows the electric potential created by the shells.
  • U(r) graph: shows the projectile's potential energy.
  • Vertical dashed lines: mark R₁, R₂, and R₃.
