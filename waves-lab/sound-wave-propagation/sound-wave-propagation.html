Speed of Sound

General Form (Newton-Laplace)

v = √(B / ρ)

Where B is the bulk modulus (elastic modulus for compression) and ρ is the medium density. This applies universally to all media.

In an Ideal Gas

v = √(γ · P / ρ) = √(γ · R · T / M)

γ = adiabatic index (1.40 for diatomic, 1.67 for monatomic), R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), T = temperature in Kelvin, M = molar mass in kg/mol. Crucially, in an ideal gas the pressure and density effects cancel — speed depends on temperature only.

For air: v ≈ 331.3 · √(T / 273.15) m/s Simplified: v ≈ 331.3 + 0.606 · T_°C m/s

Temperature Dependence

Every 1°C rise in air temperature increases the speed by ≈ 0.6 m/s. At 20°C, v ≈ 343 m/s; at 100°C, v ≈ 386 m/s; at −30°C, v ≈ 313 m/s.

The Doppler Effect

General Formula

f_obs = f₀ · (v + v_obs) / (v + v_src) Sign convention: v_obs > 0 when moving TOWARD source v_src > 0 when moving AWAY from observer (both positive = approaching each other)

The observed frequency changes because the relative motion between source and observer alters the number of wave crests arriving per second. A source approaching compresses wavefronts (higher frequency); a receding source stretches them (lower frequency).

Source Moving, Observer Still

f_ahead = f₀ · v / (v − v_s) [approaching] f_behind = f₀ · v / (v + v_s) [receding]

Observer Moving, Source Still

f_approaching = f₀ · (v + v_o) / v f_receding = f₀ · (v − v_o) / v
Mach Regimes
Mach Number: M = v_source / v_sound Mach Cone Half-Angle: sin(μ) = 1 / M Cone half-angle: μ = arcsin(v_sound / v_source)

Sonic Boom

When M ≥ 1, the wavefronts emitted by the source cannot escape ahead of it. They accumulate in a conical shock wave (Mach cone). An observer first hears silence (cone has not arrived), then a sudden intense pressure jump — the sonic boom.

Wavelength Compression

λ_ahead = (v − v_s) / f₀ [compressed] λ_behind = (v + v_s) / f₀ [stretched] λ_ratio = (v + v_s) / (v − v_s)
Acoustic Intensity & Impedance

Acoustic Impedance

Z = ρ · v [Pa·s/m = Rayl]

Higher impedance means a stiffer medium. Impedance mismatch between media causes reflections. Steel has Z ≈ 47 MRayl while air has Z ≈ 420 Rayl — a ratio of over 100,000:1.

Intensity

I = p² / (2·ρ·v) = p²/(2Z) [W/m²] SPL (dB) = 20·log₁₀(p / p₀) p₀ = 20 μPa (threshold of hearing)

Inverse Square Law

I ∝ 1 / r² Each doubling of distance → −6 dB

Beat Frequency
f_beat = |f_obs_front − f_obs_behind|

When both the approaching and receding wavefronts arrive at the same observer (e.g., a source passing overhead), the two slightly different frequencies interfere to produce beats — a pulsating amplitude variation.

The frequency drop heard as an ambulance passes is called the "Doppler shift" — not a sudden jump, but a continuous sweep as the approach angle changes.
How Sound Propagates

Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave — particles in the medium oscillate back and forth in the same direction as the wave travels. The source creates alternating compressions (high pressure) and rarefactions (low pressure) that travel outward in all directions as expanding shells.

In 2D, these appear as expanding circles. In 3D, they are spheres. The speed at which these fronts travel depends entirely on the medium's elastic and inertial properties — not on the source's motion.

Sound needs a medium. It cannot travel through a vacuum. Light (electromagnetic wave) can — that's why you see lightning before you hear thunder.
The Doppler Effect: Step by Step

1. Stationary Source

Wavefronts are perfectly concentric. An observer at any position receives exactly f₀ Hz. The wavelength is uniform in all directions: λ = v / f₀.

2. Slowly Moving Source (Low Mach)

As the source moves, each new wavefront is emitted from a slightly advanced position. Wavefronts in the direction of motion are closer together (shorter λ, higher f). Behind the source, wavefronts are more spread apart (longer λ, lower f). The source is still inside all its wavefronts.

3. Fast Moving Source (High Subsonic)

As M → 1, the source nearly catches up to its own wavefronts. In front, wavefronts pile up extremely close together. The frequency observed ahead becomes very high. The bunching becomes severe near M = 0.9–0.99.

4. At Mach 1 (Transonic)

All wavefronts emitted ahead converge to a single point — the source itself. The source is moving exactly at the speed of sound, so the leading edge of every wave stays at the source position. This forms a flat wavefront (the "sound barrier"). f_obs ahead → ∞.

5. Supersonic (M > 1)

The source outpaces all its wavefronts. The emitted spheres trail behind. Their outer edges form a cone-shaped envelope: the Mach cone. The half-angle is sin⁻¹(1/M). An observer in silence until the Mach cone passes — then hears the sonic boom, carrying all compressed wavefronts simultaneously.

How to Use This Simulator

2D Tab

The source (red dot) emits circular wavefronts. Use the Source Velocity slider or the preset buttons (Still, Slow, Mach 1, etc.) to change its speed. The observer (green dot) can be repositioned by clicking on the canvas. The bottom bar shows observed frequencies from different directions and the beat frequency.

3D Tab

Spherical wavefronts are rendered in a perspective projection. Use the Camera Rotation and Elevation sliders to orbit around the scene. The Direction sliders (Azimuth + Elevation) let you aim the source in 3D space.

Medium Controls

Select a preset medium or switch to Custom and manually set temperature, pressure, density, and γ. Watch the sound speed readout change — this directly affects wavefront spacing and all Doppler calculations.

Real-World Analogues

Everyday Examples

Ambulance siren: High pitch approaching (wavefronts compressed), sudden lower pitch as it passes — the classic Doppler sweep. At highway speeds (~120 km/h), the frequency ratio is about 1.12:1 (12% higher approaching).

Weather Doppler radar: Radio waves (same physics, different medium) reflect from rain droplets. Approaching precipitation returns a higher frequency than receding — revealing wind speed and tornado rotation.

Astronomical red/blueshift: Galaxies receding from Earth have their light Doppler-shifted to longer (red) wavelengths. This is how Hubble discovered the expanding universe in 1929.

Sonic boom: A supersonic aircraft carries its Mach cone with it. The cone edge sweeps across the ground and each observer hears a single sharp crack — all compressed wavefronts arriving at once. The boom is not heard until the cone arrives, so the aircraft is already overhead when the boom hits.

Ultrasound Doppler is used in cardiology to measure blood flow velocity — the frequency shift of reflected ultrasound reveals how fast blood cells are moving.
