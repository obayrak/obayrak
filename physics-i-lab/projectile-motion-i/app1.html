Projectile Motion

Equation Derivation · Drag Force · Analytical & Numerical (RK4) · Trajectory Optimization

📐   Theory — Deriving and solving the equations of motion ▲
01Free-Body Diagram & Newton's 2nd Law

A projectile of mass m moves in 2D. Two forces act on it: gravity and (optionally) aerodynamic drag. Separating into x and y components via F = ma:

  • x
    Horizontal: only drag acts (no gravity component) → mẍ = F_drag,x
  • y
    Vertical: gravity pulls down, drag opposes upward motion → mÿ = −mg + F_drag,y

Without drag (vacuum): the axes decouple completely:

ẍ = 0    ÿ = −g

Initial conditions: x(0)=0, y(0)=0, ẋ(0)=v₀cosθ, ẏ(0)=v₀sinθ.

The two ODEs are independent — horizontal motion is uniform (constant velocity), vertical motion is uniformly accelerated (constant deceleration).

02Analytical Solution (No Drag)

Integrating the decoupled ODEs twice with initial conditions gives closed-form expressions:

x(t) = v₀·cosθ · t
y(t) = v₀·sinθ · t − ½·g·t²
vx(t) = v₀·cosθ  (constant!)
vy(t) = v₀·sinθ − g·t

Key derived quantities:

QuantityFormula
Time of flightT = 2·v₀·sinθ / g
Max heightH = (v₀·sinθ)² / (2g)
RangeR = v₀²·sin(2θ) / g
Optimal angleθ_opt = 45° (maximises R)

The parabolic trajectory y = x·tanθ − gx²/(2v₀²cos²θ) follows directly from eliminating t.

03Aerodynamic Drag — Why the ODE becomes Nonlinear

At typical speeds, drag force is quadratic in speed and always opposes the velocity vector:

F_drag = −½·ρ·Cd·A·|v|² · v̂

where: ρ = air density, Cd = drag coefficient, A = cross-sectional area, v̂ = unit velocity vector.

Let k = ½·ρ·Cd·A/m. The component equations become:

ẍ = −k·|v|·vx
ÿ = −g − k·|v|·vy

where |v| = √(vx² + vy²). These are now coupled and nonlinear — no closed-form solution exists. Numerical integration (RK4) is required.

Consequence: range is reduced, optimal angle drops below 45° (typically 30–40°), and the trajectory becomes asymmetric — steeper on the descending side.

04Numerical Integration — RK4 on the State Vector

The two 2nd-order ODEs are rewritten as four coupled 1st-order equations using the state vector [x, y, vx, vy]:

ẋ = vx    ẏ = vy
v̇x = −k·|v|·vx
v̇y = −g − k·|v|·vy

RK4 evaluates four weighted slope estimates per time step h:

k₁ — start
f(tₙ, yₙ)
k₂ — mid est. 1
f(t+h/2, y+h·k₁/2)
k₃ — mid est. 2
f(t+h/2, y+h·k₂/2)
k₄ — end
f(t+h, y+h·k₃)
yₙ₊₁ = yₙ + (h/6)(k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄)

With Cd = 0, RK4 reproduces the analytical solution to machine precision. As Cd increases, trajectories diverge — compare the cyan (analytical vacuum) vs orange (numerical with drag) curves in the simulation.

05Trajectory Characteristics & Energy

Without drag, the total mechanical energy is conserved at every point:

E = ½m|v|² + mgy = ½mv₀² (constant)

This means speed at any height h can be found purely from energy: |v| = √(v₀² − 2gh).

The velocity vector rotates continuously — at peak it points horizontally, on landing it is symmetric with the launch angle (no drag).

Speed at impact = v₀ (no drag, flat ground)

With drag, energy is dissipated and the impact speed is always less than v₀. The drag force does negative work W = ∫F_drag · ds along the path.

06Range Optimisation & the Effect of Drag

Without drag the range formula R = v₀²·sin(2θ)/g is maximised at exactly θ = 45° (since sin(2θ) peaks at 90°).

With quadratic drag this simple result breaks down. Numerically sweeping angles shows the optimal angle shifts below 45° — typically 30–42° depending on the drag parameter k:
