Moment of Inertia of Continuous Mass Distributions

Σ→∫ From Point Masses to a Continuous Rod

For a rigid collection of point masses spinning about a fixed axis, the moment of inertia is a weighted sum of squared distances from that axis:

$$I=\sum_i m_i r_i^2$$

A rod is not a collection of discrete points — it's a continuous smear of mass along a line. The bridge is the same Riemann-sum argument used for charge distributions: slice the rod into $N$ tiny segments of width $\Delta x$, each carrying mass $\Delta m_i\approx\lambda(x_i)\,\Delta x$, small enough to treat as a point at $x_i$.

$$I\approx\sum_{i=1}^N r_i^2\,\lambda(x_i)\,\Delta x\ \xrightarrow[\Delta x\to0]{N\to\infty}\ I=\int r^2\,dm=\int r(x)^2\,\lambda(x)\,dx$$

$\lambda(x)$ is the linear mass density (kg/m) — how much mass sits per unit length at position $x$. When $\lambda$ is uniform it factors outside the integral and $I$ reduces to the textbook results; when it varies with $x$, the shape of $\lambda(x)$ itself becomes part of the physics — a baseball bat, a tapered wind-turbine blade, or a loaded pendulum rod are all rods with non-uniform $\lambda(x)$.

💡 Why this matters practically. Real rotating rods are rarely uniform — a cricket bat is denser near the striking end, a robot arm often tapers, a diving board is reinforced at the mount. The integral $I=\int r^2\lambda\,dx$ handles all of these with the same machinery; only $\lambda(x)$ changes.
📏 Setting Up the Rod
$$dm=\lambda(x)\,dx,\qquad M=\int_0^L\lambda(x)\,dx,\qquad I(a)=\int_0^L (x-a)^2\,\lambda(x)\,dx$$

The rod occupies $x\in[0,L]$. The rotation axis is perpendicular to the rod (think of the rod as a clock hand or helicopter blade), passing through a chosen pivot point at $x=a$ — so $r=|x-a|$ is the distance from a mass element to the axis.

Two Useful Companions

Center of mass: $x_{cm}=\dfrac1M\displaystyle\int_0^L x\,\lambda(x)\,dx$.

Radius of gyration: $k=\sqrt{I/M}$ — the distance at which all the mass could be concentrated as a single point and reproduce the same $I$.

⬡ Seven Density Models, One Integral

This app lets $\lambda(x)$ be any of the following, all built from the same $I(a)=\int_0^L(x-a)^2\lambda(x)\,dx$ engine:

  • Uniform: $\lambda_0$ — the textbook rod.
  • Linear: $\lambda_0\bigl[1+\alpha\,x/L\bigr]$ — heavier toward one end.
  • Quadratic: $\lambda_0\bigl[1+\alpha\,(x/L)^2\bigr]$ — mass concentrated near an end, flat near the other.
  • Exponential: $\lambda_0\,e^{\beta x/L}$ — rapid taper, like a whip or antenna.
  • Gaussian: $\lambda_0\,e^{-(x-x_c)^2/2\sigma^2}$ — a localized mass bump (a weld, a weight clamp).
  • Piecewise: $\lambda_1$ then $\lambda_2$ at a breakpoint — a bat or dumbbell-like step change.
  • User-defined: type any $\lambda(x)$ — the app evaluates it numerically, exactly like the built-in profiles.
⚠️ Density must stay non-negative over $[0,L]$ for the model to represent physical mass — the Simulation tab will still compute a number for a profile that dips negative, but that number no longer corresponds to a real rod.
⟲ The Parallel-Axis Theorem, Previewed

Moving the pivot doesn't require redoing the whole integral. Writing $x-a=(x-x_{cm})+(x_{cm}-a)$ and expanding the square inside $I(a)$ shows that $I(a)$ is an exact parabola in $a$, minimized at $a=x_{cm}$:

$$I(a)=I_{cm}+M\,(a-x_{cm})^2$$

Every profile in this app obeys this exactly — it's a consequence of expanding the square, not an approximation — and the Simulation tab plots $I(a)$ across the whole rod so you can watch the parabola directly.

🖼 Geometry

Every mass element $dm=\lambda(x)\,dx$ sits a distance $r=|x-a|$ from the rotation axis; since $r^2=(x-a)^2$ the absolute value never matters once squared.

∂ The General Integral, Expanded
Write out $(x-a)^2$
$$I(a)=\int_0^L (x-a)^2\lambda(x)\,dx=\int_0^L\bigl[x^2-2ax+a^2\bigr]\lambda(x)\,dx$$
Split into three moments of $\lambda$

Since $a$ is a constant, it comes out of two of the three pieces, leaving three integrals that depend only on the density profile — not on where the pivot is:

$$I(a)=\underbrace{\int_0^L x^2\lambda\,dx}_{I_0}\ -\ 2a\underbrace{\int_0^L x\lambda\,dx}_{S_x}\ +\ a^2\underbrace{\int_0^L \lambda\,dx}_{M}$$

$M$ is the total mass, $S_x$ is the "first moment" (mass $\times$ lever-arm sum), and $I_0=I(0)$ is the moment of inertia about the very end $x=0$. This is the computational trick this app actually uses: compute $M,S_x,I_0$ once per density profile, then $I(a)$ for any pivot $a$ is just plugging into the quadratic above — no re-integration needed.

Center of mass falls out of the same $S_x$
$$x_{cm}=\frac{S_x}{M}$$
Complete the square to isolate $I_{cm}$

Evaluate the Step-2 quadratic at $a=x_{cm}=S_x/M$:

$$I_{cm}=I_0-2\,\frac{S_x}{M}\,S_x+\left(\frac{S_x}{M}\right)^2 M=I_0-\frac{S_x^2}{M}$$

Substituting $S_x=Mx_{cm}$ back into the Step-2 formula and simplifying term by term reproduces exactly the parallel-axis form quoted on the Theory tab:

$$I(a)=I_{cm}+M(a-x_{cm})^2$$
📍 Worked Special Case — the Uniform Rod

Set $\lambda(x)=\lambda_0$ constant, so $M=\lambda_0 L$.

About one end ($a=0$)
$$I_0=\int_0^L x^2\lambda_0\,dx=\lambda_0\left[\frac{x^3}3\right]_0^L=\frac{\lambda_0 L^3}3=\frac13 ML^2$$
About the center ($a=L/2$), via parallel axis
$$S_x=\int_0^L x\lambda_0\,dx=\frac{\lambda_0L^2}2=M\cdot\frac L2\ \Rightarrow\ x_{cm}=\frac L2\quad\text{(as expected by symmetry)}$$
$$I_{cm}=I_0-\frac{S_x^2}{M}=\frac13ML^2-\frac{(ML/2)^2}{M}=\frac13ML^2-\frac14ML^2=\frac1{12}ML^2$$

Both are the standard textbook results — reached here as special cases of the same general $M,S_x,I_0$ machinery this app uses for every other profile.

⚖ Cross-Check: Direct Integration vs the $M,S_x,I_0$ Shortcut

Computing $I(a)$ by directly integrating $(x-a)^2\lambda(x)$ at one specific $a$, and computing it via $I_0-2aS_x+a^2M$ using $M,S_x,I_0$ from a single pass over the rod, must agree for any $a$ — they're algebraically identical, just reorganized. The Simulation tab's comparison table checks this at the current pivot, and the $I(a)$ sweep plot checks it continuously across every pivot position at once.

🔢 Fully Worked Numerical Example

Take a linear rod, $\lambda_0=3$ kg/m, $L=2$ m, $\alpha=0.6$ (denser at $x=L$), pivot at the near end $a=0$.

Mass and first moment
$$M=\lambda_0L\Bigl(1+\tfrac\alpha2\Bigr)=3(2)(1.3)=7.80\ \text{kg}$$
$$S_x=\lambda_0L^2\Bigl(\tfrac12+\tfrac\alpha3\Bigr)=3(4)(0.7)=8.40\ \text{kg·m}\ \Rightarrow\ x_{cm}=S_x/M=1.077\ \text{m}$$
Moment of inertia about the end, then the center
$$I_0=\lambda_0L^3\Bigl(\tfrac13+\tfrac\alpha4\Bigr)=3(8)(0.483)=11.60\ \text{kg·m}^2$$
$$I_{cm}=I_0-\frac{S_x^2}M=11.60-\frac{8.40^2}{7.80}=11.60-9.046=2.554\ \text{kg·m}^2$$

The Simulation tab reproduces $M=7.80$ kg, $x_{cm}=1.077$ m, $I_0=11.60$ kg·m² directly — set the profile to "Linear" with these exact parameters and pivot $a=0$ to check.

Σ→∬ From a Rod to a Rectangular Plate

A flat rectangular plate carries a surface mass density $\sigma(x,y)$ (kg/m²) over $x\in[0,L_x]$, $y\in[0,L_y]$. The rotation axis is perpendicular to the plate (out of the page), passing through a pivot $(a,b)$ in the plane — the plate spins like a turntable or fan blade.

$$dm=\sigma(x,y)\,dA,\qquad M=\iint_A\sigma\,dA,\qquad I(a,b)=\iint_A\bigl[(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2\bigr]\sigma\,dA$$

Every mass element $dm$ sits a distance $r^2=(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2$ from the axis — the plate version of the rod's $r=|x-a|$, now with two coordinates.

💡 This is a polar moment. $I(a,b)$ about an axis perpendicular to the lamina equals $I_x+I_y$ about that same point in-plane (perpendicular-axis theorem) — one integral does the job of two.
⬡ Seven Density Models — Product-Separable

Every profile here is $\sigma(x,y)=\sigma_0\,f_x(x)\,f_y(y)$, so it factors cleanly into an $x$-shape and a $y$-shape:

  • Uniform: $\sigma_0$.
  • Linear: $\sigma_0[1+\alpha x/L_x][1+\alpha y/L_y]$.
  • Quadratic: $\sigma_0[1+\alpha(x/L_x)^2][1+\alpha(y/L_y)^2]$.
  • Exponential: $\sigma_0e^{\beta x/L_x}e^{\beta y/L_y}$.
  • Gaussian: $\sigma_0\exp\!\bigl[-\tfrac{(x-x_c)^2+(y-y_c)^2}{2\sigma^2}\bigr]$ — a localized bump.
  • Piecewise: $\sigma_1$ then $\sigma_2$ at $x=fL_x$, uniform in $y$.
  • User-defined: any $\sigma(x,y)$ typed in, evaluated numerically.
⚠️ Density must stay non-negative on the rectangle for the model to be physical.
⟲ 2D Parallel/Perpendicular-Axis Theorem
$$I(a,b)=I_{cm}+M\bigl[(a-x_{cm})^2+(b-y_{cm})^2\bigr]$$

$I(a,b)$ is an exact paraboloid in $(a,b)$, minimized at the centre of mass — the Simulation tab's contour plot shows this paraboloid directly.

∂ Expanding the Integral
Expand $(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2$
$$I(a,b)=\iint\bigl[x^2-2ax+a^2+y^2-2by+b^2\bigr]\sigma\,dA$$
Split into five moments of $\sigma$, independent of pivot
$$I(a,b)=\underbrace{\iint x^2\sigma\,dA}_{I_{xx0}}+\underbrace{\iint y^2\sigma\,dA}_{I_{yy0}}-2a\underbrace{\iint x\sigma\,dA}_{S_x}-2b\underbrace{\iint y\sigma\,dA}_{S_y}+(a^2+b^2)\underbrace{\iint\sigma\,dA}_{M}$$

Compute $M,S_x,S_y,I_{xx0},I_{yy0}$ once per profile; $I(a,b)$ at any pivot is then just algebra — no re-integration, same trick as the rod's $M,S_x,I_0$.

Centre of mass and completing the square
$$x_{cm}=\frac{S_x}{M},\quad y_{cm}=\frac{S_y}{M},\qquad I_{cm}=I_0-\frac{S_x^2+S_y^2}{M},\quad I_0=I_{xx0}+I_{yy0}$$
Separable profiles factor into 1D integrals

For $\sigma=\sigma_0f_x(x)f_y(y)$, each double integral splits: e.g. $S_x=\iint x f_x f_y\,dA=\sigma_0\Bigl[\int_0^{L_x}\!xf_x\,dx\Bigr]\Bigl[\int_0^{L_y}\!f_y\,dy\Bigr]=\sigma_0A_1B_0$. Define

$$A_n=\int_0^{L_x}x^nf_x(x)\,dx,\qquad B_n=\int_0^{L_y}y^nf_y(y)\,dy\quad(n=0,1,2)$$
$$M=\sigma_0A_0B_0,\ \ S_x=\sigma_0A_1B_0,\ \ S_y=\sigma_0A_0B_1,\ \ I_{xx0}=\sigma_0A_2B_0,\ \ I_{yy0}=\sigma_0A_0B_2$$
📍 Worked Special Case — Uniform Plate

$f_x=f_y=1\Rightarrow A_0=L_x,A_1=L_x^2/2,A_2=L_x^3/3$ (same for $B$ with $L_y$). So $M=\sigma_0L_xL_y$, and about the centre $(L_x/2,L_y/2)$:

$$I_{cm}=\tfrac1{12}M(L_x^2+L_y^2)$$

— the textbook thin-plate result, reached as a special case of the same $A_n,B_n$ machinery used for every other profile.

Σ→∬ From a Plate to an Annular Ring (Washer)

A ring here means a flat annulus: a washer-shaped lamina between an inner radius $R_1$ and outer radius $R_2$, carrying a surface mass density $\sigma(r)$ (kg/m²) that depends only on the radial coordinate $r$ — the ring is azimuthally symmetric, even if it isn't azimuthally uniform (denser near the inner edge vs. the outer edge, for example). The rotation axis is perpendicular to the ring's plane, passing through a pivot $(a,b)$ measured from the ring's own geometric centre.

$$dm=\sigma(r)\,dA=\sigma(r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta,\qquad M=\iint_A\sigma\,dA,\qquad I(a,b)=\iint_A\bigl[(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2\bigr]\sigma\,dA$$

This is the same polar-moment setup used for the Plate tab — but because $\sigma$ has no $\theta$-dependence, the angular integral factors out cleanly and collapses the double integral to a single radial integral, exactly the way the Rod's $\int r^2\lambda\,dx$ machinery worked in 1D.

💡 Why azimuthal symmetry matters. Any mass distribution with $\sigma=\sigma(r)$ only has its centre of mass exactly at the ring's own geometric centre — no first-moment integral needed. That single fact is what makes every formula below so much shorter than the Plate's.
⬡ Seven Density Models, One Radial Integral

Every profile below is a function of $r\in[R_1,R_2]$ only, with $w=R_2-R_1$:

  • Uniform: $\sigma_0$ — the textbook washer.
  • Linear: $\sigma_0\bigl[1+\alpha\,(r-R_1)/w\bigr]$ — denser toward one edge.
  • Quadratic: $\sigma_0\bigl[1+\alpha\,((r-R_1)/w)^2\bigr]$ — concentrated near an edge.
  • Exponential: $\sigma_0\,e^{\beta(r-R_1)/w}$ — rapid radial taper.
  • Gaussian: $\sigma_0\,e^{-(r-r_c)^2/2\sigma^2}$ — a localized radial band (a reinforcing rib).
  • Piecewise: $\sigma_1$ then $\sigma_2$ at a breakpoint radius — a stepped washer.
  • User-defined: type any $\sigma(r)$ — evaluated numerically, same as the built-ins.
⚠️ Density must stay non-negative over $[R_1,R_2]$ for the model to represent physical mass.
⟲ Parallel-Axis Theorem, Simplified by Symmetry
$$I(a,b)=I_{c}+M\,(a^2+b^2)$$

Because the centre of mass sits exactly at the ring's own centre $(0,0)$, the general Plate formula $I(a,b)=I_{cm}+M[(a-x_{cm})^2+(b-y_{cm})^2]$ reduces to this — no offset terms. $I_c$ is the polar moment about the ring's centre, computed once per profile; the Simulation tab's contour plot shows the resulting paraboloid centred exactly at the origin.

🖼 Geometry

A point on the annulus at radius $r\in[R_1,R_2]$ and angle $\theta$ has Cartesian position $x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta$. The rotation axis passes through the pivot $(a,b)$, perpendicular to the ring's plane (out of the page) — so a mass element's distance to the axis is $\rho^2=(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2$, the same Plate-tab setup expressed in polar coordinates, now restricted to an inner-cut-out radial range.

$$x=r\cos\theta,\quad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad dA=r\,dr\,d\theta,\qquad dm=\sigma(r)\,dA=\sigma(r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta,\qquad r\in[R_1,R_2]$$

The highlighted band is a single shell: all mass between radius $r$ and $r+dr$, swept through the full angle $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$. Its total mass and its contribution to $I$ about the ring's own centre come from integrating $d\theta$ around that one shell before touching $r$:

$$dm_{\text{shell}}=\sigma(r)\left[\int_0^{2\pi}r\,d\theta\right]dr=\sigma(r)\,2\pi r\,dr,\qquad dI_{c,\text{shell}}=r^2\,dm_{\text{shell}}=2\pi\sigma(r)\,r^3\,dr$$

The second equality holds with no extra angular work because every point on a shell of radius $r$ sits exactly distance $r$ from the ring's centre, so $r^2$ pulls straight out of the $\theta$-integral — the same symmetry argument as the Disk tab, just with the radial range starting at $R_1$ instead of $0$. Summing (integrating) these shells from $R_1$ to $R_2$ gives $M$ and $I_c$ with no leftover angular dependence.

💡 Off-centre pivot, still simple. Once $M$ and $I_c$ are known from the radial integrals over $[R_1,R_2]$ (about the ring's own centre), moving to any pivot $(a,b)$ is pure algebra via $I(a,b)=I_c+M(a^2+b^2)$ — derived formally in the next step — with no need to redo the shell integral for each new pivot.
∂ Separating the Angular Integral
Expand the pivot-shifted squared distance
$$I(a,b)=\iint\bigl[(r\cos\theta-a)^2+(r\sin\theta-b)^2\bigr]\sigma(r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta$$

Expanding: $(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2 = r^2 - 2ar\cos\theta-2br\sin\theta+a^2+b^2$.

Integrate over $\theta$ first — the cross terms vanish
$$\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,d\theta=0,\qquad\int_0^{2\pi}\sin\theta\,d\theta=0,\qquad\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta=2\pi$$

Since $\sigma(r)$ carries no $\theta$-dependence, every term proportional to $\cos\theta$ or $\sin\theta$ integrates to exactly zero over a full turn — this is the same reasoning that makes $S_x=S_y=0$ for the Plate tab's uniform case, but here it holds for every radial profile, not just uniform ones.

What survives
$$M=2\pi\int_{R_1}^{R_2}\sigma(r)\,r\,dr,\qquad I_c=2\pi\int_{R_1}^{R_2}r^2\,\sigma(r)\,r\,dr=2\pi\int_{R_1}^{R_2}\sigma(r)\,r^3\,dr$$

$I_c$ is simultaneously the moment of inertia about the ring's centre and $I_{cm}$, since $x_{cm}=y_{cm}=0$ by the vanishing integrals above.

Re-attach the pivot terms
$$I(a,b)=I_c+(a^2+b^2)\underbrace{\iint\sigma\,dA}_{M}=I_c+M(a^2+b^2)$$

This app computes $M,I_c$ once per density profile via the radial integrals above; $I(a,b)$ at any pivot is then pure algebra, exactly mirroring the Rod's $M,S_x,I_0$ shortcut and the Plate's $M,S_x,S_y,I_{xx0},I_{yy0}$ shortcut.

📍 Worked Special Case — Uniform Washer

Set $\sigma(r)=\sigma_0$ constant:

$$M=2\pi\sigma_0\int_{R_1}^{R_2}r\,dr=\pi\sigma_0\bigl(R_2^2-R_1^2\bigr)$$
$$I_c=2\pi\sigma_0\int_{R_1}^{R_2}r^3\,dr=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\sigma_0\bigl(R_2^4-R_1^4\bigr)=\tfrac12M\bigl(R_1^2+R_2^2\bigr)$$

Σ→∬ From Ring to Disk — Filling In the Hole

A solid circular disk of radius $R$ carries a surface mass density $\sigma(r)$ (kg/m²) that depends only on the radial coordinate $r\in[0,R]$ — same azimuthal symmetry as the Ring tab, but with no inner cutout. Build it the same way the Rod tab built a 1D rod from point masses: slice the disk into $N$ concentric shells (thin rings) of radius $r_i$ and thickness $\Delta r$, each carrying mass $\Delta m_i\approx\sigma(r_i)\,(2\pi r_i)\,\Delta r$ — circumference $\times$ thickness $\times$ local density — small enough to treat as a thin ring of uniform radius.

$$\Delta m_i\approx\sigma(r_i)\,2\pi r_i\,\Delta r\ \xrightarrow[\Delta r\to0]{N\to\infty}\ dm=\sigma(r)\,2\pi r\,dr,\qquad M=\int_0^R\sigma(r)\,2\pi r\,dr$$

The rotation axis is perpendicular to the disk, passing through a pivot $(a,b)$ measured from the disk's own geometric centre — the disk spins like a flywheel or turntable.

$$I(a,b)=\iint_A\bigl[(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2\bigr]\sigma\,dA$$
💡 The disk is a stack of shells, each a thin Ring-tab washer. Every result below is the $R_1\to0$ limit of the Ring tab's machinery — same radial integral, same vanishing-angular-integral argument, just starting the shells at the centre instead of an inner bore.
⬡ Seven Density Models, One Radial Integral

Every profile is a function of $r\in[0,R]$ only:

  • Uniform: $\sigma_0$ — the textbook disk.
  • Linear: $\sigma_0[1+\alpha\,r/R]$ — denser toward the rim (or centre).
  • Quadratic: $\sigma_0[1+\alpha\,(r/R)^2]$ — mass concentrated near the rim.
  • Exponential: $\sigma_0\,e^{\beta r/R}$ — rapid radial taper.
  • Gaussian: $\sigma_0\,e^{-(r-r_c)^2/2\sigma^2}$ — a reinforcing ridge at radius $r_c$.
  • Piecewise: $\sigma_1$ (hub) then $\sigma_2$ (rim) at a breakpoint radius — a flywheel with a heavier rim.
  • User-defined: any $\sigma(r)$ typed in, evaluated numerically.
⚠️ Density must stay non-negative on $[0,R]$ for the model to be physical. Note $r=0$ is included — a Gaussian or user-defined profile centred at $r_c=0$ is a physically valid "hub bump."
⟲ Parallel-Axis Theorem, Simplified by Symmetry
$$I(a,b)=I_c+M\,(a^2+b^2)$$

Because $\sigma(r)$ has no $\theta$-dependence, the centre of mass sits exactly at the disk's own centre $(0,0)$ — no first-moment integral needed. $I_c$, the polar moment about that centre, is computed once per profile; the Simulation tab's contour plot shows the resulting paraboloid centred exactly at the origin.

🖼 Geometry

A point on the disk at radius $r$ and angle $\theta$ has Cartesian position $x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta$. The rotation axis passes through the pivot $(a,b)$, perpendicular to the disk's plane (out of the page) — so a mass element's distance to the axis is $\rho^2=(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2$, exactly the Plate tab's setup, now expressed in polar coordinates.

$$x=r\cos\theta,\quad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad dA=r\,dr\,d\theta,\qquad dm=\sigma(r)\,dA=\sigma(r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta$$

The highlighted band is a single shell: all mass between radius $r$ and $r+dr$, swept through the full angle $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$. Its total mass and its contribution to $I$ about the disk's own centre are found by integrating $d\theta$ around that one shell before ever touching $r$:

$$dm_{\text{shell}}=\sigma(r)\left[\int_0^{2\pi}r\,d\theta\right]dr=\sigma(r)\,2\pi r\,dr,\qquad dI_{c,\text{shell}}=r^2\,dm_{\text{shell}}=2\pi\sigma(r)\,r^3\,dr$$

The second equality — $dI_{c,\text{shell}}=r^2\,dm_{\text{shell}}$ — holds with no extra angular work because every point on a shell of radius $r$ is exactly distance $r$ from the disk's centre, so $r^2$ pulls straight out of the $\theta$-integral. This is the geometric reason the Disk tab's radial integral is so much simpler than the Plate tab's $(x,y)$ double integral: symmetry does the angular part for free, leaving one ordinary integral over $r\in[0,R]$.

💡 Off-centre pivot, still simple. Once $M$ and $I_c$ are known from the radial integrals above (about the disk's own centre), moving to any pivot $(a,b)$ is pure algebra via $I(a,b)=I_c+M(a^2+b^2)$ — derived formally in the next step — with no need to redo the shell integral for each new pivot.
∂ Separating the Angular Integral
Expand the pivot-shifted squared distance
$$I(a,b)=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^R\bigl[(r\cos\theta-a)^2+(r\sin\theta-b)^2\bigr]\sigma(r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta$$

Expanding: $(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2-2ar\cos\theta-2br\sin\theta+a^2+b^2$ — identical to the Ring tab's expansion.

Integrate over $\theta$ first — the cross terms vanish
$$\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,d\theta=0,\qquad\int_0^{2\pi}\sin\theta\,d\theta=0,\qquad\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta=2\pi$$

Every $\cos\theta$/$\sin\theta$ term integrates to zero over a full turn regardless of the radial profile — the same reasoning used on the Ring tab, now applied down to $r=0$.

What survives — mass and centroidal moment as concentric-shell sums
$$M=2\pi\int_0^R\sigma(r)\,r\,dr,\qquad I_c=2\pi\int_0^R\sigma(r)\,r^3\,dr$$

Each shell of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ contributes mass $dm=\sigma(r)\,2\pi r\,dr$ and, since every point of a thin ring sits the same distance $r$ from the centre, contributes moment of inertia $r^2\,dm=2\pi\sigma(r)\,r^3\,dr$ directly — no further angular work needed once a shell is treated as a ring.

Re-attach the pivot terms
$$I(a,b)=I_c+(a^2+b^2)\underbrace{\iint\sigma\,dA}_{M}=I_c+M(a^2+b^2)$$

This app computes $M,I_c$ once per density profile via the two radial integrals above; $I(a,b)$ at any pivot is then pure algebra — the same $R_1\to0$ specialization of the Ring tab's $M,I_c$ shortcut.

Σ→∭ From Disk to Solid Sphere — Shells of Volume

A solid sphere of radius $R$ carries a volume mass density $\rho(r)$ (kg/m³) depending only on the radial distance $r\in[0,R]$ from the centre. Build it the way the Disk tab built a disk from concentric rings: slice the sphere into $N$ concentric spherical shells of radius $r_i$ and thickness $\Delta r$. A thin shell of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ has volume equal to its surface area times thickness:

$$dV=4\pi r^2\,dr\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad dm=\rho(r)\,4\pi r^2\,dr,\qquad M=\int_0^R\rho(r)\,4\pi r^2\,dr$$

Unlike the Disk tab's flat rings, a spherical shell is not all at one distance from a rotation axis through the centre — different points on the same shell sit at different perpendicular distances depending on their polar angle. Working that out is this tab's key extra step (done in full on the Derivation tab); the payoff is a clean result:

$$dI_{c,\text{shell}}=\tfrac23\,r^2\,dm,\qquad I_c=\frac{8\pi}{3}\int_0^R\rho(r)\,r^4\,dr$$

The rotation axis is a line through the pivot $(a,b)$ (measured in the plane perpendicular to the axis, e.g. $x$ and $z$ if the axis runs along world $y$) — the sphere spins about that line like a planet or a flywheel-ball.

💡 Isotropy is the sphere's superpower. Because $\rho$ depends only on $r$, $I_c$ about any diameter through the centre is the same number — there's no "which axis" question the way the Plate tab had. That's what makes the parallel-axis step below apply immediately, with $d^2=a^2+b^2$ regardless of which direction the axis points.
⬡ Seven Density Models, One Radial Integral

Every profile is a function of $r\in[0,R]$ only — same menu as every other tab, now describing a volume density:

  • Uniform: $\rho_0$ — the textbook solid ball.
  • Linear: $\rho_0[1+\alpha\,r/R]$ — denser toward the surface (or core).
  • Quadratic: $\rho_0[1+\alpha\,(r/R)^2]$ — mass concentrated near the surface.
  • Exponential: $\rho_0\,e^{\beta r/R}$ — rapid radial taper.
  • Gaussian: $\rho_0\,e^{-(r-r_c)^2/2\sigma^2}$ — a dense shell at radius $r_c$ (e.g. a planetary core-mantle boundary).
  • Piecewise: $\rho_1$ (core) then $\rho_2$ (mantle) at a breakpoint radius.
  • User-defined: any $\rho(r)$ typed in, evaluated numerically.
⚠️ Density must stay non-negative on $[0,R]$ for the model to represent physical mass. $r=0$ is included — a Gaussian centred at $r_c=0$ is a valid "dense-core" profile.
⟲ Parallel-Axis Theorem, Simplified by Isotropy
$$I(a,b)=I_c+M\,(a^2+b^2)$$

The centre of mass sits exactly at the sphere's centre $(0,0,0)$ by radial symmetry, and $I_c$ is the same for every diameter — so this is the direct 3D analogue of the Disk tab's formula, with $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ now the perpendicular distance from the centre to a rotation axis running parallel to the third coordinate.

🖼 Geometry

A point on a shell of radius $r$ has spherical coordinates $(r,\theta,\varphi)$, with $\theta$ measured from the rotation axis. Its perpendicular distance to the axis is $r\sin\theta$ — not $r$ itself, unlike the flat Disk/Ring case where every point of a ring at radius $r$ is automatically at distance $r$ from a perpendicular axis through the centre.

$$dV=r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi,\qquad dm=\rho(r)\,dV,\qquad \text{perp. distance to axis}=r\sin\theta$$
∂ Moment of Inertia of a Thin Shell About a Diameter

Because $\rho$ depends only on $r$, a shell of radius $r$ has mass spread uniformly over its surface — so the fraction of the shell's mass at polar angle $\theta$ is proportional to that ring's share of the total solid angle:

Mass of the shell, and the fraction at angle θ
$$dm_{\text{shell}}=\rho(r)\,4\pi r^2\,dr,\qquad dm(\theta,\varphi)=dm_{\text{shell}}\cdot\frac{\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi}{4\pi}$$
Integrate (distance)² over the whole shell
$$I_{\text{shell}}=\int(r\sin\theta)^2\,dm(\theta,\varphi)=\frac{dm_{\text{shell}}\,r^2}{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!d\varphi\int_0^{\pi}\sin^3\theta\,d\theta$$
Evaluate the two elementary integrals
$$\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi=2\pi,\qquad \int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta=\int_0^\pi\sin\theta(1-\cos^2\theta)\,d\theta=\Bigl[-\cos\theta+\tfrac{\cos^3\theta}3\Bigr]_0^\pi=\frac43$$

The $\sin^3\theta$ integral is the standard reduction $\sin^3\theta=\sin\theta(1-\cos^2\theta)$, substituted with $u=\cos\theta$.

Combine
$$I_{\text{shell}}=\frac{dm_{\text{shell}}\,r^2}{4\pi}\cdot2\pi\cdot\frac43=\frac23\,r^2\,dm_{\text{shell}}$$

This is the origin of the famous $\tfrac23MR^2$ thin-shell result and the $\tfrac23$ factor used throughout this tab. It applies to every shell regardless of $\rho(r)$'s shape, since the derivation never assumed uniformity in $r$ — only azimuthal/polar uniformity of each individual shell, which radial-only density always guarantees.

∂ Stacking Shells Into the Solid Sphere
Mass and centroidal moment, as radial integrals
$$M=\int_0^R\rho(r)\,4\pi r^2\,dr,\qquad I_c=\int_0^R\frac23r^2\,dm_{\text{shell}}=\int_0^R\frac23r^2\bigl[\rho(r)4\pi r^2\,dr\bigr]=\frac{8\pi}{3}\int_0^R\rho(r)\,r^4\,dr$$

This app computes $M,I_c$ once per density profile via these two radial integrals — the direct 3D analogue of the Rod's $M,I_0$ and the Disk's $M,I_c$ shortcuts.

Re-attach the pivot terms (parallel-axis, 3D form)
$$I(a,b)=I_c+M(a^2+b^2)$$

Valid for a rotation axis running parallel to the third coordinate through $(a,b)$, offset from the sphere's centre — isotropy guarantees $I_c$ is the same no matter which diameter direction that axis is parallel to.

$$I_c=\frac25MR^2$$

Σ→⬤ From a Rod to a Solid Cylinder — Adding Thickness

The Rod tab treats mass as smeared along an infinitely thin line. Give that line a radius $R$ — thicken it into a solid right-circular cylinder of length $L$ — and every axial slice, instead of being a point mass $dm=\lambda(x)\,dx$, becomes a thin disk of radius $R$ and mass $dm=\lambda(x)\,dx$ still, but now spread over a circular face rather than concentrated at a point.

The rotation axis stays transverse — perpendicular to the cylinder's long axis, through a pivot at $x=a$ along the length — exactly the Rod tab's "clock hand" / tumbling-baton configuration, just with a fattened cross-section.

$$dm=\lambda(x)\,dx,\qquad dI = \Bigl[(x-a)^2+\frac{R^2}{4}\Bigr]dm,\qquad I(a)=\int_0^L(x-a)^2\lambda(x)\,dx+\frac{R^2}{4}\int_0^L\lambda(x)\,dx$$

The extra $\tfrac{R^2}{4}dm$ term is the moment of inertia of a thin disk about one of its own diameters — an intrinsic shape property, independent of where that disk sits along $x$. It adds a single constant, $\tfrac{R^2}{4}M$, on top of everything the Rod tab already computes.

💡 Volume from thickness. As $R\to0$ this collapses back to the pure Rod result — the cylinder "is" the rod, thickened. As $R$ grows, mass moves away from the central axis, and the disk-diameter term $\tfrac14MR^2$ grows with it, exactly the way stacking a Riemann sum of thin disks (rather than points) builds up a genuine 3D volume from a 1D line.

⬡ Seven Density Models — Same as the Rod

$\lambda(x)$ (kg/m) uses the identical model menu as the Rod tab, now describing mass per unit length along a cylinder of radius $R$:

  • Uniform: $\lambda_0$.
  • Linear: $\lambda_0[1+\alpha x/L]$.
  • Quadratic: $\lambda_0[1+\alpha(x/L)^2]$.
  • Exponential: $\lambda_0e^{\beta x/L}$.
  • Gaussian: $\lambda_0e^{-(x-x_c)^2/2\sigma^2}$ — a dense collar/weld band.
  • Piecewise: $\lambda_1$ then $\lambda_2$ — a stepped shaft.
  • User-defined: any $\lambda(x)$, evaluated numerically.
⚠️ Cross-sectional radius $R$ is held constant along the whole length (a true right-circular cylinder, not a cone) — only $\lambda(x)$, i.e. how densely that constant cross-section is packed, varies with $x$.
⟲ Parallel-Axis Theorem, With the Disk Correction
$$I(a)=I_{cm}+M(a-x_{cm})^2,\qquad I_{cm}=I_{cm,\text{rod}}+\frac{R^2}{4}M$$

Since $\tfrac{R^2}{4}M$ doesn't depend on $a$, it shifts the whole Rod-tab parabola $I_{\text{rod}}(a)$ upward by a constant — the minimum still sits at $x_{cm}=S_x/M$ (the disk correction doesn't move the centre of mass), it just raises the floor.

◈ Bonus: Spinning About Its Own Axis

If instead the cylinder spins like a flywheel about its own central long axis (not the transverse pivot above), only the radial mass distribution matters. For a uniform cylinder, built from concentric cylindrical shells of radius $r$, thickness $dr$, length $L$: $dm=\rho_V(r)\,2\pi rL\,dr$, giving the classic result

$$I_{\text{axis}}=\frac12MR^2\quad\text{(uniform)}$$

This app's interactive controls model the transverse-axis case above (matching the Rod tab's pivot presets); the concentric-shell 3D toggle in the Simulation/3D tabs is a visual illustration of this same shell-building idea used throughout the Disk and Sphere tabs.

🖼 Geometry

The disk at position $x$, thickness $dx$, has mass $dm=\lambda(x)\,dx$ spread over its circular face of radius $R$. Its own moment of inertia about a diameter through its centre — a standard thin-disk result — is $\tfrac14\,dm\,R^2$; the parallel-axis theorem then carries that disk's contribution out to the pivot at $a$.

∂ Building $I(a)$ From Disk Slices
Moment of inertia of one thin disk, about its own diameter
$$dI_{\text{disk, own diameter}}=\frac14\,r_\perp^2\Big|_{r_\perp=R\text{-avg}}dm=\frac{R^2}{4}\,dm$$

(Standard thin-disk-about-a-diameter result — the disk's own $\tfrac14MR^2$, derived the same way the Disk tab derives $\tfrac12MR^2$ about the perpendicular axis, but for an axis lying in the disk's plane instead of through it.)

Shift each disk out to the pivot at $a$ (parallel-axis theorem)
$$dI(a) = dI_{\text{own diameter}} + (x-a)^2\,dm = \Bigl[\frac{R^2}{4}+(x-a)^2\Bigr]\lambda(x)\,dx$$
Integrate — the $R^2/4$ term factors straight out
$$I(a)=\int_0^L(x-a)^2\lambda(x)\,dx+\frac{R^2}{4}\int_0^L\lambda(x)\,dx = I_{\text{rod}}(a)+\frac{R^2}{4}M$$

Because $R$ is constant along the whole cylinder, $\tfrac{R^2}{4}$ pulls outside the integral entirely — no new machinery is needed beyond the Rod tab's $M,S_x,I_0$.

Centre of mass and $I_{cm}$
$$x_{cm}=\frac{S_x}{M}\ \text{(unchanged from Rod)},\qquad I_{cm}=\Bigl(I_0-\frac{S_x^2}{M}\Bigr)+\frac{R^2}{4}M$$
📍 Worked Special Case — Uniform Solid Cylinder

Set $\lambda(x)=\lambda_0=M/L$ constant. From the Rod tab: $I_{cm,\text{rod}}=\tfrac1{12}ML^2$. Adding the disk term:

$$I_{cm}=\frac1{12}ML^2+\frac14MR^2$$

— the textbook solid-cylinder result for rotation about a diameter through the centre, reached here as $R\to$ finite of the same Rod machinery.

⚖ Cross-Check

Direct integration of $[(x-a)^2+R^2/4]\lambda(x)$ at a fixed $a$ must agree with $I_0-2aS_x+a^2M+\tfrac{R^2}4M$ computed once from $M,S_x,I_0$ — they're algebraically identical. The Simulation tab checks this at the current pivot; the $I(a)$ sweep checks it continuously.

◉ From a Continuous Sphere to Discrete Shells

The Sphere tab treats the nucleon density $\rho(r)$ as a smooth function integrated continuously from $r=0$ to $r=R$. The real nuclear shell model instead organizes nucleons into a handful of discrete radial layers. This tab partitions $[0,R]$ into $N_{\text{shells}}$ concentric spherical bands and evaluates the same mass/inertia integrals one band at a time — the continuous Sphere result is recovered exactly as $N_{\text{shells}}\to\infty$; a single dense point-like nucleus is recovered as $N_{\text{shells}}\to1$.

$$dm=\rho(r)\,4\pi r^2dr,\qquad dI_{c}=\frac23r^2\,dm,\qquad r_i=\frac{iR}{N_{\text{shells}}},\ \ i=0,1,\dots,N_{\text{shells}}$$
$$\Delta M_i=4\pi\int_{r_{i-1}}^{r_i}\rho(r)\,r^2\,dr,\qquad \Delta I_i=\frac{8\pi}{3}\int_{r_{i-1}}^{r_i}\rho(r)\,r^4\,dr$$

Summing every band exactly reproduces the whole-nucleus totals — this is just the additivity of a definite integral over a partition of its domain:

$$M=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{shells}}}\Delta M_i,\qquad I_c=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{shells}}}\Delta I_i$$
💡 Why this matters. Real nuclei aren't uniform balls of nucleon soup — magic-number shells, a diffuse neutron/proton "skin," or a dense core each show up as a different $\rho(r)$ shape, and the shell-by-shell breakdown here lets you see how much mass (and how much moment of inertia) each radial layer actually carries.
⬡ Seven Density Models, Evaluated Shell-by-Shell
  • Uniform: $\rho_0$ — liquid-drop-model nucleus.
  • Linear: $\rho_0[1+\alpha r/R]$.
  • Quadratic: $\rho_0[1+\alpha(r/R)^2]$ — $\alpha<0$ mimics a dense core, $\alpha>0$ a diffuse skin/halo.
  • Exponential: $\rho_0e^{\beta r/R}$.
  • Gaussian: $\rho_0e^{-(r-r_c)^2/2\sigma^2}$ — a single dominant shell peak (e.g. a magic-number layer) at radius $r_c$.
  • Piecewise: $\rho_1$ (core) then $\rho_2$ (mantle) at a breakpoint radius.
  • User-defined: any $\rho(r)$ typed in, evaluated numerically per shell.
⚠️ Density must stay non-negative on $[0,R]$ for every shell's mass to be physical.
⟲ Derived Observables
$$\bar\rho_i=\frac{\Delta M_i}{\tfrac{4\pi}{3}(r_i^3-r_{i-1}^3)}\ \text{(volume-averaged shell density)},\qquad \langle r^2\rangle=\frac{3}{2}\frac{I_c}{M},\qquad r_{\text{rms}}=\sqrt{\langle r^2\rangle}$$

The $\langle r^2\rangle=\tfrac32 I_c/M$ relation follows directly from $I_c=\tfrac23\int r^2dm=\tfrac23M\langle r^2\rangle$ — the same shell-derivation used on the Sphere tab, just solved for $\langle r^2\rangle$.

🖼 Geometry — Partitioning the Radial Domain

Each shell $i$ is bounded by $r_{i-1}=\tfrac{(i-1)R}{N_{\text{shells}}}$ and $r_i=\tfrac{iR}{N_{\text{shells}}}$. Within it, the Sphere tab's exact shell-mass and shell-inertia integrands are simply integrated over the narrower range $[r_{i-1},r_i]$ instead of $[0,R]$.

∂ Shell-by-Shell Integration
Mass and moment of a single band
$$\Delta M_i=\int_{r_{i-1}}^{r_i}\rho(r)\,4\pi r^2\,dr,\qquad \Delta I_i=\int_{r_{i-1}}^{r_i}\rho(r)\,\frac{8\pi}{3}r^4\,dr$$

These are the exact same integrands derived in full on the Sphere tab (the $\tfrac23r^2$ factor per shell comes from averaging $(r\sin\theta)^2$ over a spherical shell's surface) — only the limits of integration change.

Additivity — summing the partition reproduces the whole
$$\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{shells}}}\Delta M_i=\int_0^R\rho(r)4\pi r^2dr=M,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{shells}}}\Delta I_i=I_c$$

This is a direct consequence of $\int_0^R=\sum_i\int_{r_{i-1}}^{r_i}$ — no approximation is involved in the summation itself (only the per-shell quadrature, if numerical, carries any error).

Closed forms for polynomial profiles, generalized to a shell's bounds

Define $N_k(r_1,r_2)=\int_{r_1}^{r_2}r^k\,dr=\dfrac{r_2^{k+1}-r_1^{k+1}}{k+1}$. For Uniform, Linear, and Quadratic profiles, $\Delta M_i,\Delta I_i$ reduce to the exact same coefficient formulas used on the Sphere tab — just with $N_k(0,R)$ replaced by $N_k(r_{i-1},r_i)$:

$$\text{Uniform: }\Delta M_i=4\pi\rho_0N_2(r_{i-1},r_i),\quad \Delta I_i=\tfrac{8\pi}3\rho_0N_4(r_{i-1},r_i)$$
Volume-averaged shell density and $\langle r^2\rangle$
$$\bar\rho_i=\frac{\Delta M_i}{V_i},\quad V_i=\frac{4\pi}{3}(r_i^3-r_{i-1}^3),\qquad \langle r^2\rangle=\frac{3}{2}\frac{I_c}{M}$$
⚖ Cross-Check: Partitioned Sum vs. Whole-Domain Integral

Summing $\Delta M_i$ across all shells (each evaluated numerically or in closed form) must equal a single high-resolution Simpson pass over the entire $[0,R]$ domain — they're the same integral, computed two different ways. The Simulation tab's comparison row checks exactly this.

◐ From a Sphere to a Deformed (Prolate/Oblate) Spheroid

Real nuclei are rarely perfect spheres. Many — especially those away from closed shells — are permanently deformed into an axially-symmetric ellipsoid of revolution (a spheroid): stretched along one axis into a prolate "American-football" shape, or flattened into an oblate "M&M" shape. This tab takes the Sphere/Nucleus tabs' radial density $\rho(u)$ and re-derives $M$ and the moment of inertia on a spheroidal geometry with two independent semi-axes instead of one radius.

$$\text{equatorial semi-axis } a,\qquad \text{polar (symmetry) semi-axis } c,\qquad u=\sqrt{\frac{x^2+y^2}{a^2}+\frac{z^2}{c^2}}\in[0,1]$$

$u$ is the natural "reduced radius" for a spheroid: surfaces of constant $u$ are similar spheroids, and $u=1$ is the nuclear surface itself. Exactly as the Sphere tab lets $\rho$ depend on $r/R$, this tab lets $\rho$ depend on $u$ only — density is constant on each similar spheroidal shell.

💡 Volume-conserving deformation. A single parameter $\beta$ (the quadrupole deformation) sets both semi-axes from one equivalent-sphere radius $R_0$ (the radius the nucleus would have if it were spherical with the same volume): $$a=R_0\Bigl(1-\frac\beta3\Bigr),\qquad c=R_0\Bigl(1+\frac{2\beta}3\Bigr)$$.

This is the standard first-order nuclear quadrupole parametrization — volume is conserved to $O(\beta^2)$, so $R_0$ (not $a$ or $c$ individually) is what you dial in as "the size." $\beta>0$ is prolate ($c>a$), $\beta<0$ is oblate ($c

Identical menu to the Sphere/Nucleus tabs, now a function of $u\in[0,1]$ instead of $r/R$:

  • Uniform: $\rho_0$ — the liquid-drop deformed nucleus.
  • Linear: $\rho_0[1+\alpha u]$.
  • Quadratic: $\rho_0[1+\alpha u^2]$ — core-dense or skin-heavy.
  • Exponential: $\rho_0e^{\beta_{\text{rate}} u}$.
  • Gaussian: $\rho_0e^{-(u-u_c)^2/2\sigma^2}$ — a dense shell at reduced radius $u_c$.
  • Piecewise: $\rho_1$ (core) then $\rho_2$ (mantle) at breakpoint $u=f$.
  • User-defined: any $\rho(u)$ typed in, evaluated numerically.

⟲ One Shape Integral, Two Geometric Prefactors

Because the spheroid is axially symmetric, there are exactly two distinct moments of inertia about the centre: about the symmetry (figure) axis $z$, and about any transverse axis $x$ or $y$ through the centre (both transverse axes are equivalent by symmetry).

$$M=4\pi a^2c\,N_2,\qquad I_z=\frac{8\pi}{3}a^4c\,N_4,\qquad I_x=\frac{4\pi}{3}a^2c(a^2+c^2)\,N_4,\qquad N_k=\int_0^1\rho(u)\,u^k\,du$$

Both moments of inertia are built from the same shape integral $N_4$ — only the geometric prefactor out front differs between the symmetry axis and the transverse axis. Setting $a=c=R$ collapses both formulas to the Sphere tab's $I_c=\tfrac{8\pi}{3}\rho_0\int r^4dr$ (with $N_4\to R^{-5}\!\int r^4dr$ after the obvious rescaling) and $I_z=I_x=\tfrac25MR^2$, confirming this tab is the genuine two-axis generalization of the Sphere tab, not a different model.

💡 Prolate spins "easier" about its long axis. For $\beta>0$ ($c>a$), mass sits farther from the $z$-axis than from a transverse axis passing through the "waist," so $I_x>I_z$ always — a football is easier to spin about its long axis than to tumble end-over-end. For $\beta<0$ (oblate, like a coin), the reverse holds once $cI_x$.
🖼 Geometry

Use scaled spherical coordinates $x=au\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ y=au\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ z=cu\cos\theta$. The Jacobian of this map is $a^2c\,u^2\sin\theta$ — a sphere's Jacobian $r^2\sin\theta$ stretched by the constant factor $a^2c$.

$$dV=a^2c\,u^2\sin\theta\,du\,d\theta\,d\varphi,\qquad dm=\rho(u)\,dV$$
∂ Mass and the Two Moments of Inertia
Mass — angular integral gives the usual $4\pi$
$$M=\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^\pi\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}\rho(u)\,a^2cu^2\sin\theta\,d\varphi\,d\theta\,du=4\pi a^2c\int_0^1\rho(u)u^2du=4\pi a^2c\,N_2$$
About the symmetry axis: $x^2+y^2=a^2u^2\sin^2\theta$
$$I_z=\int(x^2+y^2)\,dm=a^4c\int_0^1\rho u^4du\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi=a^4c\,N_4\cdot\frac43\cdot2\pi=\frac{8\pi}3a^4c\,N_4$$

The $\int\sin^3\theta\,d\theta=4/3$ step is identical to the Sphere tab's thin-shell derivation — deformation changes only which power of $a$ or $c$ multiplies the shape integral, not the angular reduction itself.

About a transverse axis: $y^2+z^2=a^2u^2\sin^2\theta\sin^2\varphi+c^2u^2\cos^2\theta$
$$\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\varphi\,d\varphi=\pi,\quad\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta=\frac43,\qquad\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi=2\pi,\quad\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\cos^2\theta\,d\theta=\frac23$$
$$I_x=a^2c\,N_4\Bigl[a^2\cdot\frac43\pi+c^2\cdot\frac43\pi\Bigr]=\frac{4\pi}3a^2c(a^2+c^2)\,N_4$$
Special case: sphere ($a=c=R$)

Substituting $a=c=R$ collapses both to $I_z=I_x=\tfrac{8\pi}3R^5N_4=\tfrac25MR^2$ (uniform case), exactly reproducing the Sphere tab — confirming the two formulas above are the genuine generalization, with no double-counting or missing angular factors.

📍 Worked Special Case — Uniform Spheroid

Set $\rho(u)=\rho_0$ constant, so $N_2=\rho_0/3$, $N_4=\rho_0/5$:

$$M=4\pi a^2c\cdot\frac{\rho_0}3=\frac43\pi\rho_0a^2c\quad(\text{the standard ellipsoid volume}\times\rho_0)$$
$$I_z=\frac{8\pi}{15}\rho_0a^4c=\frac25Ma^2,\qquad I_x=\frac{4\pi}{15}\rho_0a^2c(a^2+c^2)=\frac15M(a^2+c^2)$$

— the textbook uniform-ellipsoid-of-revolution results, reached here as the $\rho(u)=$const special case of the same shell machinery used for every other profile.

🔢 Fully Worked Numerical Example

Uniform prolate nucleus: $\rho_0=0.16$ amu/fm³, equivalent-sphere radius $R_0=6$ fm, deformation $\beta=0.3$.

Semi-axes
$$a=R_0(1-\beta/3)=6(0.9)=5.4\text{ fm},\qquad c=R_0(1+2\beta/3)=6(1.2)=7.2\text{ fm}$$
