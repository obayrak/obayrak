Nuclear Bound, Quasi-bound and Scattering States — Theory

A nucleon outside a closed core, two light nuclei in contact, an α particle trapped inside a heavy nucleus, and a neutron fired at lead all obey the same radial Schrödinger equation. Its solutions fall into three classes: bound states at discrete negative energies (single-particle levels, the deuteron, halo nuclei), quasi-bound states or resonances held for a finite time behind a centrifugal or Coulomb barrier (⁵He, ⁸Be, α and proton emitters), and scattering states at every positive energy (elastic scattering, the optical model). This chapter covers the physics of all three and explains the numerical methods used in this lab.

1. The Two-body Radial Equation

For a projectile (n, p, d or α) interacting with a core of mass number A through a central potential plus a spin–orbit term, the relative motion separates. The particle moves with the reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂) in the partial wave with orbital angular momentum l and total angular momentum j = l ± ½. Writing ψ = [ulj(r)/r] 𝒴ljm gives

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}u'' + \Big[V_N(r) + V_{so}(r)\,2\langle\mathbf l\cdot\mathbf s\rangle + V_C(r) + \frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2\mu r^2}\Big]u = Eu,\qquad u(0) = 0\]

with 2⟨l·s⟩ = l for j = l + ½ and −(l + 1) for j = l − ½. The square bracket is the effective potential. Its three ingredients set the character of every state in this lab:

Nuclear well: short-ranged, about 50 MeV deep, with the shape of the nuclear density. Centrifugal barrier: zero for s waves, so s-wave nucleons can spread far outside the core, which is what produces halos. Coulomb barrier: for charged projectiles the long-range 1/r repulsion makes a barrier several MeV high even for l = 0, and that barrier traps α particles and protons for times from nanoseconds to billions of years.

2. Units and Kinematics

The natural units are MeV and fm. Two constants carry all the scales:

\[\hbar c = 197.327\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm},\qquad \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 1.439965\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm},\qquad \frac{\hbar^2}{2m_u} = 20.9008\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm^2}\]

The lab uses atomic masses (in u, 1 u = 931.494 MeV). Reaction data are quoted in the laboratory frame (projectile energy Elab, target at rest), but the radial equation lives in the centre-of-mass frame:

\[E_{cm} = E_{lab}\,\frac{M_t}{M_p + M_t},\qquad k = \frac{\sqrt{2\mu E_{cm}}}{\hbar}\]

For 14 MeV neutrons on lead the difference is half a percent. For α + α it is a factor of two, so the 92 keV ⁸Be resonance is seen at Elab = 184 keV. Bound-state energies are always relative to the threshold of the two fragments, E = −S, where S is the separation energy (Sn, Sp, Sα).

A width Γ and a mean life τ are linked by τ = ħ/Γ, with ħ = 6.5821·10⁻²² MeV s. The half-life is T½ = τ ln 2. A width of 1 eV means τ = 0.66 fs; 10⁻²¹ MeV means τ = 0.66 s.

3. The Nucleon–Nucleon System

The deuteron is the only bound two-nucleon state: Jπ = 1⁺, binding energy B = 2.224575 MeV. It is mostly ³S₁ with a 4–6 % ³D₁ admixture produced by the tensor force; this lab keeps the S wave only. The binding is so weak that the wavefunction extends far beyond the range of the force:

\[\kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2\mu B}}{\hbar} = 0.2316\ \mathrm{fm^{-1}},\qquad \frac1\kappa = 4.32\ \mathrm{fm},\qquad u(r) \to A_S\,e^{-\kappa r}\]

About 70–80 % of the probability lies outside the potential, so the deuteron is the first halo nucleus. Its matter radius rd = ½√⟨r²⟩ = 1.97 fm and its asymptotic normalization AS = 0.885 fm−½ depend almost entirely on B. Any well (Yukawa, square, Hulthén) fitted to B gives close to the same values, an example of shape independence.

In the spin singlet (¹S₀) the same force does not bind. The scattering length is large and negative, as = −23.74 fm: the pole of the S-matrix sits on the negative imaginary k axis, a virtual state 66 keV from threshold. It shows up as the huge zero-energy n–p cross section (Problem 20.6), not as a level.

Yukawa and Hulthén forms. One-pion exchange gives the Yukawa shape V₀e−r/b/(r/b) with b = ħ/mπc = 1.41 fm. The Hulthén potential V₀/(er/b − 1) has the same tail and is solvable in closed form for l = 0, En = −(ħ²/2μb²)[(g − n²)/2n]² with g = 2μV₀b²/ħ². The Validation tab uses it as an exact check.

4. The Mean Field: Woods–Saxon

In a heavy nucleus each nucleon moves in the average field of the others. That field follows the density: flat inside, falling to zero over a surface about 2 fm thick. The Woods–Saxon form captures it with three numbers:

\[V_N(r) = -V_0\,f(r, R, a),\qquad f = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(r-R)/a}},\qquad R = r_0A^{1/3}\]

Typical values are r₀ ≈ 1.25 fm, a ≈ 0.65 fm and V₀ ≈ 51 ± 33(N − Z)/A MeV, deeper for protons than for neutrons in neutron-rich nuclei (the symmetry term). In the presets V₀ is fitted to one measured separation energy, and the other levels are then predictions.

A pure harmonic oscillator, ½μω²r², is the analytic limit of a deep well. Its levels E = ħω(2nr + l + 3/2) group into degenerate shells N = 2nr + l with (N + 1)(N + 2) states each (spin included), so the shells close at 2, 8, 20, 40, 70, 112, 168. Only the first three are real magic numbers. The flat bottom of the Woods–Saxon well lowers high-l states relative to low-l states within a shell, but it still cannot produce 28, 50, 82 or 126.

5. Spin–Orbit Coupling and Magic Numbers

The missing ingredient, found by Mayer and by Haxel, Jensen and Suess in 1949, is a strong spin–orbit force concentrated at the nuclear surface. In the Thomas form used here,

\[V_{so}(r)\,2\langle\mathbf l\cdot\mathbf s\rangle = V_{so}\Big(\frac{\hbar}{m_\pi c}\Big)^2\frac1r\Big|\frac{df_{so}}{dr}\Big|\,2\langle\mathbf l\cdot\mathbf s\rangle,\qquad \Big(\frac{\hbar}{m_\pi c}\Big)^2 \approx 2\ \mathrm{fm^2}\]

With Vso ≈ 6–7 MeV the j = l + ½ member of each high-l doublet drops by about l·(1–1.5) MeV and the j = l − ½ member rises. The splitting grows as 2l + 1, so the 1f₇/₂, 1g₉/₂, 1h₁₁/₂ and 1i₁₃/₂ levels are pushed down into the shell below, creating the gaps at 28, 50, 82 and 126.

6. The Coulomb Potential of the Core

For a charged projectile the core is modelled as a uniformly charged sphere of radius Rc = rcA1/3:

\[V_C(r) = \frac{Z_1Z_2e^2}{2R_c}\Big(3 - \frac{r^2}{R_c^2}\Big)\ \ (r < R_c),\qquad V_C(r) = \frac{Z_1Z_2e^2}{r}\ \ (r \ge R_c)\]

Inside, it adds a nearly constant repulsion of about 1.5Z₁Z₂e²/Rc, about 24 MeV for a proton in lead. This is why proton levels lie higher than neutron levels with the same quantum numbers. The Coulomb barrier for α + ²⁰⁸Pb peaks near 26 MeV, nearly three times the α-decay Q-value of ²¹²Po.

Outside the core the solutions are the regular and irregular Coulomb functions Fl(η, kr) and Gl(η, kr), with the Sommerfeld parameter

\[\eta = \frac{Z_1Z_2e^2\mu}{\hbar^2k} = Z_1Z_2\,\alpha\sqrt{\frac{\mu c^2}{2E}}\]

When η ≫ 1 the particle sees a classical barrier and tunnelling dominates; when η ≪ 1 the Coulomb field is a small correction. The lab computes F and G by Steed's continued-fraction method, and below the barrier by direct integration, so that the penetrability 1/(F² + G²) at the matching radius stays accurate when it is 10⁻³⁰.

7. Weak Binding: Halos and the ANC

Beyond the range of the nuclear force a bound state decays as

\[u(r) \to C\,W_{-\eta,\,l+\frac12}(2\kappa r)\ \ \text{(charged)},\qquad u(r) \to C\,\kappa r\,h_l^{(+)}(i\kappa r) \propto e^{-\kappa r}\ \ \text{(neutral)}\]

The constant C is the asymptotic normalization coefficient (ANC). It is model-independent in the sense that it is what peripheral reactions measure: transfer at sub-Coulomb energies and radiative capture at astrophysical energies probe only the tail. The lab extracts C by integrating inward from a large radius and matching to the Whittaker function.

For a weakly bound s- or p-wave neutron the tail dominates: ⟨r²⟩ grows as 1/κ² for l = 0 and as 1/κ for l = 1, and diverges at threshold. This is a neutron halo. ¹¹Be (Sn = 0.502 MeV, 2s₁/₂ ground state) has an rms radius of about 7 fm for the halo neutron, against about 2.3 fm for the ¹⁰Be core. The lab flags a halo when more than half of the probability lies outside R and the rms radius exceeds 1.3R.

Parity inversion in ¹¹Be. A simple shell model puts the 1p₁/₂ neutron below 2s₁/₂, as in ¹³C. In ¹¹Be the order is reversed: the 1/2⁺ ground state lies 0.32 MeV below the 1/2⁻ state. A single Woods–Saxon fitted to the s-state binds the p₁/₂ far too deeply. The inversion is explained by core excitation (coupling to the 2⁺ state of deformed ¹⁰Be), which is outside a one-channel model. The preset displays the failure deliberately.

8. Mirror Nuclei and the Thomas–Ehrman Shift

Charge symmetry says that ¹⁷O (n + ¹⁶O) and ¹⁷F (p + ¹⁶O) should have the same spectrum apart from the Coulomb energy. For the deeply bound 1d₅/₂ ground states the shift is uniform. The 2s₁/₂ excited state, however, lies at 0.871 MeV in ¹⁷O but only 0.495 MeV in ¹⁷F.

The reason is the extended s-wave tail. In ¹⁷F the 1/2⁺ state is bound by just 0.105 MeV, so the proton spends much of its time outside the core, where the Coulomb repulsion is weaker than inside. The state therefore gains less Coulomb energy than the compact d₅/₂ level. This Thomas–Ehrman shift (1951–52) is reproduced by the same Woods–Saxon well with the Coulomb term switched on, and ¹⁷F(1/2⁺) is a textbook proton halo.

9. Partial Waves with Spin ½

For a spin-½ projectile on a spin-0 target each partial wave splits into j = l ± ½, with S-matrix elements Sl± = ηl±e2iδl±. Outside the nuclear range the radial function is a combination of incoming and outgoing Coulomb waves H∓ = G ∓ iF, and the lab obtains S directly from two neighbouring grid points:

\[S_{lj} = \frac{u_2H_1^- - u_1H_2^-}{u_2H_1^+ - u_1H_2^+}\]

The scattering amplitude is a 2 × 2 matrix in spin space, M = f(θ) + g(θ) σ·n̂, with

\[f(\theta) = f_C(\theta) + \frac1{2ik}\sum_l e^{2i\sigma_l}\big[(l+1)(S_{l+} - 1) + l(S_{l-} - 1)\big]P_l(\cos\theta)\]
\[g(\theta) = \frac1{2k}\sum_l e^{2i\sigma_l}(S_{l+} - S_{l-})\,P_l^1(\cos\theta)\]

where σl = arg Γ(l + 1 + iη) are the Coulomb phases. The spin-flip amplitude g exists only because the spin–orbit force makes Sl+ ≠ Sl−.

For a spin-0 projectile (α, or a model spin-0 particle) there is a single Sl per l and g = 0.

10. Coulomb Scattering and the Mott Formula

The pure Coulomb amplitude is known exactly:

\[f_C(\theta) = -\frac{\eta}{2k\sin^2(\theta/2)}\exp\big[-i\eta\ln\sin^2(\theta/2) + 2i\sigma_0\big]\]

|fC|² is the Rutherford cross section, (Z₁Z₂e²/4E)²/sin⁴(θ/2). Because the Coulomb partial-wave series does not converge, the nuclear part is always added as the difference S − 1 multiplied by e2iσl, as in Section 9. Far below the barrier the nuclear S-matrix tends to 1 and the ratio σ/σR tends to 1; the Validation tab checks this for p + ²⁰⁸Pb at 5 MeV.

Identical particles. For two identical spin-0 bosons (α + α) the wavefunction must be symmetric, so only even l survive and the amplitudes at θ and π − θ add:

\[\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(\theta) + f(\pi - \theta)|^2\]

For pure Coulomb scattering this gives the Mott formula, with an interference term cos[η ln tan²(θ/2)] that oscillates around 90° in the c.m. frame. The lab uses the symmetrized sum with even partial waves doubled.

11. Low-Energy Scattering

For a short-range potential and k → 0 only the s wave matters, and its phase shift follows the effective-range expansion (Bethe 1949):

\[k\cot\delta_0 = -\frac1a + \frac12r_0k^2 + \dots,\qquad \sigma(E \to 0) = 4\pi a^2\]

Two numbers, the scattering length a and the effective range r₀, summarize the potential at low energy. The sign of a tells which side of threshold the nearest pole lies on: a > 0 for a bound state (the deuteron, at = 5.419 fm) and a < 0 for a virtual state (singlet n–p, as = −23.74 fm). For a weakly bound state a ≈ 1/κ plus a correction of order r₀.

Levinson's theorem. δl(0) − δl(∞) = Nlπ, where Nl is the number of bound states in the partial wave. (For l = 0 a zero-energy resonance adds π/2.) The lab plots δ/π, so the bound-state count can be read from the zero-energy end.

For charged particles the expansion is modified by the Coulomb field. The lab fits a and r₀ only for neutral projectiles and only when kminR < 0.3, where the expansion is reliable.

12. The Optical Model

When a nucleon hits a nucleus, part of the flux goes into inelastic channels and reactions. To describe the elastic channel alone, all of these are lumped into an imaginary potential that removes flux:

\[U(r) = -V f(r, R_V, a_V) - iW f(r, R_W, a_W) + 4iW_Da_D\frac{df(r, R_D, a_D)}{dr} + V_{so}\ \text{term} + V_C\]

The volume absorption W dominates at high energy. The surface term WD (a derivative Woods–Saxon peaked at the surface) dominates at low energy, where only the surface nucleons can be excited. With W ≠ 0 the S-matrix satisfies |S| < 1, and

\[\sigma_R = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_{lj}\frac{2j+1}{2s+1}\big(1 - |S_{lj}|^2\big)\]

is the reaction (absorption) cross section.

Becchetti–Greenlees. The global parameter set of Becchetti and Greenlees (1969) gives V, W, WD and Vso as linear functions of E, (N − Z)/A and Z/A1/3, fitted to elastic scattering of protons and neutrons with A > 40 and E < 50 MeV. The global OMP button recomputes the parameters at every energy of a scan. Modern sets (Koning–Delaroche 2003) are more accurate but more complex; BG is still within 5–10 % for total cross sections in its range.

13. Cross Sections and Polarization

For spin ½ on spin 0 the observables are

\[\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f|^2 + |g|^2,\qquad A_y(\theta) = \frac{2\,\mathrm{Im}(fg^*)}{|f|^2 + |g|^2}\]

Ay is the analyzing power: the left–right asymmetry seen when a polarized beam scatters. It is non-zero only with spin–orbit coupling and is the most direct measurement of Vso.

For neutrons (no Coulomb) the integrated cross sections are finite:

\[\sigma_{el} = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum\frac{2j+1}{2}|1 - S_{lj}|^2,\qquad \sigma_{tot} = \sigma_{el} + \sigma_R = \frac{4\pi}{k}\,\mathrm{Im}\,f(0)\]

The last equality is the optical theorem, a consequence of flux conservation, and the Validation tab checks it to 10⁻¹³. The measured neutron total cross section of lead at 14 MeV is about 5.4 b, close to the black-disc estimate 2π(R + 1/k)² (Problem 20.7). Its slow oscillations with energy are the Ramsauer effect: interference between the wave going through the nucleus and the wave going around it.

For charged projectiles σel and σtot diverge (the Coulomb force has infinite range), so the lab reports σR and the ratio of dσ/dΩ to Rutherford. A drop of that ratio below 1 at forward-backward angles marks the onset of nuclear absorption; the grazing partial wave lg, where |Sl| = ½, measures the strong-absorption radius.

14. Resonances

A quasi-bound state is a pole of Sl(E) in the lower half of the complex energy plane, at E = Er − iΓ/2. Near the pole the phase shift jumps by π through 90° and the partial cross section takes the Breit–Wigner form:

\[\delta_l(E) = \delta_{bg} + \arctan\frac{\Gamma/2}{E_r - E},\qquad \sigma_l = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\frac{2j+1}{2s+1}\frac{\Gamma^2}{(E - E_r)^2 + \Gamma^2/4}\]

The state is trapped behind the centrifugal barrier (neutrons with l > 0) or the Coulomb barrier (charged particles). Neutron s-waves have no barrier, so they show no sharp single-particle resonances, only virtual states. Light-nucleus examples in the lab:

15. Barrier Penetration and α Decay

Gamow, and independently Gurney and Condon, explained α decay in 1928 as tunnelling. In the WKB approximation the decay width is

\[\Gamma = \hbar\nu P,\qquad P = \exp\Big[-2\int_{r_2}^{r_3}\frac{\sqrt{2\mu(V_{\text{eff}} - Q)}}{\hbar}dr\Big],\qquad \nu = \Big[\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\frac{2\mu\,dr}{\hbar k(r)}\Big]^{-1}\]

where r₁ < r₂ < r₃ are the classical turning points, ν is the assault frequency and P the barrier transmission. The exponent depends on Q mostly through the Gamow factor 2πη ∝ Z₁Z₂/√Q, which gives the Geiger–Nuttall law:

\[\log_{10}T_{1/2} \approx \frac{aZ}{\sqrt Q} + b\]

A change of Q from 4 to 9 MeV changes T½ by more than 20 orders of magnitude, from 10¹⁷ s (²³²Th) to 10⁻⁷ s (²¹²Po). The Geiger–Nuttall sweep on the Quasi-bound tab reproduces this by moving the resonance energy with fixed potential geometry.

Cluster model. In the Buck–Merchant–Perez approach the α particle moves in a potential (here the cosh shape) whose radius is fixed by requiring a state with the Wildermuth quantum number G = 2n + l = 22 at the measured Q. With no free parameters this gives T½(²¹²Po) = 0.15 µs against 0.299 µs measured. The ratio, ≈ 0.5, is the α preformation probability: well-understood for this nucleus with two nucleons outside each closed shell.

16. Proton Emission

Beyond the proton drip line a proton can be unbound yet held by the Coulomb and centrifugal barriers for milliseconds. Proton emitters are very clean single-particle systems: the proton occupies a definite orbit (πh₁₁/₂ in ¹⁵¹Lu, l = 5), so the half-life depends sharply on l through the centrifugal barrier and is a direct measurement of the orbital.

¹⁵¹Lu (Qp = 1.241 MeV, T½ = 80.6 ms, 63 % proton branch) was the first ground-state proton emitter, discovered at GSI (Hofmann et al., 1982). With V₀ fitted to Q the lab gives a partial half-life of 37 ms against 127 ms measured; the ratio is the spectroscopic factor, about 0.3, in line with the partial occupation of the h₁₁/₂ orbit expected from BCS pairing.

Changing l at fixed Q shows the sensitivity: an s₁/₂ proton at the same energy would escape about 10⁵ times faster.

17. Cluster States: ⁸Be

⁸Be is unbound by 91.84 keV against breakup into two α particles, yet has a half-life of 8·10⁻¹⁷ s, about 10⁵ times longer than the time an α needs to cross the nucleus. It is a Coulomb-barrier resonance of two bosons. Its existence (and the 7.65 MeV Hoyle state of ¹²C built on it) makes the triple-α process in stars possible.

The Ali–Bodmer potential describes α–α scattering with two Gaussians: a short-range repulsion that mimics the Pauli exclusion of the nucleons in the two clusters and a longer-range attraction. Fitting the attraction to Er = 91.84 keV gives Γ ≈ 7.3 eV, close to the measured 5.57 eV. Since the α particles are identical spin-0 bosons, only even l are allowed and the angular distribution is symmetric about 90° (Section 10).

A width of a few eV at 92 keV means Γ/E ≈ 10⁻⁴, far too sharp to locate on a uniform energy grid. The lab finds it by locating the energy where a box state stabilizes, then refines it with the phase-shift derivative and the complex pole (Section 18).

20. Practice Problems

1. Find the decay constant κ and the size 1/κ of the deuteron.
μc² = mnmp/(mn + mp) = 469.46 MeV. κ = √(2 × 469.46 × 2.2246)/197.327 = 45.70/197.327 = 0.2316 fm⁻¹, so 1/κ = 4.32 fm, three times the range of the force.
2. How many neutrons fill the shells up to and including 1d₃/₂?
1s₁/₂ (2) + 1p₃/₂ (4) + 1p₁/₂ (2) + 1d₅/₂ (6) + 2s₁/₂ (2) + 1d₃/₂ (4) = 20, a magic number (⁴⁰Ca).
3. Estimate the Coulomb barrier for α + ²⁰⁸Pb, with R = 1.2(A₁1/3 + A₂1/3) fm.
R = 1.2(1.587 + 5.925) = 9.01 fm. VB = 2 × 82 × 1.44/9.01 = 26.2 MeV, about three times Q(²¹²Po) = 8.95 MeV, so the α must tunnel.
4. Compute η and the Gamow factor 2πη for the α in ²¹²Po decay.
μc² = (4 × 208/212) × 931.5 = 3656 MeV. η = Z₁Z₂α √(μc²/2E) = (164/137.036) √(3656/17.91) = 1.197 × 14.29 = 17.1. 2πη = 107, so the point-charge Gamow factor is e−2πη ≈ 10⁻⁴⁷. The finite nuclear radius removes the inner, thickest part of the barrier and reduces the exponent a great deal, but the transmission is still tiny: with an assault frequency near 10²¹ s⁻¹ the measured 0.3 µs half-life implies P ≈ 10⁻¹⁵.
5. A state has Γ = 10⁻²¹ MeV. What are its mean life and half-life?
τ = 6.582·10⁻²²/10⁻²¹ = 0.658 s; T½ = 0.658 × 0.693 = 0.456 s.
6. Use as = −23.74 fm and at = 5.42 fm to estimate the zero-energy n–p cross section.
Singlet 4πas² = 70.8 b, triplet 4πat² = 3.69 b. Spin weights ¼ and ¾: σ = 17.7 + 2.8 = 20.5 b, close to the measured free n–p cross section of 20.4 b. The singlet, with only a quarter of the weight, dominates because of the virtual state.
7. Estimate the total cross section for 14 MeV neutrons on ²⁰⁸Pb from the black-disc model.
R = 1.25 × 2081/3 = 7.4 fm. Ecm = 13.93 MeV, μc² ≈ 935 MeV, k = √(2 × 935 × 13.93)/197.3 = 0.818 fm⁻¹, 1/k = 1.22 fm. σtot = 2π(R + 1/k)² = 2π × 8.62² = 467 fm² = 4.7 b (measured ≈ 5.4 b; the optical model gives 5.38 b).
8. Why does the ¹⁷F 1/2⁺ state lie lower relative to its ¹⁷O mirror than the 5/2⁺ ground state does?
The s₁/₂ proton is barely bound and spends much of its time outside the core, where the Coulomb potential is weaker than the near-constant 3Ze²/2Rc inside. It therefore gains less Coulomb energy than the compact d₅/₂ state: the Thomas–Ehrman shift (0.871 → 0.495 MeV excitation).
9. The Geiger–Nuttall plot gives a slope of about 1.6 Z per unit of 1/√(Q/MeV) in log₁₀ T½. How much shorter is the half-life if Q rises from 8.0 to 9.0 MeV for Z = 84?
Δ(1/√Q) = 0.3536 − 0.3333 = 0.0202. Δlog₁₀T½ = 1.6 × 84 × 0.0202 ≈ 2.7, so about 500 times shorter.

22. Limits of the Models

Single channel. The core is inert: no core excitation (needed for the ¹¹Be parity inversion and for most spectroscopic factors), no coupled channels, no compound-nucleus resonances. The tensor force and the deuteron D state are absent here, so the quadrupole moment is zero (the Few-body nuclei tab treats the coupled ³S₁–³D₁ deuteron).

Local, energy-independent potentials. Real nuclear mean fields are non-local (or energy-dependent with an effective mass m* ≈ 0.7m deep inside), which spreads the calculated level scheme compared with experiment. The spin–orbit form is phenomenological. Pauli blocking between the projectile and the core is mimicked only by the repulsive core of Ali–Bodmer or by the choice of nodes.

Optical model. Becchetti–Greenlees is fitted for A > 40 and 10 < E < 50 MeV. Outside that range (light targets, low-energy neutrons) it gives the trend, not the detail; compound-elastic scattering, which matters below a few MeV, is not included. The deuteron and α optical potentials need their own parameter sets.

Decay rates. Calculated half-lives are single-particle values. The comparison with experiment yields a spectroscopic or preformation factor; it is not a free-parameter fit. Uncertainties of a factor of 2–3 come from the potential radius alone.

Many-body tabs. Hartree–Fock is spherical (no deformation, no time-odd terms, no Lipkin–Nogami or particle-number projection); pairing is constant-G BCS in a fixed window, which fails near the drip lines where the continuum matters. The Nilsson model has no self-consistency and no Strutinsky correction. The few-body calculations use central forces without three-nucleon terms (Minnesota) or a 1968 potential (Reid); the halo model is S-wave only, with a point core and no Pauli blocking. Hypernuclei: one Λ in a local well, no ΛN spin dependence.

Reactions. Coupled channels and DWBA ignore the projectile spin; DWBA is zero-range with fixed optical potentials (no finite-range or non-locality corrections), so absolute spectroscopic factors carry 20–30 % uncertainties. Fusion uses parabolic barriers and constant couplings. The statistical model is Weisskopf–Ewing (no angular momentum) with first-chance emission only. R-matrix is one channel with spins ignored; astrophysical S-factors are linear and non-resonant; the neutron resonances are a few s-wave levels; electron scattering is in the Born approximation.

Resonance poles and decay modes. Gamow states are computed for neutrons only (no complex Coulomb functions). The particle–rotor model keeps the 0⁺ and 2⁺ core states, an undeformed spin–orbit term and parity-dependent depths, and removes Pauli-forbidden states only by skipping levels. Cluster, α and two-proton half-lives are WKB estimates with a schematic barrier: their ratio to experiment, not their absolute value, carries the physics. The fission barrier is a pure liquid drop with a three-parameter shape family that cannot reach the saddle of light nuclei. The β rate function omits screening, recoil and the radiative and isospin corrections. The α and cluster data are approximate values from compilations.

Numerics. Resonances with Γ/E below about 10⁻⁹ are given by WKB only. Phase shifts neglect the nuclear potential beyond Rm. Box-stabilization can miss resonances wider than their own energy; the phase-shift view (fitPhase mode for ⁵He) is the reliable one there.

23. Self-consistent Mean Fields: Skyrme–Hartree–Fock

The Woods–Saxon well of the Bound states tab is put in by hand. In Hartree–Fock theory the nucleons generate it themselves: each moves in the average field of all the others, and the field is recomputed from the new orbitals until nothing changes. With a zero-range effective interaction (Skyrme 1959; Vautherin and Brink 1972) the energy is an integral of local densities,

\[E = \int\!\Big[\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\tau + \tfrac14 t_0\big((2+x_0)\rho^2 - (2x_0+1)\textstyle\sum_q\rho_q^2\big) + \tfrac1{24}t_3\rho^\alpha\big((2+x_3)\rho^2 - (2x_3+1)\sum_q\rho_q^2\big) + \mathcal H_{\text{eff}} + \mathcal H_{\text{fin}} + \tfrac12W_0\big(\mathbf J\cdot\nabla\rho + \textstyle\sum_q\mathbf J_q\cdot\nabla\rho_q\big)\Big]d^3r + E_C + E_{\text{pair}}\]

with the densities \(\rho_q = \sum_i v_i^2|\phi_i|^2\), \(\tau_q = \sum_i v_i^2|\nabla\phi_i|^2\) and the spin–orbit density \(\mathbf J_q\). Varying with respect to the orbitals gives Schrödinger-like equations with a position-dependent effective mass,

\[\Big[-\nabla\cdot\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_q^*(r)}\nabla + U_q(r) + \frac{W_q(r)}{r}\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf l\Big]\phi_i = e_i\phi_i,\qquad \frac{\hbar^2}{2m^*_q} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} + \tfrac18[t_1(2+x_1) + t_2(2+x_2)]\rho + \tfrac18[t_2(2x_2+1) - t_1(2x_1+1)]\rho_q\]

In spherical symmetry each \((l, j)\) block is a tridiagonal matrix on the radial grid for \(u = rR\); the lab finds its lowest eigenvalues by Sturm-sequence bisection and the vectors by inverse iteration, mixes old and new densities, and repeats (20–50 iterations). The protons feel the direct Coulomb potential of their own charge distribution and the Slater approximation to Coulomb exchange, \(-e^2(3\rho_p/\pi)^{1/3}\). The finite-difference error is \(O(h^2)\): the validation extrapolates two grids to \(h \to 0\).

The forces are fitted to binding energies and radii of a few doubly magic nuclei and to nuclear matter. The same parameters give infinite matter analytically, for example \(E/A = \frac{3\hbar^2}{10m}k_F^2 + \frac38t_0\rho + \frac1{16}t_3\rho^{\alpha+1} + \frac3{80}[3t_1 + (5+4x_2)t_2]\rho k_F^2\) for symmetric matter: saturation at \(\rho_0 \approx 0.16\ \text{fm}^{-3}\), \(E/A \approx -16\) MeV, and incompressibility \(K = 9\rho_0^2\,d^2(E/A)/d\rho^2 \approx 230\) MeV. The neutron-matter curve and the neutron skin \(r_n - r_p\) both depend on the density dependence of the symmetry energy, which PREX-II (²⁰⁸Pb) and CREX (⁴⁸Ca) probe with parity-violating electron scattering.

24. Pairing

Even–even nuclei all have \(0^+\) ground states, odd nuclei are less bound than the average of their neighbours, and the first excited states of even nuclei lie well above the single-particle spacing. A short-range attraction between nucleons in time-reversed orbits explains this. In the BCS approximation (Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer 1957; Bohr, Mottelson and Pines 1958) with a constant matrix element G,

\[1 = \frac G2\sum_k\frac{\Omega_k}{\sqrt{(e_k - \lambda)^2 + \Delta^2}},\qquad N = \sum_k 2\Omega_k v_k^2,\qquad v_k^2 = \frac12\Big(1 - \frac{e_k - \lambda}{\sqrt{(e_k-\lambda)^2 + \Delta^2}}\Big)\]

with \(\Omega_k = j_k + \tfrac12\) pairs per level. The occupation drops smoothly from 1 to 0 over about \(2\Delta\) around the Fermi energy λ. Below a critical G, or at a closed shell, the only solution is \(\Delta = 0\). In an odd nucleus one level is blocked by the unpaired nucleon; its quasiparticle energy \(\sqrt{(e-\lambda)^2 + \Delta^2} \ge \Delta\) is the cost of being odd. The three-point difference

\[\Delta^{(3)}(N) = \frac{(-1)^N}{2}\big[E(N+1) - 2E(N) + E(N-1)\big]\]

measures it from masses: \(\Delta^{(3)} \approx 12/\sqrt A\) MeV at odd N; at even N it is larger, because it also contains half the single-particle spacing at the Fermi level (Satuła, Dobaczewski and Nazarewicz 1998). For a single j shell the exact (seniority) ground-state energy is \(-G\frac n2(\Omega - \frac n2 + 1)\); BCS misses the last term, a fluctuation in particle number that matters for small systems.

25. Deformed Nuclei: the Nilsson Model

Between closed shells most nuclei are deformed: rotational bands \(E = \hbar^2I(I+1)/2\mathcal I\) and large quadrupole moments show it. Nilsson (1955) replaced the spherical well by an axially deformed oscillator. In coordinates stretched along the symmetry axis,

\[h = \hbar\omega_0(\varepsilon)\Big[N + \tfrac32 - \tfrac23\varepsilon\sqrt{\tfrac{4\pi}5}\,\rho^2Y_{20}\Big] - \kappa\hbar\mathring\omega\big[2\mathbf l\cdot\mathbf s + \mu(\mathbf l^2 - \langle\mathbf l^2\rangle_N)\big],\qquad \omega_0(\varepsilon) = \mathring\omega\Big(1 - \frac{\varepsilon^2}3 - \frac{2\varepsilon^3}{27}\Big)^{-1/3}\]

the deformation term has no matrix elements between shells, so each major shell N and each projection \(\Omega = \Lambda + \Sigma\) gives a small matrix (the lab diagonalises it in the basis \(|N l \Lambda \Sigma\rangle\)). With κ = 0 the eigenvalues are those of the anisotropic oscillator, \(\hbar\omega_0[N + \frac32 + \varepsilon(N - 3n_z)/3]\), a check of the matrix elements. As ε grows, each spherical \(j\) level splits into \(j + \frac12\) Kramers doublets: for prolate shapes low Ω moves down (orbits in the equatorial plane overlap less with the elongated core). Levels with the same \(\Omega^\pi\) repel, others cross. Where the Fermi level falls into a large gap of this diagram the nucleus deforms; the sum of the occupied levels has its minimum there (²⁰Ne, ²⁴Mg prolate; ¹⁶O, ⁴⁰Ca spherical). Quantitative deformation energies need the Strutinsky shell correction added to a liquid drop.

26. Λ Hypernuclei

A Λ hyperon (uds, isospin 0, mass 1115.7 MeV) inside a nucleus lives about 200 ps, long enough to have a level scheme. It is distinguishable from the nucleons, so it can occupy 1s, 1p, 1d … of its own well regardless of how the nucleons fill theirs. Spectroscopy with (π⁺, K⁺) and (e, e′K⁺) reactions finds the levels on a single line when plotted against \(A^{-2/3}\): a Woods–Saxon well with \(V_0 \approx 30\) MeV, about 60 % of the nucleon depth. The ΛN force is weaker than NN because a Λ cannot exchange a single pion. The Λ spin–orbit splitting is tiny (< 0.2 MeV in ¹³C_Λ). One well cannot fit both heavy hypernuclei and ⁵He_Λ (B_Λ = 3.12 MeV): this overbinding problem points to ΛN–ΣN conversion and three-body ΛNN forces, which also matter for the "hyperon puzzle" of neutron stars (Gal, Hungerford and Millener 2016).

27. Few-body Nuclei

27.1 The deuteron with a realistic force

Realistic NN potentials fit the phase shifts up to pion production. The Reid soft-core potential (1968) writes each component as Yukawa functions of \(x = 0.7\,r\) (fm): one-pion exchange \(-10.463\,e^{-x}/x\) at long range, shorter-range terms with \(e^{-2x}, e^{-4x}, e^{-6x}\) and a repulsive core. In the deuteron (\(J^\pi = 1^+\)) the tensor force couples \(^3S_1\) and \(^3D_1\):

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{m}u'' + V_Cu + \sqrt8V_Tw = Eu,\qquad -\frac{\hbar^2}{m}w'' + \Big[\frac{6\hbar^2}{mr^2} + V_C - 2V_T - 3V_{LS}\Big]w + \sqrt8V_Tu = Ew\]

The lab solves them as a 2 × 2 block-tridiagonal matrix by inverse iteration. Observables: \(Q = \frac{\sqrt2}{10}\int r^2uw\,dr - \frac1{20}\int r^2w^2dr\), \(\eta = A_D/A_S\), \(\mu_d = \mu_p + \mu_n - \frac32(\mu_p + \mu_n - \frac12)P_D\), and the body form factors \(F_C(q) = \int(u^2 + w^2)j_0(qr/2)\,dr\) and \(F_Q(q)\), multiplied by the nucleon charge form factor. The Reid force gives \(P_D = 6.5\) % and \(Q = 0.280\) fm², slightly below 0.2859 fm²; the difference, like the deficit in \(\mu_d\), is attributed to meson-exchange currents.

27.2 ³H, ³He and ⁴He with correlated Gaussians

For A = 3 and 4 the wavefunction is expanded in explicitly correlated Gaussians \(\exp(-\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf x)\) in the Jacobi coordinates times spin and isospin functions built from all intermediate couplings, and antisymmetrised by summing over the \(A!\) permutations. Matrix elements are analytic; the stochastic variational method chooses each new basis function from random candidates by the energy it gains (Varga and Suzuki 1995). With the central Minnesota force (fitted to the deuteron and to NN scattering) the lab reproduces the literature values 8.38 MeV for ³H and 29.94 MeV for ⁴He. The ³H–³He difference of 0.76 MeV is mostly Coulomb (about 0.68 MeV); the rest is charge-symmetry breaking (the Nolen–Schiffer anomaly).

27.3 The Tjon line

Two-nucleon forces that agree on the deuteron and on low-energy scattering still differ at short distance, and they give different \(B(^3\mathrm H)\) and \(B(^4\mathrm{He})\). The points fall on a line (Tjon 1975), so the four-body binding is largely fixed by the three-body one. No two-body force reaches the experimental point; three-nucleon forces (Fujita–Miyazawa two-pion exchange, chiral EFT) shift the calculation along the line to it.

27.4 Borromean halos

¹¹Li (S₂ₙ = 0.369 MeV) is bound, but neither ¹⁰Li nor the dineutron is: like the Borromean rings, the three pieces hold together only all at once. In a three-body model core + n + n the n–core force can be tuned up to the point where it would bind ¹⁰Li; just below, the three-body state already exists. Its wavefunction extends far beyond the core, giving the large matter radius found from interaction cross sections (Tanihata et al. 1985). For a short-range force at unitarity three identical bosons show the Efimov effect: an infinite tower of states with energies scaled by \(e^{2\pi/s_0} \approx 515\). Real halos are far from that limit, and ⁶He (with ⁵He unbound in a p wave) needs p-wave n–α forces that the lab's S-wave model replaces by an effective strength.

28. Numerical Methods of the Many-body Tabs

Hartree–Fock: radial grid \(h = 0.05\) fm, \(u(0) = u(R) = 0\), symmetric three-point stencil for \(-(B u')'\) with \(B\) at half-points, Sturm count for the eigenvalue index, three steps of inverse iteration, linear density mixing (0.45), convergence when the energy changes by less than 10⁻⁶ MeV and the density by less than 10⁻⁶ fm⁻³. Nilsson: Clebsch–Gordan coefficients from the Racah formula; each (N, Ω) block has at most N + 1 states. BCS: the gap equation and the particle-number condition by nested bisection. Correlated Gaussians: 2 × 2 or 3 × 3 matrices per permutation; the generalized eigenproblem is solved by Cholesky reduction; a candidate basis function is accepted by the eigenvalue it gives, found from the secular equation of the enlarged basis without rediagonalising. Deuteron: 3000-point grid to 30 fm, block Thomas algorithm.

29. Gamow States and the Poles of the S-matrix

For a neutron in a short-range well the S-matrix of each partial wave is \(S_l(k) = \mathcal F_l(-k)/\mathcal F_l(k)\) with the Jost function \(\mathcal F_l\). Its zeros give poles of \(S_l\) where the radial solution that is regular at the origin is purely outgoing outside the well,

\[u_l(r) \propto h_l^+(kr),\qquad u_l'(R)\,h_l^+(kR) - k\,u_l(R)\,h_l^{+\prime}(kR) = 0,\qquad E = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2\mu}\]

(Gamow 1928; Siegert 1939). Poles on the positive imaginary axis \(k = i\kappa\) are bound states. Poles in the lower half plane, \(k = k_r - ik_i\), are resonances with \(E = E_r - i\Gamma/2\); their wavefunctions grow like \(e^{k_ir}\) outside, the price of describing a state that decays in time as \(e^{-\Gamma t/2\hbar}\). On a complex-rotated contour \(r = R + te^{i\theta}\) with \(\tan\theta > k_i/k_r\) they decay, which is why complex scaling turns resonances into square-integrable states and the Berggren basis (bound + resonant + a complex contour of scattering states) makes them part of a complete set (Berggren 1968).

Making the well shallower moves a bound pole down the imaginary axis to \(k = 0\). With \(l = 0\) nothing holds it there: it continues onto the negative imaginary axis as a virtual state, which is not a state at all but enhances low-energy scattering (\(a \approx -1/\kappa\), as for ¹⁰Li and the ¹S₀ neutron–neutron system). With \(l > 0\) the centrifugal barrier keeps it near threshold as a narrow resonance whose width grows like \(E^{l+1/2}\), the penetrability of the barrier. The lab follows the pole by continuation: the bound part from the box solver refined by the pole condition, the rest by a complex secant search with Riccati–Hankel functions \(h_0^+ = e^{iz}\), \(h_{l+1}^+ = \frac{2l+1}z h_l^+ - h_{l-1}^+\).

30. Core Excitation: Particle–Rotor Coupled Channels

In ¹¹Be the 1/2⁺ intruder lies below the 1/2⁻ state expected from the shell model (parity inversion), and the halo neutron is not purely in an \(s_{1/2}\) orbit around a ¹⁰Be ground state. ¹⁰Be is deformed (β₂ ≈ 0.6–0.7) with a 2⁺ state at 3.368 MeV, so the neutron also sees an excited core. In the particle–rotor model the well is deformed along the core axis ê,

\[V(\mathbf r) = -V_0\,f\big(r - R_0[1 + \beta_2Y_{20}(\theta')]\big) = \sum_\lambda V_\lambda(r)\,P_\lambda(\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}),\qquad V_\lambda(r) = \frac{2\lambda+1}2\int_{-1}^{1}V(r, x)P_\lambda(x)\,dx\]

and the channels \(|((l\tfrac12)j, I)J^\pi\rangle\) with \(I = 0^+, 2^+\) couple through the matrix elements of \(P_\lambda(\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}) = \frac{4\pi}{2\lambda+1}\sum_\mu Y^*_{\lambda\mu}(\hat{\mathbf r})Y_{\lambda\mu}(\hat{\mathbf e})\) (the K = 0 rotor wavefunctions are spherical harmonics of the axis). The coupled radial equations are a block-tridiagonal matrix on the grid. The lab counts its eigenvalues below E from the inertia of the block LDLᵀ factorisation (Sylvester's law), finds the k-th level by bisection, so Pauli-forbidden deeper states can be skipped, and the vector by inverse iteration. The weight of the \((l j)\otimes 0^+\) channel is the spectroscopic factor measured in transfer and knockout reactions. With β₂ = 0.67 and depths fitted to each state, the ¹¹Be ground state is about 85 % \(s_{1/2}\otimes 0^+\) and 13 % \(d_{5/2}\otimes 2^+\), close to Nunes, Thompson and Johnson (1996).

31. Mirror Nuclei and Coulomb Displacement Energies

Exchanging all protons and neutrons maps a nucleus onto its mirror. The nuclear force is nearly charge-symmetric, so the binding energies differ mainly through the Coulomb energy: \(\Delta E_C = B(Z_<) - B(Z_>) = Q_{EC} + (m_n - m_{\mathrm H})c^2\), with \(m_n - m_{\mathrm H} = 0.782\) MeV. A Skyrme–Hartree–Fock calculation with Coulomb direct and exchange terms gives \(\Delta E_C\) about 5–10 % below experiment for every pair: the Nolen–Schiffer anomaly (1969). Part of it comes from charge-symmetry breaking of the NN force (the nn scattering length −18.9 fm against −17.3 fm for pp after Coulomb correction), part from Coulomb effects beyond the mean field. The same displacement energies fix the energies of isobaric analogue states, and the analogue of the parent ground state is the final state of the superallowed β decays of § 33.

32. Charged-particle Radioactivity: α, Clusters and Two Protons

WKB widths. With a Woods–Saxon well inside and the Coulomb potential of a uniformly charged sphere outside, the semiclassical width is

\[\Gamma = \frac{\hbar^2}{4\mu}\Big[\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\frac{dr}{k(r)}\Big]^{-1}\exp\Big(-2\int_{r_2}^{r_3}\kappa(r)\,dr\Big),\qquad \hbar\kappa = \sqrt{2\mu\Big(V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2(l+\frac12)^2}{2\mu r^2} - Q\Big)}\]

(Gurvitz and Kalbermann 1987). With a touching-sphere radius \(R = 1.2(A_d^{1/3} + A_c^{1/3})\) fm and a sharp surface the computed α half-lives are about ten times shorter than measured: the cluster is present in the parent only with a preformation probability \(S \approx 0.1\). For heavier clusters (¹⁴C from ²²³Ra, Rose and Jones 1984; ²⁴Ne, ²⁸Mg, ³²Si …) S falls by roughly a constant factor per nucleon of the cluster, \(S_c \approx S_\alpha^{(A_c-1)/3}\) (Blendowske and Walliser 1988).

Universal decay law. R-matrix theory with a channel radius \(R = r_0(A_c^{1/3} + A_d^{1/3})\) gives \(\log_{10}T_{1/2} = a\chi' + b\rho' + c\), \(\chi' = Z_cZ_d\sqrt{\mathcal A/Q}\), \(\rho' = \sqrt{\mathcal A Z_cZ_d(A_c^{1/3} + A_d^{1/3})}\), \(\mathcal A = A_cA_d/(A_c + A_d)\), with one set of coefficients for α and cluster decays (Qi et al. 2009). The Geiger–Nuttall law is its α-decay special case.

Two-proton radioactivity. Beyond the proton drip line pairing can make a nucleus stable against one-proton emission but not against two (Goldansky 1960). Then both protons must tunnel together. Two simple limits bracket the rate: a ²He "diproton" cluster carrying the whole \(Q_{2p}\) (too fast) and two independent protons sharing \(Q_{2p}\), \(\Gamma \approx \hbar\omega\int_0^1 P(\varepsilon Q)P((1-\varepsilon)Q)\,d\varepsilon\) (too slow; Galitsky and Cheltsov 1964). The product of penetrabilities favours equal energies. ⁴⁵Fe was the first ground-state two-proton emitter (Pfützner et al.; Giovinazzo et al. 2002); time projection chambers now image the proton tracks and their correlations, which three-body theory reproduces (Grigorenko et al.).

33. Fission, Superallowed β Decay and γ Decay

Liquid-drop fission barrier. Deforming a charged drop at constant volume increases its surface energy and decreases its Coulomb energy. With \(R(\theta) = R_0\lambda(1 + \alpha_2P_2 + \alpha_4P_4 + \alpha_6P_6)\),

\[E_{\text{def}} = E_s^{(0)}(B_s - 1) + E_C^{(0)}(B_c - 1),\qquad B_s = 1 + \tfrac25\alpha_2^2 + \dots,\quad B_c = 1 - \tfrac15\alpha_2^2 + \dots,\qquad x = \frac{E_C^{(0)}}{2E_s^{(0)}}\]

so the sphere is stable for fissility \(x < 1\), and the barrier is the saddle of \(E_{\text{def}}\) (Bohr and Wheeler 1939; near \(x = 1\), \(B_f \approx 0.726(1-x)^3E_s^{(0)}\)). The lab computes \(B_c\) from the surface double integral \(E_C = -\frac{\rho^2}{12}\oint\oint(d\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf d)(d\mathbf S'\cdot\mathbf d)/d\) (Davies and Sierk 1975), normalised to the sphere, and follows the minimum over α₄ and α₆ at each α₂. Actinide barriers (≈ 6 MeV) differ from the liquid drop by shell corrections, which also make them double-humped and create fission isomers (Strutinsky 1967).

Superallowed β decay. For \(0^+ \to 0^+\) transitions between isospin analogues only the vector current contributes, with the matrix element \(\sqrt2\). If the vector current is conserved, every corrected \(\mathcal Ft = ft(1+\delta_R')(1+\delta_{NS}-\delta_C)\) is the same; the world average is 3072.24(57) s. With the electroweak radiative correction \(\Delta_R^V\), \(|V_{ud}|^2 = K/(2G_F^2(1+\Delta_R^V)\mathcal Ft) = 0.97373(31)\), the most precise CKM element (Hardy and Towner 2020). The statistical rate function \(f = \int_1^{W_0}F(Z,W)\,pW(W_0-W)^2\,dW\) needs the Fermi function with finite-size corrections; the lab's version reaches the tabulated ft to 0.3 % for light emitters. With \(|V_{us}| = 0.2243\) the first row sums to 0.9985, a 2–3σ deficit that is an open problem.

γ decay. Weisskopf's single-particle estimates \(\lambda(E\lambda) \propto A^{2\lambda/3}E_\gamma^{2\lambda+1}\) and \(\lambda(M\lambda) \propto A^{2(\lambda-1)/3}E_\gamma^{2\lambda+1}\) set the scale. Measured rates divided by them are reduced transition probabilities in Weisskopf units: tens to hundreds for E2 transitions in deformed nuclei (collective rotation), far below 1 for transitions hindered by selection rules (K isomers).

34. Inelastic Scattering: Coupled Channels

A projectile scattered by a deformed nucleus can leave it in a rotational state. Writing the optical potential with a deformed radius \(R(\theta') = R_0[1 + \beta_2Y_{20}(\theta') + \beta_4Y_{40}(\theta')]\) and expanding in Legendre polynomials of the angle between the projectile direction and the nuclear axis gives the coupled equations

\[\Big[\frac{d^2}{dr^2} - \frac{l(l+1)}{r^2} + k_c^2\Big]u_c(r) = \frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\sum_{c'}\langle c|V(r,\theta')|c'\rangle\,u_{c'}(r),\qquad |c\rangle = |(l, I)J\rangle,\quad k_c^2 = \frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}(E - \varepsilon_I)\]

with \(\langle c|V|c'\rangle = \sum_\lambda V_\lambda(r)\langle(lI)J|P_\lambda(\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf e})|(l'I')J\rangle\) (Tamura 1965). The lab integrates N independent regular solutions with a matrix Numerov method and matches to Coulomb functions in every channel: with \(Q = U(R_2)U(R_1)^{-1}\) and \(\hat H^\pm = H^\pm/\sqrt k\), \(S = (\hat H^+_2 - Q\hat H^+_1)^{-1}(\hat H^-_2 - Q\hat H^-_1)\). For a real potential S is unitary and symmetric. The inelastic cross section grows as \(\beta_2^2\) in first order (DWBA); its size measures the deformation length \(\delta_2 = \beta_2R\), and departures from \(\beta_2^2\) are multistep effects.

35. Transfer Reactions and Spectroscopic Factors

In a (d,p) reaction the neutron of the deuteron is captured into an orbit of the target. In the distorted-wave Born approximation with zero range (the deuteron's n–p interaction acts only at zero separation),

\[T_m = D_0\int\chi_p^{(-)*}(\mathbf k_p,\mathbf r)\,\phi^*_{lm}(\mathbf r)\,\chi_d^{(+)}(\mathbf k_d,\mathbf r)\,d^3r,\qquad \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = S\,\frac{2J_f+1}{2(2J_i+1)(2l+1)}\Big(\frac{\mu_d\mu_p}{2\pi\hbar^2}\Big)^2\frac{k_p}{k_d}\sum_m|T_m|^2\]

with \(D_0^2 = 1.55\times10^4\) MeV² fm³. The distorted waves come from optical potentials fitted to elastic scattering; the bound state from a Woods–Saxon well whose depth reproduces the separation energy. The angular distribution identifies l (the first maximum moves out with l, roughly at \(qR \approx l\)); the ratio of measured to calculated cross sections is the spectroscopic factor S, the occupancy of the single-particle orbit (Macfarlane and Schiffer 1974). For weakly bound states the reaction samples only the tail of the bound state, \(u(r) \to C\,w_l(\kappa r)\): then the cross section is proportional to the asymptotic normalization coefficient C², which is less model-dependent than S and directly fixes direct-capture rates in astrophysics (Mukhamedzhanov and Tribble 1999).

36. Heavy-ion Fusion

Two heavy ions fuse when they cross the barrier formed by the nuclear attraction and the Coulomb repulsion. With a parabolic barrier for each partial wave,

\[\sigma_{\text{fus}} = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_l(2l+1)\Big[1 + \exp\frac{2\pi(V_B(l) - E)}{\hbar\omega_l}\Big]^{-1}\;\approx\;\frac{\hbar\omega R_B^2}{2E}\ln\Big[1 + \exp\frac{2\pi(E - V_B)}{\hbar\omega}\Big]\]

(Hill and Wheeler 1953; Wong 1973). Measured cross sections far below the barrier are orders of magnitude larger than this: the colliding nuclei vibrate and rotate, and the coupling to these states splits the single barrier into a distribution of barriers. In the constant-coupling approximation the coupling matrix is diagonalised at the barrier, \(\sigma = \sum_iw_i\,\sigma_{1D}(E - \lambda_i)\) (Dasso, Landowne and Winther 1983). The barrier distribution \(D(E) = d^2(E\sigma)/dE^2\) shows one peak per eigenbarrier; Rowley, Satchler and Stelson (1991) introduced it and high-precision data revealed the structure predicted for vibrational and rotational couplings (Dasgupta et al. 1998).

37. Compound-nucleus Decay

A compound nucleus forgets how it was formed (Bohr 1936) and decays statistically. In the Weisskopf–Ewing approximation the rate for emitting a particle x with energy ε is proportional to the inverse cross section and to the level density of the residual nucleus,

\[\frac{d\Gamma_x}{d\varepsilon} \propto (2s_x+1)\,\mu_x\,\varepsilon\,\sigma_{\text{inv}}(\varepsilon)\,\rho(E^* - S_x - \varepsilon),\qquad \rho(U) = \frac{\sqrt\pi}{12}\frac{e^{2\sqrt{aU}}}{a^{1/4}U^{5/4}},\quad a \approx \frac A8\ \mathrm{MeV}^{-1}\]

so the spectrum is nearly Maxwellian, \(\varepsilon e^{-\varepsilon/T}\) with \(T = \sqrt{U/a}\). Neutrons dominate because charged particles must overcome the Coulomb barrier. The Hauser–Feshbach theory adds angular-momentum and parity conservation and is the basis of the evaluated cross sections used in reactor physics and nucleosynthesis.

38. Global Optical Potentials and the Dispersion Relation

Global nucleon optical potentials give the parameters as smooth functions of A, Z and E: Becchetti–Greenlees (1969), CH89 (Varner et al. 1991, A = 40–209, 10–65 MeV) and Koning–Delaroche (2003, A = 24–209, 1 keV–200 MeV), which writes each depth as a function of \(E - E_F\). The volume integral per nucleon of the real part falls with energy, that of the imaginary part rises. Causality connects the two: the potential is analytic in the upper half energy plane, so \(\Delta V(E) = \frac{\mathcal P}{\pi}\int\frac{W(E')}{E' - E}\,dE'\) (Mahaux, Bortignon, Broglia and Dasso 1986), which explains the "Fermi surface anomaly" of the real potential near E_F.

39. R-matrix Theory

Inside a channel radius a the nuclear Hamiltonian has a discrete spectrum \(E_\lambda\); outside only Coulomb and centrifugal forces act. Matching gives, for one channel,

\[R(E) = \sum_\lambda\frac{\gamma_\lambda^2}{E_\lambda - E},\qquad \delta_l = -\phi_l + \arctan\frac{P_lR}{1 - (S_l - B)R},\qquad \Gamma = \frac{2P_l\gamma^2}{1 + \gamma^2\,dS_l/dE}\]

with the penetrability \(P = \rho/(F^2 + G^2)\), shift \(S = \rho(FF' + GG')/(F^2 + G^2)\) and hard-sphere phase \(\phi = \arctan(F/G)\) at \(\rho = ka\) (Wigner and Eisenbud 1947; Lane and Thomas 1958). The reduced width γ² measures how much the state looks like the two particles at the channel radius; its ratio to the Wigner limit \(3\hbar^2/(2\mu a^2)\) is the dimensionless reduced width θ². ⁸Be and the Hoyle state of ¹²C have widths of a few eV only because of the Coulomb barrier: their θ² are of order one, the signature of α-cluster structure. Multilevel, multichannel R-matrix (with radiative channels) is the standard tool for fitting resonance data and extrapolating astrophysical S-factors (Descouvemont and Baye 2010; AZURE).

40. Thermonuclear Reaction Rates

In a star the reaction rate per particle pair is \(\langle\sigma v\rangle = \sqrt{8/\pi\mu}\,(kT)^{-3/2}\int\sigma(E)E\,e^{-E/kT}dE\). Below the Coulomb barrier \(\sigma(E) = S(E)\,e^{-2\pi\eta}/E\) with a slowly varying S-factor, and the product of the Maxwell–Boltzmann tail and the tunnelling factor peaks in the Gamow window

\[E_0 = 0.1220\,(Z_1^2Z_2^2\mu T_9^2)^{1/3}\ \mathrm{MeV},\qquad \Delta = 0.2368\,(Z_1^2Z_2^2\mu T_9^5)^{1/6}\ \mathrm{MeV},\qquad N_A\langle\sigma v\rangle \approx \frac{4.339\times10^8}{\mu Z_1Z_2}S_{\text{eff}}\,\tau^2e^{-\tau}\]

with \(\tau = 3E_0/kT\). For the Sun (T₉ ≈ 0.015) E₀ is 6 keV for p + p and 18 keV for ⁷Be + p, far below where most cross sections can be measured, so S-factors are extrapolated (Adelberger et al. 2011). Electrons screen the nuclear charges: in stellar plasmas the rate is enhanced by \(\exp(0.188Z_1Z_2\zeta\rho^{1/2}T_6^{-3/2})\) (Salpeter 1954); in laboratory targets by \(\exp(\pi\eta U_e/E)\), an effect that must be removed from low-energy data and is often larger than expected.

41. Neutron Resonances and Doppler Broadening

Slow neutrons have no Coulomb barrier and see the compound-nucleus levels of heavy nuclei as sharp resonances. For s waves on a 0⁺ target the multilevel Breit–Wigner collision function is \(U = e^{-2ikR'}[1 + \sum_\lambda i\Gamma_{n\lambda}(E)/(E_\lambda - E - i\Gamma_\lambda/2)]\), with \(\Gamma_n(E) \propto \sqrt E\), so that \(\sigma_t = (2\pi/k^2)(1 - \mathrm{Re}\,U)\) and \(\sigma_n = (\pi/k^2)|1 - U|^2\). Below the first resonance the capture cross section follows 1/v. In a material at temperature T the thermal motion of the target broadens each resonance into a Voigt-like shape of width \(\Delta = \sqrt{4EkT/A}\): the peak drops, the area stays. For ²³⁸U in reactor fuel this increases resonance capture as the fuel heats up, the Doppler feedback that stabilises thermal reactors.

42. Elastic Electron Scattering

Electrons interact with the nucleus only electromagnetically, so their scattering measures the charge distribution model-independently. In the plane-wave Born approximation \(d\sigma/d\Omega = \sigma_{\text{Mott}}|F(q)|^2\) with \(F(q) = (4\pi/Z)\int\rho_{ch}(r)j_0(qr)r^2dr\) and \(q = 2E\sin(\theta/2)/\hbar c\). The diffraction minima reflect the sharp nuclear surface: for a uniform sphere F has zeros at \(qR = 4.49, 7.73, \dots\) Hofstadter's measurements (Nobel prize 1961) established \(R \approx 1.2A^{1/3}\) fm and a surface thickness of about 2.3 fm; model-independent Fourier–Bessel analyses (de Vries, de Jager and de Vries 1987) give the charge densities against which Hartree–Fock calculations are tested.

43. Masses, the Chart of Nuclides and Statistical Comparison

The semi-empirical mass formula (Weizsäcker 1935; Bethe and Bacher 1936)

\[B(N, Z) = a_vA - a_sA^{2/3} - a_c\frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1/3}} - a_a\frac{(N-Z)^2}{A} + \frac{\delta}{\sqrt A} - C\Big[\frac{F(N) + F(Z)}{(A/2)^{2/3}} - cA^{1/3}\Big]\]

is linear in its coefficients, so the lab fits them by least squares to the binding energies of its table. The last term is the shell correction of Myers and Swiatecki (1966), built from a function F that vanishes at the magic numbers and peaks between them; it roughly halves the rms deviation. Separation energies \(S_n = B(N, Z) - B(N-1, Z)\), \(S_p\), \(S_{2n}\) and \(Q_\alpha\) follow; the edges of the region with positive separation energies are the drip lines. About 3300 nuclides are known; mass models predict roughly 7000 bound ones, with the neutron drip line known only up to neon (⁶⁰Ca and beyond are being explored at RIKEN and FRIB).

Comparisons with data are summarised by pulls \((x_{\exp} - x_{\text{th}})/\sigma\) and by \(\chi^2/\text{ndf}\), where σ combines experimental and assumed theoretical uncertainties. For nuclear models the theoretical part dominates: a χ²/ndf near 1 means the assumed σ describes the scatter, not that the model is right. The Tools tab sweeps any input of several tabs, fits up to three inputs to data by Nelder–Mead minimisation of the summed squared residuals, and exports every plot (CSV, PNG) and every model summary as text.

Deuteron: the Reid soft-core potential with coupled S and D waves: binding, D-state probability, quadrupole moment, radius, asymptotic ratio η, magnetic moment and form factors; scale the tensor force to see its role. ³H, ³He, ⁴He: correlated Gaussians with all spin–isospin channels and the Minnesota force; the energy is shown against the basis size (it converges from above). Tjon line: forces with scaled repulsion, refitted to the same two-body data, give B(⁴He) against B(³H) on a line. Halo nuclei: ¹¹Li, ⁶He or ²²C as core + n + n; the n–core depth is fitted to S₂ₙ, the result is checked to be Borromean, and the matter radius is compared with experiment. The correlated-Gaussian runs take a few seconds each and can be stopped.

Nuclear chart

Chart of nuclides: the bound region, drip lines and magic numbers from a mass formula fitted to the binding energies in the table, coloured by B/A, S_n, S_p, S_2n, Q_α or the shell energy; white dots mark the data nuclei. Mass formula: coefficients, rms deviation, pulls and χ²/ndf with and without the shell term. Hartree–Fock against data: binding energies and charge radii of seven doubly magic nuclei for SLy4, SkM* and SIII, with pulls and χ² for assumed uncertainties.

Tools

Under every plot: CSV export of the plotted data, PNG, and a copy of the model description and results as text. Sweep: choose a tab (Bound states, Scattering, Hartree–Fock, coupled channels, deuteron, thermonuclear rates), an input, a range and an output; each point is a full calculation, and the inputs are restored afterwards. Fit: paste data "x y", choose the abscissa input and up to three parameters; Nelder–Mead adjusts them and leaves the result in the tab. The examples load synthetic data generated by the model.

14

Potential and system panels

Every problem in Scattering and reactions, Astrophysics and resonances, Resonance physics, Decay modes, Mean field and Few-body has a Potential and system panel above its Model list. It states the interaction that problem uses (its form, or that the problem is a parametrised formula rather than a potential, as for the Weisskopf–Ewing level density or the liquid-drop fission barrier) and gives every parameter as an editable field: depths, radii, diffusenesses, spin–orbit and Coulomb radii, Skyrme or Minnesota constants, ħ²/2m, and the system itself (masses m₁, m₂ or A, charges Z₁, Z₂, core masses, separation energies). The presets fill these fields; editing a field after a preset overrides it, and the result list then begins with Potential and parameters used, a snapshot of the values that run actually used, including derived ones such as the reduced mass or the fitted halo depth.
