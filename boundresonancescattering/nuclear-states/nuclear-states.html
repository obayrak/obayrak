Nuclear Bound, Quasi-bound and Scattering States — Theory

A nucleon outside a closed core, two light nuclei in contact, an α particle trapped inside a heavy nucleus, and a neutron fired at lead all obey the same radial Schrödinger equation. Its solutions fall into three classes: bound states at discrete negative energies (single-particle levels, the deuteron, halo nuclei), quasi-bound states or resonances held for a finite time behind a centrifugal or Coulomb barrier (⁵He, ⁸Be, α and proton emitters), and scattering states at every positive energy (elastic scattering, the optical model). This chapter covers the physics of all three and explains the numerical methods used in this lab.

1. The Two-body Radial Equation

For a projectile (n, p, d or α) interacting with a core of mass number A through a central potential plus a spin–orbit term, the relative motion separates. The particle moves with the reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂) in the partial wave with orbital angular momentum l and total angular momentum j = l ± ½. Writing ψ = [ulj(r)/r] 𝒴ljm gives

−(ħ²/2μ) u″ + [VN(r) + Vso(r) 2⟨l·s⟩ + VC(r) + ħ²l(l+1)/(2μr²)] u = E u, u(0) = 0

with 2⟨l·s⟩ = l for j = l + ½ and −(l + 1) for j = l − ½. The square bracket is the effective potential. Its three ingredients set the character of every state in this lab:

Nuclear well: short-ranged, about 50 MeV deep, with the shape of the nuclear density. Centrifugal barrier: zero for s waves, so s-wave nucleons can spread far outside the core, which is what produces halos. Coulomb barrier: for charged projectiles the long-range 1/r repulsion makes a barrier several MeV high even for l = 0, and that barrier traps α particles and protons for times from nanoseconds to billions of years.

2. Units and Kinematics

The natural units are MeV and fm. Two constants carry all the scales:

ħc = 197.327 MeV fm, e²/4πε₀ = 1.439965 MeV fm, ħ²/2mu = 20.9008 MeV fm²

The lab uses atomic masses (in u, 1 u = 931.494 MeV). Reaction data are quoted in the laboratory frame (projectile energy Elab, target at rest), but the radial equation lives in the centre-of-mass frame:

Ecm = Elab · Mt/(Mp + Mt), k = √(2μEcm)/ħ

For 14 MeV neutrons on lead the difference is half a percent. For α + α it is a factor of two, so the 92 keV ⁸Be resonance is seen at Elab = 184 keV. Bound-state energies are always relative to the threshold of the two fragments, E = −S, where S is the separation energy (Sn, Sp, Sα).

A width Γ and a mean life τ are linked by τ = ħ/Γ, with ħ = 6.5821·10⁻²² MeV s. The half-life is T½ = τ ln 2. A width of 1 eV means τ = 0.66 fs; 10⁻²¹ MeV means τ = 0.66 s.

3. The Nucleon–Nucleon System

The deuteron is the only bound two-nucleon state: Jπ = 1⁺, binding energy B = 2.224575 MeV. It is mostly ³S₁ with a 4–6 % ³D₁ admixture produced by the tensor force; this lab keeps the S wave only. The binding is so weak that the wavefunction extends far beyond the range of the force:

κ = √(2μB)/ħ = 0.2316 fm⁻¹, 1/κ = 4.32 fm, u(r) → AS e−κr

About 70–80 % of the probability lies outside the potential, so the deuteron is the first halo nucleus. Its matter radius rd = ½√⟨r²⟩ = 1.97 fm and its asymptotic normalization AS = 0.885 fm−½ depend almost entirely on B. Any well (Yukawa, square, Hulthén) fitted to B gives close to the same values, an example of shape independence.

In the spin singlet (¹S₀) the same force does not bind. The scattering length is large and negative, as = −23.74 fm: the pole of the S-matrix sits on the negative imaginary k axis, a virtual state 66 keV from threshold. It shows up as the huge zero-energy n–p cross section (Problem 20.6), not as a level.

Yukawa and Hulthén forms. One-pion exchange gives the Yukawa shape V₀e−r/b/(r/b) with b = ħ/mπc = 1.41 fm. The Hulthén potential V₀/(er/b − 1) has the same tail and is solvable in closed form for l = 0, En = −(ħ²/2μb²)[(g − n²)/2n]² with g = 2μV₀b²/ħ². The Validation tab uses it as an exact check.

4. The Mean Field: Woods–Saxon

In a heavy nucleus each nucleon moves in the average field of the others. That field follows the density: flat inside, falling to zero over a surface about 2 fm thick. The Woods–Saxon form captures it with three numbers:

VN(r) = −V₀ f(r, R, a), f = 1/[1 + e(r−R)/a], R = r₀A1/3

Typical values are r₀ ≈ 1.25 fm, a ≈ 0.65 fm and V₀ ≈ 51 ± 33(N − Z)/A MeV, deeper for protons than for neutrons in neutron-rich nuclei (the symmetry term). In the presets V₀ is fitted to one measured separation energy, and the other levels are then predictions.

A pure harmonic oscillator, ½μω²r², is the analytic limit of a deep well. Its levels E = ħω(2nr + l + 3/2) group into degenerate shells N = 2nr + l with (N + 1)(N + 2) states each (spin included), so the shells close at 2, 8, 20, 40, 70, 112, 168. Only the first three are real magic numbers. The flat bottom of the Woods–Saxon well lowers high-l states relative to low-l states within a shell, but it still cannot produce 28, 50, 82 or 126.

5. Spin–Orbit Coupling and Magic Numbers

The missing ingredient, found by Mayer and by Haxel, Jensen and Suess in 1949, is a strong spin–orbit force concentrated at the nuclear surface. In the Thomas form used here,

Vso(r) 2⟨l·s⟩ = Vso (ħ/mπc)² (1/r) |dfso/dr| · 2⟨l·s⟩, (ħ/mπc)² ≈ 2 fm²

With Vso ≈ 6–7 MeV the j = l + ½ member of each high-l doublet drops by about l·(1–1.5) MeV and the j = l − ½ member rises. The splitting grows as 2l + 1, so the 1f₇/₂, 1g₉/₂, 1h₁₁/₂ and 1i₁₃/₂ levels are pushed down into the shell below, creating the gaps at 28, 50, 82 and 126.

The remaining errors come from the momentum dependence of the real mean field (the effective mass) and from coupling to surface vibrations, which compress the levels near the Fermi surface.

6. The Coulomb Potential of the Core

For a charged projectile the core is modelled as a uniformly charged sphere of radius Rc = rcA1/3:

VC(r) = (Z₁Z₂e²/2Rc)(3 − r²/Rc²) for r < Rc, Z₁Z₂e²/r for r ≥ Rc

Inside, it adds a nearly constant repulsion of about 1.5Z₁Z₂e²/Rc, about 24 MeV for a proton in lead. This is why proton levels lie higher than neutron levels with the same quantum numbers. The Coulomb barrier for α + ²⁰⁸Pb peaks near 26 MeV, nearly three times the α-decay Q-value of ²¹²Po.

Outside the core the solutions are the regular and irregular Coulomb functions Fl(η, kr) and Gl(η, kr), with the Sommerfeld parameter

η = Z₁Z₂e²μ/(ħ²k) = Z₁Z₂α √(μc²/2E)

When η ≫ 1 the particle sees a classical barrier and tunnelling dominates; when η ≪ 1 the Coulomb field is a small correction. The lab computes F and G by Steed's continued-fraction method, and below the barrier by direct integration, so that the penetrability 1/(F² + G²) at the matching radius stays accurate when it is 10⁻³⁰.

7. Weak Binding: Halos and the ANC

Beyond the range of the nuclear force a bound state decays as

u(r) → C W−η,l+½(2κr) (charged), u(r) → C κ r hl(+)(iκr) ∝ e−κr (neutral)

The constant C is the asymptotic normalization coefficient (ANC). It is model-independent in the sense that it is what peripheral reactions measure: transfer at sub-Coulomb energies and radiative capture at astrophysical energies probe only the tail. The lab extracts C by integrating inward from a large radius and matching to the Whittaker function.

For a weakly bound s- or p-wave neutron the tail dominates: ⟨r²⟩ grows as 1/κ² for l = 0 and as 1/κ for l = 1, and diverges at threshold. This is a neutron halo. ¹¹Be (Sn = 0.502 MeV, 2s₁/₂ ground state) has an rms radius of about 7 fm for the halo neutron, against about 2.3 fm for the ¹⁰Be core. The lab flags a halo when more than half of the probability lies outside R and the rms radius exceeds 1.3R.

Parity inversion in ¹¹Be. A simple shell model puts the 1p₁/₂ neutron below 2s₁/₂, as in ¹³C. In ¹¹Be the order is reversed: the 1/2⁺ ground state lies 0.32 MeV below the 1/2⁻ state. A single Woods–Saxon fitted to the s-state binds the p₁/₂ far too deeply. The inversion is explained by core excitation (coupling to the 2⁺ state of deformed ¹⁰Be), which is outside a one-channel model. The preset displays the failure deliberately.

8. Mirror Nuclei and the Thomas–Ehrman Shift

Charge symmetry says that ¹⁷O (n + ¹⁶O) and ¹⁷F (p + ¹⁶O) should have the same spectrum apart from the Coulomb energy. For the deeply bound 1d₅/₂ ground states the shift is uniform. The 2s₁/₂ excited state, however, lies at 0.871 MeV in ¹⁷O but only 0.495 MeV in ¹⁷F.

The reason is the extended s-wave tail. In ¹⁷F the 1/2⁺ state is bound by just 0.105 MeV, so the proton spends much of its time outside the core, where the Coulomb repulsion is weaker than inside. The state therefore gains less Coulomb energy than the compact d₅/₂ level. This Thomas–Ehrman shift (1951–52) is reproduced by the same Woods–Saxon well with the Coulomb term switched on, and ¹⁷F(1/2⁺) is a textbook proton halo.

9. Partial Waves with Spin ½

For a spin-½ projectile on a spin-0 target each partial wave splits into j = l ± ½, with S-matrix elements Sl± = ηl±e2iδl±. Outside the nuclear range the radial function is a combination of incoming and outgoing Coulomb waves H∓ = G ∓ iF, and the lab obtains S directly from two neighbouring grid points:

Slj = (u₂H₁− − u₁H₂−) / (u₂H₁+ − u₁H₂+)

The scattering amplitude is a 2 × 2 matrix in spin space, M = f(θ) + g(θ) σ·n̂, with

f(θ) = fC(θ) + (1/2ik) Σl e2iσl[(l + 1)(Sl+ − 1) + l(Sl− − 1)] Pl(cos θ)
g(θ) = (1/2k) Σl e2iσl(Sl+ − Sl−) Pl¹(cos θ)

where σl = arg Γ(l + 1 + iη) are the Coulomb phases. The spin-flip amplitude g exists only because the spin–orbit force makes Sl+ ≠ Sl−.

For a spin-0 projectile (α, or a model spin-0 particle) there is a single Sl per l and g = 0.

10. Coulomb Scattering and the Mott Formula

The pure Coulomb amplitude is known exactly:

fC(θ) = −[η/(2k sin²(θ/2))] exp[−iη ln sin²(θ/2) + 2iσ₀]

|fC|² is the Rutherford cross section, (Z₁Z₂e²/4E)²/sin⁴(θ/2). Because the Coulomb partial-wave series does not converge, the nuclear part is always added as the difference S − 1 multiplied by e2iσl, as in Section 9. Far below the barrier the nuclear S-matrix tends to 1 and the ratio σ/σR tends to 1; the Validation tab checks this for p + ²⁰⁸Pb at 5 MeV.

Identical particles. For two identical spin-0 bosons (α + α) the wavefunction must be symmetric, so only even l survive and the amplitudes at θ and π − θ add:

dσ/dΩ = |f(θ) + f(π − θ)|²

For pure Coulomb scattering this gives the Mott formula, with an interference term cos[η ln tan²(θ/2)] that oscillates around 90° in the c.m. frame. The lab uses the symmetrized sum with even partial waves doubled.

11. Low-Energy Scattering

For a short-range potential and k → 0 only the s wave matters, and its phase shift follows the effective-range expansion (Bethe 1949):

k cot δ₀ = −1/a + ½ r₀ k² + …, σ(E → 0) = 4πa²

Two numbers, the scattering length a and the effective range r₀, summarize the potential at low energy. The sign of a tells which side of threshold the nearest pole lies on: a > 0 for a bound state (the deuteron, at = 5.419 fm) and a < 0 for a virtual state (singlet n–p, as = −23.74 fm). For a weakly bound state a ≈ 1/κ plus a correction of order r₀.

Levinson's theorem. δl(0) − δl(∞) = Nlπ, where Nl is the number of bound states in the partial wave. (For l = 0 a zero-energy resonance adds π/2.) The lab plots δ/π, so the bound-state count can be read from the zero-energy end.

For charged particles the expansion is modified by the Coulomb field. The lab fits a and r₀ only for neutral projectiles and only when kminR < 0.3, where the expansion is reliable.

12. The Optical Model

When a nucleon hits a nucleus, part of the flux goes into inelastic channels and reactions. To describe the elastic channel alone, all of these are lumped into an imaginary potential that removes flux:

U(r) = −V f(r, RV, aV) − iW f(r, RW, aW) + 4iWD aD df(r, RD, aD)/dr + Vso term + VC

The volume absorption W dominates at high energy. The surface term WD (a derivative Woods–Saxon peaked at the surface) dominates at low energy, where only the surface nucleons can be excited. With W ≠ 0 the S-matrix satisfies |S| < 1, and

σR = (π/k²) Σlj [(2j + 1)/(2s + 1)] (1 − |Slj|²)

is the reaction (absorption) cross section.

Becchetti–Greenlees. The global parameter set of Becchetti and Greenlees (1969) gives V, W, WD and Vso as linear functions of E, (N − Z)/A and Z/A1/3, fitted to elastic scattering of protons and neutrons with A > 40 and E < 50 MeV. The global OMP button recomputes the parameters at every energy of a scan. Modern sets (Koning–Delaroche 2003) are more accurate but more complex; BG is still within 5–10 % for total cross sections in its range.

13. Cross Sections and Polarization

For spin ½ on spin 0 the observables are

dσ/dΩ = |f|² + |g|², Ay(θ) = 2 Im(f g*)/(|f|² + |g|²)

Ay is the analyzing power: the left–right asymmetry seen when a polarized beam scatters. It is non-zero only with spin–orbit coupling and is the most direct measurement of Vso.

For neutrons (no Coulomb) the integrated cross sections are finite:

σel = (π/k²) Σ (2j + 1)/2 |1 − Slj|², σtot = σel + σR = (4π/k) Im f(0)

The last equality is the optical theorem, a consequence of flux conservation, and the Validation tab checks it to 10⁻¹³. The measured neutron total cross section of lead at 14 MeV is about 5.4 b, close to the black-disc estimate 2π(R + 1/k)² (Problem 20.7). Its slow oscillations with energy are the Ramsauer effect: interference between the wave going through the nucleus and the wave going around it.

For charged projectiles σel and σtot diverge (the Coulomb force has infinite range), so the lab reports σR and the ratio of dσ/dΩ to Rutherford. A drop of that ratio below 1 at forward-backward angles marks the onset of nuclear absorption; the grazing partial wave lg, where |Sl| = ½, measures the strong-absorption radius.

14. Resonances

A quasi-bound state is a pole of Sl(E) in the lower half of the complex energy plane, at E = Er − iΓ/2. Near the pole the phase shift jumps by π through 90° and the partial cross section takes the Breit–Wigner form:

δl(E) = δbg + arctan[Γ/2(Er − E)], σl = (π/k²)(2j + 1)/(2s + 1) · Γ²/[(E − Er)² + Γ²/4]

The state is trapped behind the centrifugal barrier (neutrons with l > 0) or the Coulomb barrier (charged particles). Neutron s-waves have no barrier, so they show no sharp single-particle resonances, only virtual states. Light-nucleus examples in the lab:

The single-particle limit γ²sp ≈ ħ²/μR² sets the scale. The measured width divided by the calculated single-particle width is the spectroscopic factor, or for α decay the preformation factor.

15. Barrier Penetration and α Decay

Gamow, and independently Gurney and Condon, explained α decay in 1928 as tunnelling. In the WKB approximation the decay width is

Γ = ħ ν P, P = exp[−2∫r₂r₃ √(2μ(Veff − Q))/ħ dr], ν = [∫r₁r₂ 2μ dr / ħk(r)]⁻¹

where r₁ < r₂ < r₃ are the classical turning points, ν is the assault frequency and P the barrier transmission. The exponent depends on Q mostly through the Gamow factor 2πη ∝ Z₁Z₂/√Q, which gives the Geiger–Nuttall law:

log₁₀ T½ ≈ a Z/√Q + b

A change of Q from 4 to 9 MeV changes T½ by more than 20 orders of magnitude, from 10¹⁷ s (²³²Th) to 10⁻⁷ s (²¹²Po). The Geiger–Nuttall sweep on the Quasi-bound tab reproduces this by moving the resonance energy with fixed potential geometry.

Cluster model. In the Buck–Merchant–Perez approach the α particle moves in a potential (here the cosh shape) whose radius is fixed by requiring a state with the Wildermuth quantum number G = 2n + l = 22 at the measured Q. With no free parameters this gives T½(²¹²Po) = 0.15 µs against 0.299 µs measured. The ratio, ≈ 0.5, is the α preformation probability: well-understood for this nucleus with two nucleons outside each closed shell.

16. Proton Emission

Beyond the proton drip line a proton can be unbound yet held by the Coulomb and centrifugal barriers for milliseconds. Proton emitters are very clean single-particle systems: the proton occupies a definite orbit (πh₁₁/₂ in ¹⁵¹Lu, l = 5), so the half-life depends sharply on l through the centrifugal barrier and is a direct measurement of the orbital.

¹⁵¹Lu (Qp = 1.241 MeV, T½ = 80.6 ms, 63 % proton branch) was the first ground-state proton emitter, discovered at GSI (Hofmann et al., 1982). With V₀ fitted to Q the lab gives a partial half-life of 37 ms against 127 ms measured; the ratio is the spectroscopic factor, about 0.3, in line with the partial occupation of the h₁₁/₂ orbit expected from BCS pairing.

Changing l at fixed Q shows the sensitivity: an s₁/₂ proton at the same energy would escape about 10⁵ times faster.

17. Cluster States: ⁸Be

⁸Be is unbound by 91.84 keV against breakup into two α particles, yet has a half-life of 8·10⁻¹⁷ s, about 10⁵ times longer than the time an α needs to cross the nucleus. It is a Coulomb-barrier resonance of two bosons. Its existence (and the 7.65 MeV Hoyle state of ¹²C built on it) makes the triple-α process in stars possible.

The Ali–Bodmer potential describes α–α scattering with two Gaussians: a short-range repulsion that mimics the Pauli exclusion of the nucleons in the two clusters and a longer-range attraction. Fitting the attraction to Er = 91.84 keV gives Γ ≈ 7.3 eV, close to the measured 5.57 eV. Since the α particles are identical spin-0 bosons, only even l are allowed and the angular distribution is symmetric about 90° (Section 10).

A width of a few eV at 92 keV means Γ/E ≈ 10⁻⁴, far too sharp to locate on a uniform energy grid. The lab finds it by locating the energy where a box state stabilizes, then refines it with the phase-shift derivative and the complex pole (Section 18).

20. Practice Problems

1. Find the decay constant κ and the size 1/κ of the deuteron.
μc² = mnmp/(mn + mp) = 469.46 MeV. κ = √(2 × 469.46 × 2.2246)/197.327 = 45.70/197.327 = 0.2316 fm⁻¹, so 1/κ = 4.32 fm, three times the range of the force.
2. How many neutrons fill the shells up to and including 1d₃/₂?
1s₁/₂ (2) + 1p₃/₂ (4) + 1p₁/₂ (2) + 1d₅/₂ (6) + 2s₁/₂ (2) + 1d₃/₂ (4) = 20, a magic number (⁴⁰Ca).
3. Estimate the Coulomb barrier for α + ²⁰⁸Pb, with R = 1.2(A₁1/3 + A₂1/3) fm.
R = 1.2(1.587 + 5.925) = 9.01 fm. VB = 2 × 82 × 1.44/9.01 = 26.2 MeV, about three times Q(²¹²Po) = 8.95 MeV, so the α must tunnel.
4. Compute η and the Gamow factor 2πη for the α in ²¹²Po decay.
μc² = (4 × 208/212) × 931.5 = 3656 MeV. η = Z₁Z₂α √(μc²/2E) = (164/137.036) √(3656/17.91) = 1.197 × 14.29 = 17.1. 2πη = 107, so the point-charge Gamow factor is e−2πη ≈ 10⁻⁴⁷. The finite nuclear radius removes the inner, thickest part of the barrier and reduces the exponent a great deal, but the transmission is still tiny: with an assault frequency near 10²¹ s⁻¹ the measured 0.3 µs half-life implies P ≈ 10⁻¹⁵.
5. A state has Γ = 10⁻²¹ MeV. What are its mean life and half-life?
τ = 6.582·10⁻²²/10⁻²¹ = 0.658 s; T½ = 0.658 × 0.693 = 0.456 s.
6. Use as = −23.74 fm and at = 5.42 fm to estimate the zero-energy n–p cross section.
Singlet 4πas² = 70.8 b, triplet 4πat² = 3.69 b. Spin weights ¼ and ¾: σ = 17.7 + 2.8 = 20.5 b, close to the measured free n–p cross section of 20.4 b. The singlet, with only a quarter of the weight, dominates because of the virtual state.
7. Estimate the total cross section for 14 MeV neutrons on ²⁰⁸Pb from the black-disc model.
R = 1.25 × 2081/3 = 7.4 fm. Ecm = 13.93 MeV, μc² ≈ 935 MeV, k = √(2 × 935 × 13.93)/197.3 = 0.818 fm⁻¹, 1/k = 1.22 fm. σtot = 2π(R + 1/k)² = 2π × 8.62² = 467 fm² = 4.7 b (measured ≈ 5.4 b; the optical model gives 5.38 b).
8. Why does the ¹⁷F 1/2⁺ state lie lower relative to its ¹⁷O mirror than the 5/2⁺ ground state does?
The s₁/₂ proton is barely bound and spends much of its time outside the core, where the Coulomb potential is weaker than the near-constant 3Ze²/2Rc inside. It therefore gains less Coulomb energy than the compact d₅/₂ state: the Thomas–Ehrman shift (0.871 → 0.495 MeV excitation).
9. The Geiger–Nuttall plot gives a slope of about 1.6 Z per unit of 1/√(Q/MeV) in log₁₀ T½. How much shorter is the half-life if Q rises from 8.0 to 9.0 MeV for Z = 84?
Δ(1/√Q) = 0.3536 − 0.3333 = 0.0202. Δlog₁₀T½ = 1.6 × 84 × 0.0202 ≈ 2.7, so about 500 times shorter.

21. History

1928: Gamow, and Gurney and Condon, explain α decay as quantum tunnelling, the first application of quantum mechanics to the nucleus. 1930: Mott derives the cross section for identical particles. 1932: Chadwick discovers the neutron; the deuteron is identified the same year by Urey. 1935: Yukawa proposes the meson theory of the nuclear force. 1949: Bethe's effective-range theory; Mayer and Haxel, Jensen and Suess explain the magic numbers with spin–orbit coupling (Nobel Prize 1963). 1951–52: Thomas and Ehrman explain the level shifts in mirror nuclei. 1954: Woods and Saxon introduce their potential for proton scattering; Feshbach, Porter and Weisskopf formulate the optical model. 1966: Ali and Bodmer fit the α–α potential. 1969: Becchetti and Greenlees publish their global optical potential. 1981–82: ground-state proton emission is discovered at GSI (¹⁵¹Lu). 1985: Tanihata measures the large interaction cross section of ¹¹Li, revealing halo nuclei.

22. Limits of the Models

Single channel. The core is inert: no core excitation (needed for the ¹¹Be parity inversion and for most spectroscopic factors), no coupled channels, no compound-nucleus resonances. The tensor force and the deuteron D state are absent, so the quadrupole moment is zero.

Local, energy-independent potentials. Real nuclear mean fields are non-local (or energy-dependent with an effective mass m* ≈ 0.7m deep inside), which spreads the calculated level scheme compared with experiment. The spin–orbit form is phenomenological. Pauli blocking between the projectile and the core is mimicked only by the repulsive core of Ali–Bodmer or by the choice of nodes.

Optical model. Becchetti–Greenlees is fitted for A > 40 and 10 < E < 50 MeV. Outside that range (light targets, low-energy neutrons) it gives the trend, not the detail; compound-elastic scattering, which matters below a few MeV, is not included. The deuteron and α optical potentials need their own parameter sets.

Decay rates. Calculated half-lives are single-particle values. The comparison with experiment yields a spectroscopic or preformation factor; it is not a free-parameter fit. Uncertainties of a factor of 2–3 come from the potential radius alone.

Numerics. Resonances with Γ/E below about 10⁻⁹ are given by WKB only. Phase shifts neglect the nuclear potential beyond Rm. Box-stabilization can miss resonances wider than their own energy; the phase-shift view (fitPhase mode for ⁵He) is the reliable one there.

01

Choose a system

Pick a preset or build your own: projectile (n, p, d, α), target A, Z and mass, the spin treatment (spin ½ with j = l ± ½, no spin, or identical spin-0 bosons for α + α) and the potential. Woods–Saxon radii are R = r₀AT⅓; the Coulomb potential is that of a uniformly charged sphere of radius rcAT⅓. The reduced mass follows from the two masses. Energies are centre-of-mass energies except on the Scattering tab, where Elab is used.

02

Bound states

Shell structure solves every (l, j) up to the chosen l and lists the levels in order with their occupancy 2j + 1, the running nucleon number and the large gaps (magic numbers), side by side with measured single-particle energies where the preset has them. One partial wave shows the wavefunctions. For the selected level the panel gives the rms radius, the probability outside the nuclear radius (halo), and the asymptotic normalization coefficient (ANC) from the Whittaker function. Fit adjusts one parameter so that a chosen level reproduces a measured energy: bound (E < 0), narrow quasi-bound, or broad (phase shift through 90°).

03

Quasi-bound states and decay

Choose l, j and an energy window, then Find resonances: Er, Γ (Breit–Wigner, complex pole, WKB), half-life T½ = ħ ln 2/Γ, and the comparison with the measured half-life (their ratio is a spectroscopic or preformation factor). For charged particles the panel adds the Coulomb barrier, the Sommerfeld parameter and the Gamow factor. Geiger–Nuttall: the WKB half-life as a function of the decay energy at fixed potential, plotted as log T½ against Z/√Q.

04

Scattering

Choose an Elab range, lmax (beyond the grazing partial wave kR + η) and Rm (outside the nuclear potential). With a global optical potential the parameters are recalculated at each energy. Plots: σel, σR and (for neutrons) σtot versus energy; at E*: the angular distribution (as a ratio to Rutherford for charged particles, Mott scattering for identical bosons), the analyzing power, the partial-wave profile |Sl|; phase shifts; and the s-wave effective-range plot for the two-nucleon system.

05

Validation

Exact spectra (oscillator, Hulthén), unitarity, the optical theorem, the Rutherford and Mott limits, the independent sinc-DVR diagonalisation, Breit–Wigner versus pole for a proton resonance, and the deuteron, ⁸Be, ²¹²Po and ¹⁵¹Lu against measured values.
