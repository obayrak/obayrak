Bound, Quasi-bound and Scattering States in Particle Physics — Theory

Most hadrons are not stable particles but resonances that live for 10⁻²³ s, and the stable ones are bound states of quarks that cannot be pulled apart. Between these extremes sit near-threshold molecules, exotic atoms and heavy-quark systems that are bound for practical purposes but decay weakly or electromagnetically. One framework covers all of them: the S-matrix and its singularities. Bound states are poles on the physical sheet below threshold, resonances are poles on an unphysical sheet, and scattering data measure the analytic function in between. This chapter covers the physics behind each tab and the methods used to compute it.

1. Three Classes of States

For two particles with relative momentum q the scattering amplitude in a partial wave is an analytic function of the energy, or of s = Ecm². Its singularities classify the states:

StatePole of S(s)Observed asExamples
bound statereal s below threshold, physical sheet (Im q > 0, q = iκ)a stable particle or a narrow lineJ/ψ, Υ, deuteron, X(3872), atomic levels
virtual statereal s below threshold, unphysical sheet (q = −iκ)a large negative scattering length, a cuspn–p singlet, possibly some near-threshold XYZ states
resonancecomplex s = m² − imΓ, unphysical sheeta peak and a phase rising through 90°ρ, Δ(1232), Z, φ, ψ(3770)
scattering statenone: the continuumcross sections, phase shiftsπN, e⁺e⁻ → ff̄

The same object can change class as parameters vary. Deepen a well and a virtual state becomes bound. Add a decay channel and a bound state acquires a width. Charmonium is bound with respect to quarks (confinement) but quasi-bound with respect to annihilation into gluons or photons.

2. Units, Kinematics and Lifetimes

Particle physicists set ħ = c = 1 and measure energy, momentum and mass in MeV or GeV. The two conversion constants are

\[\hbar c = 197.327\ \text{MeV fm} = 0.197327\ \text{GeV fm},\qquad \hbar = 6.582\times10^{-25}\ \text{GeV s},\qquad 1\ \text{GeV}^{-2} = 0.3894\ \text{mb}\]

A two-body system is described by s = (p₁ + p₂)², the square of the c.m. energy. The momentum of either particle in the c.m. frame (the break-up momentum in a decay) is

\[q(s) = \frac{\sqrt{\big[s - (m_1 + m_2)^2\big]\big[s - (m_1 - m_2)^2\big]}}{2\sqrt s}\]

A state of width Γ lives τ = ħ/Γ on average. The table spans 20 orders of magnitude:

A width of 100 MeV means the state lives only about as long as light takes to cross it (1 fm/c ≈ 3.3 × 10⁻²⁴ s). For such states "particle" and "resonance in a scattering amplitude" are the same thing.

3. Heavy Quarkonium and the QCD Potential

For a c or b quark the mass (1.5 GeV, 4.7 GeV) is large compared with the QCD scale ΛQCD ≈ 0.3 GeV. The quarks then move slowly, v²/c² ≈ 0.25 for cc̄ and 0.08 for bb̄, and a non-relativistic Schrödinger equation with a static potential is a good first approximation:

\[-\frac{1}{2\mu}\,u^{\prime\prime} + \Big[V(r) + \frac{l(l + 1)}{2\mu r^2}\Big]u = E\,u,\qquad M = m_1 + m_2 + E,\qquad \mu = \frac{m_1m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\]

QCD fixes the potential at both ends. At short distance asymptotic freedom makes one-gluon exchange dominant. That gives a Coulomb potential with the colour factor CF = 4/3 of a colour-singlet qq̄ pair. At large distance the gluon field collapses into a flux tube of constant energy per unit length (the string tension σ ≈ 0.18 GeV² ≈ 0.9 GeV/fm), giving linear confinement. The Cornell potential (Eichten et al. 1975) combines the two:

\[V(r) = -\frac{4}{3}\,\frac{\alpha_s}{r} + \sigma r + C\]

Lattice QCD computes the static potential from first principles and confirms this shape between 0.1 and 1 fm. Because gluons couple to colour and not to flavour, the potential is flavour independent: the same V describes cc̄, bb̄ and bc̄ with different masses. The Martin power law \(V = A + Br^{0.1}\) exploits a consequence: for \(V \propto \ln r\) the level spacings would not depend on the mass at all, and the 2S − 1S splitting is indeed 589 MeV in charmonium and 563 MeV in bottomonium.

Pure Coulomb and pure linear limits. For \(V = -\kappa/r\) the levels are \(E_n = -\mu\kappa^2/2n^2\), with 2S and 2P degenerate. For \(V = \sigma r\) the S waves are \(E_n = (\sigma^2/2\mu)^{1/3}|a_n|\), where \(a_n = -2.338,\ -4.088,\ -5.521\) are the zeros of the Airy function. In the Coulomb case levels shrink towards zero; in the linear case they grow as n2/3. Real quarkonium lies in between: 1P sits between 1S and 2S, and the spacings are nearly independent of the quark mass. The Validation tab checks both limits to 10⁻⁸.

4. Spectroscopic Notation and JPC

A quark and antiquark with orbital angular momentum L and total spin S = 0 or 1 couple to total J. States are written \(n\,^{2S+1}L_J\), with n the radial quantum number (n = 1 for the lowest level of each L). The quark and antiquark have opposite intrinsic parity, and the antiquark carries charge conjugation:

\[P = (-1)^{L+1},\qquad C = (-1)^{L+S}\]

Vector states (1⁻⁻) have the quantum numbers of the photon. They are made directly in e⁺e⁻ annihilation, which is why J/ψ and Υ were found first. A qq̄ pair can never have JPC = 0⁻⁻, 0⁺⁻, 1⁻⁺, 2⁺⁻, …: a state with these "exotic" quantum numbers would have to be a hybrid, glueball or multiquark.

5. Spin-Dependent Forces

The Breit–Fermi reduction of one-gluon exchange (a Lorentz vector) plus confinement (assumed to be a Lorentz scalar) gives, to order 1/m², for equal masses:

\[V_{SS} = \frac{32\pi\alpha_s}{9m^2}\,\delta^3(\mathbf r)\,\mathbf S_1\!\cdot\mathbf S_2,\qquad V_{LS} = \frac{1}{m^2}\Big(\frac{2\alpha_s}{r^3} - \frac{\sigma}{2r}\Big)\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S,\qquad V_T = \frac{\alpha_s}{3m^2r^3}\,S_{12}\]

Here \(\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2 = \tfrac14\) for S = 1 and \(-\tfrac34\) for S = 0; \(\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S = -(L+1),\ -1,\ L\) for J = L − 1, L, L + 1; and the tensor operator has \(\langle S_{12}\rangle = -2(J+2)/(2J+1),\ 2,\ -2(J-1)/(2J+1)\) for L = J + 1, J, J − 1. The lab evaluates them in first-order perturbation theory with the spin-averaged wavefunctions:

Hyperfine splitting. The contact term splits only S waves: M(J/ψ) − M(ηc) = 113 MeV. Treating δ³(r) literally overestimates it; Barnes, Godfrey and Swanson smear it into a Gaussian \((\sigma/\sqrt\pi)^3e^{-\sigma^2r^2}\), which the lab uses.

Fine structure. In the P waves the vector spin–orbit term pushes χc2 up and χc0 down. The scalar confining term, −σ/2r, enters with the opposite sign and reduces the splitting (Thomas precession). The measured pattern, M(χc2) − M(χc1) = 46 MeV against M(χc1) − M(χc0) = 96 MeV, needs the tensor force. It supports a vector short-range and a scalar long-range interaction. All spin corrections vanish on the weighted average \(\sum(2J+1)M_J/9\), and hc (¹P₁) lies within 0.1 MeV of it: the P-wave hyperfine interaction is almost zero, as a contact force predicts.

6. The Wavefunction at the Origin: Annihilation Widths

Annihilation needs the quark and antiquark at the same point, so S-wave annihilation rates are proportional to \(|\psi(0)|^2 = |R(0)|^2/4\pi\). For a vector state into a lepton pair (Van Royen–Weisskopf 1967) and a pseudoscalar into two photons:

\[\Gamma(^3S_1 \to e^+e^-) = \frac{4\alpha^2e_q^2}{M^2}\,|R(0)|^2\Big(1 - \frac{16\alpha_s}{3\pi}\Big),\qquad \Gamma(^1S_0 \to \gamma\gamma) = \frac{3\alpha^2e_q^4}{M^2}\,|R(0)|^2\Big(1 - \frac{3.4\,\alpha_s}{\pi}\Big)\]

For S waves there is an exact identity that follows from the radial equation (the "wavefunction at the origin theorem"):

\[|R(0)|^2 = 2\mu\,\Big\langle\frac{dV}{dr}\Big\rangle\]

The lab computes |R(0)|² both ways, from the small-r limit of u/r and from the identity, as an internal check. The first-order QCD correction is large for charmonium (about −0.5 at αs = 0.25), so absolute widths are uncertain to tens of percent. Ratios between radial excitations are much more robust: Γee(ψ(2S))/Γee(J/ψ) = 0.42 and Γee(Υ(2S))/Γee(Υ(1S)) = 0.46. The ratio Γee(Υ)/Γee(J/ψ) ≈ 0.24 measures the quark charges: eb²/ec² = 1/4.

7. Radiative E1 Transitions

The P-wave states were discovered through photons emitted when ψ(2S) decays to them, and they decay to J/ψ by emitting another photon. In the long-wavelength limit the rates are electric dipole:

\[\Gamma(n\,^3S_1 \to \gamma\,n^{\prime}\,^3P_J) = \frac{4}{27}\,\alpha e_q^2\,(2J + 1)\,k^3\,|\langle f|r|i\rangle|^2,\qquad \Gamma(^3P_J \to \gamma\,^3S_1) = \frac{4}{9}\,\alpha e_q^2\,k^3\,|\langle f|r|i\rangle|^2\]

where k is the photon energy, \(k = (M_i^2 - M_f^2)/2M_i\). The 1P → 1S matrix elements (no node) come out at the right size. The 2S → 1P ones involve the node of the 2S wavefunction and cancel strongly. The model overestimates them by factors of 2–3 in charmonium, a known sign of relativistic and coupled-channel effects.

8. Thresholds, String Breaking and Exotic States

Above 2MD = 3730 MeV a cc̄ state can decay strongly into D D̄ (OZI allowed): ψ(3770) is 27 MeV wide, J/ψ 93 keV. Physically the flux tube breaks by creating a light qq̄ pair, so the linear rise must stop. A screened potential \(\sigma(1 - e^{-\mu r})/\mu\) models this: it flattens at σ/μ and pulls the high radial excitations down. Coupling to open-flavour channels also shifts the levels and mixes them with D D̄ continuum states. This is the regime of the XYZ states found since 2003. Many lie close to two-meson thresholds and do not fit the qq̄ pattern: χc1(3872), Zc(3900)± (charged, hence at least four quarks), the Pc pentaquarks and Tcc⁺.

9. Toponium: Bound but Unstable

The top quark is heavy enough (172.6 GeV) for its tt̄ system to be Coulombic: with αs ≈ 0.14 the 1S state would be bound by about 1.5 GeV, with a Bohr radius near 0.02 fm. But the top decays weakly with Γt = 1.42 GeV. Each level is broadened by 2Γt ≈ 2.8 GeV, more than the 1S–2S spacing, so the levels merge into a single enhancement at the tt̄ threshold. In 2025 CMS observed such a threshold excess, consistent with a colour-singlet pseudoscalar (¹S₀) quasi-bound toponium state; a 2026 analysis in the single-lepton channel confirmed it. The toponium preset compares the Bohr radius with the distance a top quark travels before it decays.

10. Hadronic Molecules and Compositeness

When a state lies very close to a two-hadron threshold, its wavefunction outside the range of the force is fixed by the binding energy alone:

\[u(r) \to \sqrt{2\kappa}\,e^{-\kappa r},\qquad \kappa = \sqrt{2\mu B},\qquad \langle r^2\rangle^{1/2} \to \frac{1}{\kappa\sqrt2},\qquad a \to \frac{1}{\kappa}\]

This is universality. For X(3872), within about 0.1 MeV of D⁰D̄*⁰, 1/κ exceeds 10 fm and could be 20 fm or more. A hadron the size of a nucleus, made of two charmed mesons. The deuteron is the prototype, with 1/κ = 4.3 fm.

Weinberg (1965) showed how to tell a molecule from a compact state using low-energy scattering. If Z is the probability of a compact ("elementary") component,

\[a = \frac{2(1 - Z)}{2 - Z}\,\frac{1}{\kappa},\qquad r_0 = -\frac{Z}{1 - Z}\,\frac{1}{\kappa}\qquad (+\ \text{range corrections})\]

A pure molecule (Z = 0) has a ≈ 1/κ and a small positive effective range. A compact state has a large negative r₀. Range corrections make the leading-order formula unreliable when κ times the range is not small; the lab shows the improved estimator \(\bar X = 1/\sqrt{1 + |2r_0/a|}\) (Matuschek et al. 2021). It gives about 0.8 for the deuteron.

11. Exotic Atoms

A negative hadron (π⁻, K⁻, p̄) captured by a proton forms a hydrogen-like atom with reduced mass μ instead of me, smaller by the ratio of the masses. Pionic hydrogen has a Bohr radius \(\hbar c/(\mu\alpha) = 222\) fm and 1s binding \(\mu\alpha^2/2 = 3.235\) keV. The strong interaction acts only at r ≲ 1 fm, so it shifts and broadens the levels without changing their character. To leading order the shift is fixed by the hadron–hadron scattering length (Deser, Goldberger, Baumann and Thirring 1954; Trueman 1961):

\[\varepsilon_{1s} - \frac{i\Gamma_{1s}}{2} = 2\alpha^3\mu^2a\,\big[1 + 2\alpha\mu a(\ln\alpha - 1)\big]\]

(nuclear sign convention for a; meson–baryon papers often use the opposite sign.) Exotic atoms therefore measure scattering lengths at exactly zero energy, which no scattering experiment can reach. Pionic hydrogen gives a(π⁻p) to 0.1 %, a precision test of chiral perturbation theory.

The kaonic-hydrogen puzzle. The K̄N interaction is strongly attractive, yet the 1s level of kaonic hydrogen is pushed up (SIDDHARTA 2011: 283 eV, Γ = 541 eV). The resolution is Λ(1405), a K̄N bound state 27 MeV below threshold. With a bound state, the scattering length is positive in the nuclear convention, like a repulsive one. By Levinson's theorem the atomic 1s wavefunction must have an extra node inside the strong-interaction region, and the level moves up. Older experiments that reported an attractive shift were wrong. The molecules tab reproduces the sign with a single-channel potential that binds Λ(1405).

12. Resonances: Breit–Wigner Forms

Near a resonance the amplitude for a → R → b is dominated by the propagator of R. In order of refinement:

\[\text{non-relativistic: } T = \frac{\Gamma/2}{M - E - i\Gamma/2};\qquad \text{relativistic: } T = \frac{M\Gamma}{M^2 - s - iM\Gamma}\]

Real widths depend on energy. For a decay into two particles in a partial wave l, the phase space and the centrifugal barrier give

\[\Gamma(s) = \sum_i \Gamma_i\Big(\frac{q_i}{q_{i0}}\Big)^{2l+1}\frac{M}{\sqrt s}\,\frac{F_l^2(q_iR)}{F_l^2(q_{i0}R)}\]

with Blatt–Weisskopf form factors \(F_1^2 = 2z/(1 + z)\), \(F_2^2 = 13z^2/[(z - 3)^2 + 9z]\), \(z = (qR)^2\), which stop the \(q^{2l+1}\) growth beyond \(qR \approx 1\). This makes the ρ line shape asymmetric and the D*⁺ width tiny: its decay momentum is only 39 MeV/c.

Flatté. When a second channel opens near the resonance, as K K̄ does for f₀(980) at 991 MeV, its momentum q₂ is imaginary below threshold. The term \(-iMg_2\rho_2\) then becomes real and shifts the peak. The result is a cusp or dip in ππ exactly at the K K̄ threshold, which a constant-width form cannot describe.

The phase of the amplitude rises by 180° through the resonance and passes 90° at M. The Wigner time delay \(2\hbar\,d\delta/dE\) peaks at \(2\hbar/\Gamma = 2\tau\): the projectile lingers. In an elastic channel the amplitude traces a circle of diameter 1 in the Argand plane. With other channels open it traces a circle of diameter equal to the branching ratio.

13. Poles, Sheets and the Definition of Mass

q(s) has a square-root branch point at each threshold, so the amplitude lives on several Riemann sheets. On the physical sheet Im q > 0, and a pole there below threshold is a bound state. Resonance poles sit on the sheet reached by continuing downwards through the cut (Im q < 0), at

\[s_p = m_p^2 - i\,m_p\Gamma_p\qquad \big(\text{or } \sqrt{s_p} = m - i\Gamma/2\big)\]

The pole position is process independent and field-theoretically well defined. The Breit–Wigner M and Γ depend on the parametrisation. For broad resonances the difference is large: the Δ(1232) Breit–Wigner mass is 1232 MeV, but its pole is at 1210 − i50 MeV. Even for the Z it is measurable: with \(\Gamma(s) = \Gamma s/M^2\) the pole is exactly at

\[\bar m = \frac{M}{\sqrt{1 + \Gamma^2/M^2}},\qquad \bar\Gamma = \frac{\Gamma}{\sqrt{1 + \Gamma^2/M^2}},\qquad M - \bar m \approx \frac{\Gamma^2}{2M} = 34\ \text{MeV}\]

34 MeV is fifteen times the LEP uncertainty on MZ, so the definition matters. The lab finds poles by Newton iteration on the analytically continued denominator.

14. Partial Waves, Unitarity and Isospin: πN

For a spin-0 pion on a spin-½ nucleon each partial wave l splits into j = l ± ½, labelled L2I,2J (S₁₁, S₃₁, P₁₁, P₃₁, P₁₃, P₃₃). Below the 2π threshold each is elastic, \(S = e^{2i\delta}\). Unitarity then bounds the cross section:

\[\sigma_{lj} \le \frac{4\pi}{q^2}\,\frac{2j + 1}{2},\qquad \text{so } \sigma(P_{33}) \le \frac{8\pi}{q^2} \approx 190\ \text{mb at the }\Delta\]

The measured π⁺p peak, about 200 mb at Tπ = 190 MeV, saturates this bound. The amplitude is a 2 × 2 matrix in spin space, \(f(\theta) + i\,g(\theta)\,\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\). The spin-flip part g produces the polarization.

Isospin. πN states have I = ½ or 3/2. π⁺p is pure I = 3/2, while π⁻p is a mixture. Clebsch–Gordan coefficients give

\[T(\pi^+p) = T_{3/2},\qquad T(\pi^-p \to \pi^-p) = \frac{T_{3/2} + 2T_{1/2}}{3},\qquad T(\pi^-p \to \pi^0n) = \frac{\sqrt2\,(T_{3/2} - T_{1/2})}{3}\]

If only T3/2 is resonant, the three cross sections are in the ratio 9 : 1 : 2. This was the first proof (Fermi and collaborators, 1952) that the Δ has isospin 3/2. A pure P₃₃ resonance also gives the angular distribution \(1 + 3\cos^2\theta\).

15. e⁺e⁻ Annihilation: R, the Z and AFB

e⁺e⁻ → μ⁺μ⁻ through a photon is the simplest process in QED:

\[\sigma_{\text{QED}} = \frac{4\pi\alpha^2}{3s} = \frac{86.8\ \text{nb}}{s\,[\text{GeV}^2]}\]

Above a few GeV the hadronic cross section is that of free quark pairs, multiplied by the number of colours:

\[R = \frac{\sigma(e^+e^- \to \text{hadrons})}{\sigma_{\text{QED}}} = N_c\sum_q e_q^2\Big(1 + \frac{\alpha_s}{\pi} + \dots\Big) = 2,\ \tfrac{10}{3},\ \tfrac{11}{3}\quad \text{for } u\,d\,s,\ +c,\ +b\]

The steps at the charm and bottom thresholds, and the factor 3, are direct evidence for fractional quark charges and colour. On top of the continuum sit the narrow vector mesons. The J/ψ peak cross section would be tens of μb, but the beam-energy spread (≈ 1 MeV) dilutes it to a few μb.

At higher energy the Z contributes. For e⁺e⁻ → ff̄ with \(v_f = T_3 - 2Q_f\sin^2\theta_W\) and \(a_f = T_3\):

\[\sigma = \frac{4\pi\alpha^2}{3s}\,N_c\Big[Q_f^2 - 2Q_fv_ev_f\,\mathrm{Re}\,\chi + (v_e^2 + a_e^2)(v_f^2 + a_f^2)\,|\chi|^2\Big],\qquad \chi = \frac{s}{4\sin^2\theta_W\cos^2\theta_W\,(s - M_Z^2 + is\Gamma_Z/M_Z)}\]

The interference term gives a forward–backward asymmetry already at PETRA energies (AFB(μμ) ≈ −0.1 at 35 GeV), the first sign of electroweak interference in e⁺e⁻ annihilation. At the peak, \(\sigma^0_{\text{had}} = 12\pi\Gamma_{ee}\Gamma_{\text{had}}/(M^2\Gamma^2) = 41.5\) nb. Since \(\Gamma_Z = \Gamma_{\text{had}} + 3\Gamma_{\ell\ell} + N_\nu\Gamma_{\nu\nu}\), the line shape counts neutrinos: LEP found Nν = 2.984 ± 0.008, revised to 2.996 ± 0.007 in 2020 after a correction to the luminosity.

⟨L·S⟩ = −2, −1, 1 and ⟨S₁₂⟩ = −4, 2, −2/5 for J = 0, 1, 2. M₂ − M₁ = 2a − 12c/5 = 45.50 MeV, M₁ − M₀ = a + 6c = 95.96 MeV, so c = 10.2 MeV and a = 34.9 MeV. The centre of gravity (M₀ + 3M₁ + 5M₂)/9 = 3525.3 MeV coincides with hc at 3525.4 MeV.
4. Compute the peak cross section of e⁺e⁻ → Z → hadrons (M = 91.1876 GeV, Bee = 3.363 %, Bhad = 69.91 %).
σ = (12π/M²)BeeBhad = (37.70/8315.2 GeV²) × 0.02351 = 1.066 × 10⁻⁴ GeV⁻² = 41.5 nb (× 0.3894 mb GeV²). Initial-state radiation reduces the observed peak to about 30 nb.
5. Find the Bohr radius and 1s binding energy of pionic hydrogen (μ = 121.50 MeV).
aB = ħc/(μα) = 197.327 × 137.036/121.50 fm = 222.6 fm. E = μα²/2 = 121.50 MeV/(2 × 18 779) = 3.235 keV. The strong shift, 7.1 eV, is 0.2 % of the binding.
6. X(3872) is bound by B = 40 keV relative to D⁰D̄*⁰ (μ = 966.6 MeV). Estimate its size.
κ = √(2 × 966.6 × 0.040)/197.327 = 8.79/197.327 = 0.0446 fm⁻¹, so 1/κ = 22 fm and ⟨r²⟩½ ≈ 1/(κ√2) = 16 fm: larger than a lead nucleus.
7. What is the unitarity limit for σ(π⁺p) at √s = 1232 MeV?
q = 227.2 MeV/c (from the break-up formula). σmax = (4π/q²)(2J + 1)/2 = 8π(ħc/q)² = 8π(0.8685 fm)² = 18.96 fm² = 190 mb. The measured ~200 mb includes a small non-resonant part.
8. From ΓZ = 2495.5 MeV, Γhad = 1744.4 MeV, Γℓℓ = 83.98 MeV and Γνν(SM) = 166.9 MeV, count the neutrinos.
Γinv = 2495.5 − 1744.4 − 3 × 83.98 = 499.2 MeV; Nν = 499.2/166.9 = 2.99. A fourth light neutrino would widen the Z by 167 MeV and lower the hadronic peak by about 13 %.
9. Why is the kaonic-hydrogen 1s level pushed up although the K̄N force is attractive?
The attraction is strong enough to bind Λ(1405). A potential with one bound state has a positive scattering length (nuclear convention) at threshold, and Deser–Trueman gives a positive (upward) shift. Equivalently, the atomic 1s wavefunction must be orthogonal to the bound Λ(1405) and acquires a node, like a 2s state in a potential that already holds a 1s.

20. Limits of the Models

Quarkonium. Non-relativistic: v²/c² ≈ 0.25 for charmonium, so order-v² corrections are absorbed into effective parameters (the quark masses are not the MS̄ masses). Spin-dependent forces are first order; the Quark models tab adds exact ³S₁–³D₁ and ¹P₁–³P₁ mixing, a relativistic kinetic energy and an unquenched treatment of a single state (Coupled channels tab), but the main tab has no coupled channels. The light-quark sector misses chiral symmetry (the pion is far too heavy). Baryons use the ½ rule and a contact hyperfine term only; triply heavy baryons come out 0.1–0.6 GeV below lattice QCD. Hybrids use a model Πu potential, not lattice data. Annihilation widths carry large QCD corrections.

Molecules and exotic atoms. Single channel, s wave, local energy-independent potentials. The Coupled channels tab treats two or three channels at leading order (contact EFT and chiral unitary amplitudes, no left-hand cuts, no one-pion exchange, no three-body DD̄π effects), and some (X(3872), Tcc) have widths from the decay of a constituent. The absorptive W only mimics inelastic channels. Electromagnetic corrections in exotic atoms (vacuum polarisation, finite size) are not included; the shift is defined relative to the point-Coulomb level.

Resonances. Breit–Wigner forms are parametrisations, not theory. For overlapping resonances or strong thresholds, K-matrix or dispersive methods are required, and pole positions depend on how the amplitude is continued.

Scattering. The πN model is a low-energy parametrisation (Tπ ≲ 400 MeV): no inelasticity, no higher resonances, no D waves. The e⁺e⁻ cross sections are Born level with a fixed α: initial-state radiation optional (leading-log, Kuraev–Fadin, not applied to the narrow vector mesons), no running coupling, no hadronic resonance structure below 2 GeV except ρ, ω, φ as Breit–Wigners.

New tools. The Lüscher formula is used for S waves in the rest frame only (no partial-wave mixing, no moving frames, no three-body quantisation). The triangle loop is non-relativistic. The Dalitz model is an isobar sum with constant widths. The deuteron potential is OPEP plus a Gaussian, fitted to two numbers, not a high-precision NN force. W and Higgs line shapes are at parton level, without parton distributions, recoil or detector simulation.

21. Quark Models of Light and Heavy Hadrons

21.1 Relativistic kinetic energy

In light mesons the quarks move at relativistic speeds (\(\langle p^2\rangle \sim m^2\)), so \(m + p^2/2m\) fails. The spinless Salpeter (or "relativized") Hamiltonian keeps the square roots:

\[H = \sqrt{p^2 + m_1^2} + \sqrt{p^2 + m_2^2} - \frac{4\alpha_s}{3}\,\frac{\operatorname{erf}(r/r_s)}{r} + \sigma r + C\]

The square root of an operator is evaluated exactly in a discrete basis: on a sinc-DVR radial grid the matrix of \(p^2 = -d^2/dr^2 + l(l + 1)/r^2\) is diagonalised once, \(P = W\Lambda W^{\mathsf T}\), and \(T = W\,\mathrm{diag}\big(\sqrt{\lambda + m_1^2} + \sqrt{\lambda + m_2^2}\big)\,W^{\mathsf T}\). The Coulomb term must be smeared: with the relativistic kinetic energy a bare \(-\kappa/r\) with \(\kappa > 2/\pi\) has no ground state (Herbst 1977). One potential and one set of constituent masses then describe the mesons from the ρ to the Υ to about 30 MeV (Godfrey and Isgur 1985 refine the same idea). The pion stays far too heavy: it is the pseudo-Goldstone boson of spontaneously broken chiral symmetry, which no potential model contains.

21.2 Baryons

Three quarks in a colour singlet: each pair is in a colour \(\bar 3\) with half the colour factor of a quark–antiquark singlet, so \(V_{ij} = \frac12 V_{q\bar q}(r_{ij})\) (the "½ rule"). The spatial ground state is solved with explicitly correlated Gaussians in Jacobi coordinates (§ 23 of the solid-state module uses the same code), and the colour-magnetic hyperfine term

\[H_{hf} = \sum_{i

explains the N–Δ and Λ–Σ splittings (De Rújula, Georgi and Glashow 1975). The spin–flavour content enters only through \(\langle\boldsymbol\sigma_i\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_j\rangle\) of each pair.

21.3 Hybrids in the Born–Oppenheimer picture

Heavy quarks move slowly compared with the gluon field, which adjusts to their separation like the electrons of a molecule. Its excited states \(\Lambda_\eta^\epsilon\) (here \(\Pi_u\), projection Λ = 1 of the gluonic angular momentum) give new potentials. The lab uses

\[V_{\Pi_u}(r) = \frac{\alpha_s}{6r} + \sqrt{\sigma^2r^2 + 2\pi\sigma\,\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{12}\big)} + C,\qquad \Big[-\frac{\nabla^2}{2\mu} + \frac{L(L + 1) - 2\Lambda^2 + J_g(J_g + 1)}{2\mu r^2} + V_{\Pi_u}\Big]\psi = E\psi\]

(colour-octet repulsion at short distance, the first transverse excitation of a Nambu–Goto string at long distance). The lowest multiplet (L = 1) contains \(J^{PC} = 1^{-+}\), which a quark–antiquark pair cannot have: an exotic hybrid, predicted near 4.2–4.4 GeV for charmonium by lattice QCD (Juge, Kuti and Morningstar 2003; Hadron Spectrum Collaboration 2012).

21.4 Mixing beyond first order

The tensor force mixes \(^3S_1\) and \(^3D_1\) (\(\langle{}^3S_1|S_{12}|{}^3D_1\rangle = \sqrt8\)); the lab diagonalises it in the basis of the computed radial states. For unequal masses the spin–orbit force has an antisymmetric part,

\[V = \Big(\frac{1}{2m_1^2} + \frac{1}{2m_2^2}\Big)f(r)\,\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S + \Big(\frac{1}{2m_1^2} - \frac{1}{2m_2^2}\Big)f(r)\,\mathbf L\cdot(\mathbf S_1 - \mathbf S_2),\qquad f = \frac{2\alpha_s}{r^3} - \frac{\sigma}{2r}\]

which mixes \(^1P_1\) and \(^3P_1\) (\(\langle{}^1P_1|\mathbf L\cdot(\mathbf S_1 - \mathbf S_2)|{}^3P_1\rangle = \sqrt2\)). When one quark becomes infinitely heavy the light-quark angular momentum \(j = L + S_q\) is conserved and the mixing angle tends to \(\arctan(1/\sqrt2) = 35.3^\circ\) (or 54.7°, depending on the sign convention): heavy-quark symmetry.

21.5 Flavour independence

The global fit uses one α_s, σ and C for charmonium and bottomonium with two quark masses. The remaining pulls measure what a static, flavour-independent potential with first-order spin forces misses: the running of α_s, relativistic corrections and the coupling to open-flavour channels (§ 22).

22. Coupled Channels, Poles and Compositeness

22.1 Chiral unitary amplitudes

Chiral perturbation theory fixes the low-energy meson–meson and meson–baryon interactions, but a perturbative amplitude cannot have poles. Unitarisation in the on-shell factorised form

\[T = (1 - VG)^{-1}V,\qquad S = 1 - 2i\sqrt\rho\,T\sqrt\rho,\quad \rho = -\operatorname{Im}G\]

sums the s-channel loops. V is the lowest-order chiral amplitude (Weinberg–Tomozawa for K̄N–πΣ: \(V_{ij} = -C_{ij}(2\sqrt s - M_i - M_j)/4f^2\) times spinor factors) and G the two-particle loop, regularised dimensionally or with a cutoff. The second Riemann sheet is reached by \(G_{II} = G_I - 2i\rho\). Two results follow almost without parameters:

  • In ππ–KK̄ (I = 0) the σ = f₀(500) appears as a pole near 470 − 190i MeV, so far from the real axis that no Breit–Wigner peak is seen, and the f₀(980) as a KK̄ quasi-bound state (Oller and Oset 1997).
  • In K̄N–πΣ there are two poles, near 1430 − 15i and 1390 − 70i MeV: the Λ(1405) is a two-pole structure, coupling mostly to K̄N and πΣ respectively (Oller and Meißner 2001; Jido et al. 2003). The PDG now lists the lower pole as Λ(1380).

22.2 Near-threshold molecules and compositeness

For χ_c1(3872) and T_cc⁺ the relevant channels are the neutral and charged meson pairs, a few MeV apart. In leading-order pionless EFT

\[G_i(E) = -\frac{\mu_i}{2\pi}\Big(\frac{2\Lambda}{\pi} + ik_i\Big),\qquad k_i = \sqrt{2\mu_i(E - \Delta_i + i\Gamma_i/2)},\qquad X_i = -g_i^2\,\frac{dG_i}{dE}\Big|_{E_B}\]

with \(g_ig_j\) the residue of \(T_{ij}\) at the pole. For an energy-independent interaction \(\sum_i X_i = 1\) (Hyodo 2013): the state is entirely composite, and a shallow state lives mostly in the nearer channel. The widths of the D* make the bound state quasi-bound.

22.3 Unquenched quarkonium

A bare \(q\bar q\) state at \(E_0\) coupled to meson pairs has the propagator \([E - E_0 - \Sigma(E)]^{-1}\) with

\[\Sigma(E) = \sum_c\int_0^\infty \frac{2\mu_c\,f_c(q)}{q_c^2 - q^2}\,dq,\qquad f_c = g^2\gamma_c\,q^{2L+2}e^{-2q^2/\beta^2},\qquad q_c^2 = 2\mu_c(E - W_c)\]

The integral is taken on the rotated contour \(q = t\,e^{-i\varphi}\): this gives the physical value on the real axis and the second-sheet continuation below it, so poles are found directly. The loops shift ψ(3770) by about 10 MeV and give its 27 MeV width; for an S-wave coupling below threshold a χ_c1(2P) is pulled down to the D D̄* threshold and acquires a large meson–meson component \(1 - Z\), \(Z = [1 - \Sigma'(E_B)]^{-1}\) (Eichten et al. 1978; Kalashnikova 2005). The pole trajectory as the coupling grows shows how a bare state turns into a threshold-dominated one.

22.4 K-matrix and production

\[T = (1 - iK\rho)^{-1}K,\quad K_{ij} = \sum_\alpha\frac{g_{\alpha i}g_{\alpha j}}{m_\alpha^2 - s} + f_{ij},\qquad F = (1 - iK\rho)^{-1}P,\quad P_j = \sum_\alpha\frac{\beta_\alpha g_{\alpha j}}{m_\alpha^2 - s}\]

For real K the scattering amplitude is unitary; the P-vector (Aitchison 1972) gives production amplitudes with the same poles and final-state unitarity. The K-matrix poles \(m_\alpha\) are not the physical poles: the f₀(980) appears as a narrow dip in ππ at the KK̄ threshold.

23. Finite Volume, Triangle Singularities and Dalitz Plots

23.1 Lüscher's formula

Lattice QCD computes energies in a periodic box, not amplitudes. For two particles in an S wave below inelastic thresholds the levels obey

\[k\cot\delta_0(k) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi\,L}\,Z_{00}(1; q^2),\qquad q = \frac{kL}{2\pi},\qquad Z_{00}(1; q^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\sum_{\mathbf n\in\mathbb Z^3}\frac{1}{\mathbf n^2 - q^2}\]

(Lüscher 1986, 1991). The sum is defined by analytic continuation; the lab splits it with a heat kernel, \(\sqrt{4\pi}Z_{00} = \sum_{\mathbf n} e^{-(\mathbf n^2 - q^2)}/(\mathbf n^2 - q^2) + \pi^{3/2}\big[\int_0^1(e^{tq^2} - 1)t^{-3/2}dt - 2\big] + \sum_{\mathbf m\neq0}\int_0^1(\pi/t)^{3/2}e^{tq^2 - \pi^2\mathbf m^2/t}dt\), whose regular part at \(q^2 = 0\) is −8.91363. For small |a|/L the lowest level shifts by \(\Delta E = (2\pi a/\mu L^3)[1 + 2.8373\,a/L + 6.3752\,a^2/L^2]\) (nuclear sign). Bound states approach their infinite-volume energy exponentially, \(\propto e^{-\kappa L}\). Inverting the formula level by level gives the phase shift at the box energies, the standard route to resonances in lattice QCD.

23.2 Triangle singularities

In a decay \(A \to 1 + 2\), \(2 \to 3 + B\), \(1 + 3 \to C\), the triangle loop

\[I(E) = \int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{1}{\big(E - m_1 - m_2 - \frac{q^2}{2\mu_{12}} + \frac{i\Gamma_2}{2}\big)\big(E - E_B - m_1 - m_3 - \frac{q^2}{2m_1} - \frac{(\mathbf q + \mathbf p_B)^2}{2m_3}\big)}\]

has a logarithmic singularity when all three intermediate particles can be on shell and move collinearly (Landau 1959; Coleman and Norton 1965): a classical rescattering. It produces a peak with a resonance-like phase motion although no state exists. The a₁(1420) seen by COMPASS in f₀(980)π is most likely this effect in a₁(1260) → K*K̄ → (Kπ)K̄ → f₀π (Mikhasenko, Ketzer and Sarantsev 2015). The width of the intermediate K* smooths the logarithm.

23.3 Dalitz plots

A three-body decay at rest has two independent invariants; the density in the \((m^2_{12}, m^2_{13})\) plane is flat for pure phase space and shows each resonance as a band. In the isobar model \(A = \sum_r c_r\,\mathrm{BW}_r(m^2)\,Z_r + c_{NR}\), symmetrised over identical particles; the Zemach factor of a P wave has a node along its band. Fit fractions \(\int|c_r\mathrm{BW}_rZ_r|^2/\int|A|^2\) need not add up to 100 %: the rest is interference.

24. Nucleons and Hadronic Atoms

24.1 The deuteron and the tensor force

One-pion exchange contains a tensor part,

\[V_\pi = \frac{f^2}{4\pi}\,\frac{m_\pi}{3}\,(\boldsymbol\tau_1\cdot\boldsymbol\tau_2)\Big[\boldsymbol\sigma_1\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_2\,Y(x) + S_{12}\Big(1 + \frac3x + \frac{3}{x^2}\Big)Y(x)\Big],\qquad Y = \frac{e^{-x}}{x},\ x = m_\pi r\]

which couples \(^3S_1\) and \(^3D_1\) (\(S_{12}\) = [[0, √8], [√8, −2]]). The lab regulates it at short distance, adds a Gaussian central term fitted to the binding and solves the coupled equations on a grid (block tridiagonal, inverse iteration). The D-state admixture (about 5 %) gives the quadrupole moment \(Q = \frac{\sqrt2}{10}\int r^2uw\,dr - \frac{1}{20}\int r^2w^2\,dr = 0.286\ \mathrm{fm}^2\) and the asymptotic ratio \(\eta = A_D/A_S \approx 0.026\): the deuteron is not spherical.

24.2 The ¹S₀ virtual state

With \(a = -23.74\) fm and \(r_0 = 2.77\) fm the effective-range amplitude has a pole at \(k = -i\kappa\), \(\kappa = [1 - \sqrt{1 - 2r_0/a}]/r_0 \approx -0.040\ \mathrm{fm}^{-1}\): a virtual state 66 keV "below" threshold on the second sheet, which makes the low-energy np cross section huge.

24.3 Hadronic atoms beyond 1s

The complex eigenvalue of \(-\nabla^2/2\mu - Z_1Z_2\alpha/r - (V_0 + iW)e^{-(r/b)^2}\) is found by shooting with complex energy (outward and inward log-grid Numerov solutions matched). The 1s result agrees with Deser–Trueman (§ 11) for small shifts and goes beyond it for large ones; the 2p and 3d shifts are smaller by powers of \(r_{\text{strong}}/a_B\). Antiprotonic hydrogen and kaonic deuterium have large 1s widths, so the Kα X-ray line is broad and weak; SIDDHARTA-2 is measuring kaonic deuterium.

25. Electroweak Line Shapes

The W mass. In W → ℓν the neutrino escapes, so only transverse quantities are measured. For a W at rest, \(m_T = 2p_T^\ell\) and \(dN/dm_T \propto m_T/\sqrt{M_W^2 - m_T^2}\): a Jacobian edge at \(M_W\), smeared by \(\Gamma_W\), the W recoil and the detector. Template fits of this edge give M_W to about 10 MeV.

The Higgs width. \(\Gamma_H \approx 4\) MeV is far below the detector resolution. In H → ZZ* → 4ℓ the running width \(\Gamma_{H\to ZZ^*}(m)\) grows steeply up to the 2M_Z threshold, so the off-shell tail above 2M_Z carries a sizeable fraction of the events; its ratio to the on-shell peak (with the continuum interference) measures Γ_H (Caola and Melnikov 2013; Kauer and Passarino 2012).

Initial-state radiation. Electrons radiate before they annihilate: \(\sigma_{obs}(s) = \int_0^{x_{max}}dx\,G(x)\,\sigma(s(1 - x))\), \(G(x) = \beta x^{\beta-1}(1 + \tfrac34\beta) - \beta(1 - \tfrac x2)\), \(\beta = \frac{2\alpha}{\pi}[\ln(s/m_e^2) - 1]\) (Kuraev and Fadin 1985). The \(x^{\beta-1}\) singularity is integrated exactly by \(x = u^{1/\beta}\). At the Z the peak drops by about 26 % and moves up by about 0.1 GeV, with a radiative tail above it.

Molecules and exotic atoms

Two hadrons with masses m₁, m₂ in an s wave, bound by a short-range potential (Gaussian, Yukawa, exponential or square) and, for exotic atoms, the Coulomb attraction (Z₁Z₂ = −1). Fit sets V₀ from a binding energy or a scattering length. The threshold panel gives the binding momentum κ and size 1/κ, the rms radius, the probability of finding the pair outside the range of the force, the scattering length (from the zero-energy solution) and effective range (from phase shifts), and the compositeness X̄ = 1/√(1 + |2r/a|). For exotic atoms it compares the numerical level shift of the 1s state with the Deser–Trueman formula and gives the width from the absorptive part W.

03

Resonances

Choose a resonance and a line shape. The mass-dependent width uses the break-up momentum q, the angular-momentum barrier q2l+1 and Blatt–Weisskopf form factors with radius R; the Flatté form treats a resonance near a second threshold (f₀(980) and KK̄). The results give the S-matrix pole on the unphysical sheet (pole mass and width), the difference from the Breit–Wigner parameters, the lifetime τ = ħ/Γ and cτ, the break-up momenta and partial widths, and the peak cross section allowed by unitarity. Plots: line shapes, phase shift and time delay, Argand diagram, and Γ(√s).

04

Scattering

πN: partial waves S₁₁, S₃₁ (scattering lengths), P₁₁, P₁₃, P₃₁ (scattering volumes) and the Δ(1232) in P₃₃, combined with isospin Clebsch–Gordan coefficients into π⁺p, π⁻p elastic and π⁻p → π⁰n; cross sections against Tπ, angular distributions, polarization, phase shifts and Argand diagrams. e⁺e⁻: the Born cross section for e⁺e⁻ → ff̄ with photon and Z exchange and their interference, AFB, R with the quark thresholds and the narrow ψ and Υ states (with a beam-energy spread), and the Z line shape with its partial widths.

05

Quark models

Mesons: one potential (smeared Coulomb plus linear) and constituent masses for light, heavy–light and heavy mesons, solved with the relativistic kinetic energy √(p² + m²) and, with the same parameters, its non-relativistic limit; the table shows both and the rms deviation from the PDG. Baryons: three quarks with the ½ rule, solved with correlated Gaussians, plus the colour-magnetic hyperfine term (N, Δ, Λ, Σ, Ξ, Ω, Ωccc, Ωbbb). Hybrids: the Πu gluonic potential and the lowest multiplets, including the exotic 1⁻⁺. Mixing: exact ³S₁–³D₁ and ¹P₁–³P₁ mixing with the mixing angle against the heavy-quark mass (it tends to 35.3°). Global fit: one αs, σ, C for cc̄ and bb̄ with pulls and χ².

06

Coupled channels

Chiral unitary: K̄N–πΣ (two poles of the Λ(1405)) and ππ–KK̄ (σ and f₀(980)); poles on the second sheet, couplings, |T|² per channel; the slider scales the interaction and draws the pole trajectory. Contact EFT: X(3872) or Tcc with charged and neutral channels and D* widths; V₀ is fitted to the binding, and the compositeness Xi of each channel is given (it sums to 1). Unquenched: a bare cc̄ state dressed by meson loops; pole, Z factor and spectral function for ψ(3770), χc1(2P) → X(3872) and ψ(4040). K-matrix: a three-channel K-matrix with a P-vector for production, its poles on the chosen sheets.

07

Lattice and kinematics

Lüscher: finite-volume levels against the box size L for an effective-range amplitude, a bound state or a resonance, compared with the asymptotic expansion; Triangle: the triangle loop and where the singularity sits for a₁(1420) and η(1405) → f₀(980)π; Dalitz: a three-body Dalitz plot from an isobar model, with projections and fit fractions.

08

Nucleons, atoms, electroweak

Deuteron: OPEP with a regulator and a Gaussian, coupled ³S₁–³D₁, giving B, PD, Q, the rms radius and η; ¹S₀: the virtual state and phase shift; Hadronic atoms: complex 1s, 2p and 3d levels for πH, K⁻p, p̄p and K⁻d with the Kα line shape; W: the transverse-mass Jacobian peak; Higgs: the H → ZZ* line shape and its off-shell fraction for a chosen ΓH. In the Scattering tab the e⁺e⁻ cross sections can include initial-state radiation.

09

Tools

Pick a tab, an input and an output; Sweep plots the output against the input. Fit adjusts up to three inputs to pasted x, y (σ) data. Examples load synthetic data generated by the model. Under each plot: CSV, PNG and copy model/results.

10

Validation

Exact Coulomb and Airy spectra, the S-wave identity |R(0)|² = 2μ⟨V′⟩, a second (DVR) solver, Deser–Trueman against the numerical level shift, scattering length from two methods, the Z pole–mass shift, Z partial widths and peak cross section, unitarity and the optical theorem in πN, the Z₀₀ zeta function and the Lüscher expansion, S-matrix unitarity of the coupled-channel amplitudes, compositeness sums, the heavy-quark mixing limit, the deuteron and pionic hydrogen, the ISR peak shift, and comparisons with measured masses, widths and cross sections.
