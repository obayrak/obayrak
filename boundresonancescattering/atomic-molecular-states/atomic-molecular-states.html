Bound, Quasi-bound and Scattering States — Theory

One equation, the radial Schrödinger equation, describes an electron in an atom, two nuclei vibrating in a molecule, and two atoms colliding. Its solutions fall into three classes: bound states at discrete negative energies, scattering states at every positive energy, and in between quasi-bound states (resonances), which are trapped for a finite time behind a barrier. This chapter treats all three for atomic and molecular systems and explains the numerical methods used in this lab.

1. The Radial Equation

For two particles interacting through a central potential V(r), separating the centre-of-mass motion leaves a single particle of reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂). Writing ψ = u(r)Ylm(θ, φ)/r gives

−(ħ²/2μ) u″ + [V(r) + ħ²l(l+1)/(2μr²)] u = E u, u(0) = 0

The bracket is the effective potential. Its centrifugal term keeps particles with l > 0 away from the origin and, added to an attractive well, can form a barrier. The lab works in atomic units (ħ = mₑ = e = 4πε₀ = 1): lengths in bohr a₀ = 0.529177 Å, energies in hartree Eₕ = 27.2114 eV = 219 474.6 cm⁻¹ = 315 775 K, and μ in electron masses (1 u = 1822.888 mₑ).

2. Hydrogen-like Atoms

For V = −Z/r the levels depend only on n = nr + l + 1:

En = −μZ²/(2n²) Eₕ, ⟨r⟩nl = [3n² − l(l+1)]/(2μZ) a₀

The degeneracy in l is special to the 1/r potential (a hidden SO(4) symmetry, conservation of the Runge–Lenz vector). Any deviation from 1/r, such as screening or a finite core, lifts it. The reduced mass matters at the 10⁻⁴ level for hydrogen (the Rydberg constant for hydrogen is RH = 109 677.6 cm⁻¹ rather than R∞ = 109 737.3 cm⁻¹). For exotic atoms it is decisive. Positronium (μ = mₑ/2) has half the binding energy and twice the size of hydrogen. Muonic hydrogen (μ ≈ 186 mₑ) is 186 times smaller, so its levels are sensitive to the proton's charge radius, which is how the "proton radius puzzle" arose in 2010.

Why the levels are exact here. The lab solves the radial equation numerically for every potential, including the Coulomb one, so the exact formula is a test of the solver. On the logarithmic grid the error is about 10⁻¹² for the lowest levels.

3. Screened Coulomb Potentials

In a plasma, free charges screen a nucleus over the Debye length λD = √(ε₀kBT/(nee²)), giving the Debye–Hückel (Yukawa) potential V = −Z e−r/λD/r. Unlike the pure Coulomb potential it supports only a finite number of bound states. As λD shrinks, levels move up and disappear into the continuum one by one: the ionization potential is lowered (pressure ionization). For l = 0 the last bound state disappears at λc = 0.83990 a₀/(Zμ) (Rogers, Graboske and Harwood, 1970). Near λc the state becomes arbitrarily large and weakly bound, and its binding energy vanishes quadratically in (λ − λc).

For l > 0 the screened potential plus the centrifugal term has a barrier, so a level that leaves the bound spectrum first becomes a narrow shape resonance just above threshold. This is a clean example of a bound state turning into a quasi-bound one (Quasi-bound tab).

4. Alkali Atoms and Quantum Defects

The valence electron of an alkali atom sees the nuclear charge Z screened by the Z − 1 core electrons: −Z/r near the nucleus and −1/r outside. A one-parameter model V = −[1 + (Z − 1)e−r/d]/r captures this (the presets fit d to the ionization energy). Its levels obey the Rydberg formula

Enl = −1/[2(n − δl)²] Eₕ

with a quantum defect δl that is almost independent of n. Penetrating low-l orbits dive into the core, feel the larger charge and are bound more strongly (large δ), while high-l orbits stay outside (δ ≈ 0). For sodium, δs ≈ 1.35, δp ≈ 0.86 and δd ≈ 0.015, which is why the Na D lines (3p → 3s) lie at 589 nm instead of the hydrogenic position.

The model's deepest levels (1s, 2s, 2p for Na) are core orbitals. In the real atom they are occupied, so the Pauli principle makes the valence electron start at n = 3. The node count of the valence wavefunction reflects this: the 3s orbital has two radial nodes.

Seaton's theorem. Quantum-defect theory connects bound and continuum states. Just above threshold, an electron scattering from the ion core has a short-range phase shift (relative to Coulomb functions) δl(E → 0⁺) = π μl, where μl is the quantum defect extrapolated to n → ∞. The Rydberg series and the low-energy scattering are one analytic function of energy. Compare the analysis panel of the Bound-states tab with the Scattering tab for the same atom.

5. Diatomic Molecules

In the Born–Oppenheimer approximation the electrons adjust instantly to the nuclear separation r, providing a potential energy curve V(r) in which the nuclei move with reduced mass μ ≈ 10³–10⁵ mₑ. The rotational quantum number J plays the role of l. The Morse potential

V(r) = De[(1 − e−a(r−re))² − 1]

is exactly solvable for J = 0: G(v) = ωe(v + ½) − ωexe(v + ½)² − De, with ωe = a√(2De/μ) and ωexe = a²ħ²/(2μ). The presets build the potential from the measured ωe, ωexe and re (Huber and Herzberg), so the lowest levels are right by construction. The highest ones are not, because Morse's De = ωe²/4ωexe generally differs from the true well depth. Rotation adds

E(v, J) = G(v) + BvJ(J+1) − Dv[J(J+1)]², Bv = Be − αe(v + ½), Be = ħ²/(2μre²)

αe comes from vibration–rotation coupling (the bond lengthens in excited levels), and Dv from centrifugal stretching (Kratzer: De ≈ 4Be³/ωe²). The analysis panel extracts all of these from the computed energies, as a spectroscopist would from measured lines. The dissociation energy measured from the lowest level is D₀ = De − ZPE.

For high J the centrifugal term creates a barrier on the outer side of the well. Levels above the dissociation limit but below the barrier are quasi-bound. They decay by tunnelling (rotational predissociation), with lifetimes from picoseconds to longer than the age of the universe.

6. van der Waals Dimers

Closed-shell atoms attract each other through the dispersion force V ≈ −C₆/r⁶ at long range and repel strongly at short range, modelled by the Lennard-Jones potential 4ε[(σ/r)¹² − (σ/r)⁶]. The wells are shallow (ε/kB ≈ 10–200 K) and the dimers are extremely floppy. Ar₂ has a handful of vibrational levels. He₂ is the extreme case: its true potential holds a single level bound by about 1 mK, with a size of about 50 Å, the largest known ground-state diatomic. A near-threshold state shows up in scattering as a very large scattering length, so the low-energy cross section 8πa² is huge.

At thermal energies atom–atom collisions have hundreds of partial waves. Particles with the right l can be temporarily trapped behind the centrifugal barrier, giving orbiting resonances: narrow peaks in σ(E) that are seen in molecular-beam experiments and affect transport coefficients at low temperature. For identical spin-0 bosons (⁴He, ⁴⁰Ar) only even l exist, which removes half of the resonances.

7. Numerical Method for Bound States

The substitution r = eˣ, u = r½φ(x) turns the radial equation exactly into

φ″(x) = [(l + ½)² + 2μr²(V(r) − E)] φ(x)

A uniform grid in x is fine near the nucleus and coarse far away, which is what atomic and molecular wavefunctions need (hydrogen 1s and 20s on one grid of a few thousand points). It is integrated with the Numerov method (error O(Δx⁴) per unit length).

Finding every level. By the Sturm oscillation theorem, the number of nodes of the outward solution increases by one each time E passes an eigenvalue. Bisection on the node count therefore finds level n directly, with no initial guess and no risk of skipping a level. The wavefunction is then built from an outward and an inward solution joined at the outer classical turning point. The Numerov recursion produces spurious sign changes if Δx²f/12 > 1, so the search starts above the energy where that would happen. The fraction of each level's amplitude left at rmax is reported: a large tail means the box is too small.

8. Partial-Wave Scattering

Outside the range of the potential the radial solution is a combination of the free solutions. With Riccati functions sl(ρ) = ρjl(ρ) ~ sin(ρ − lπ/2) and cl(ρ) = −ρyl(ρ) ~ cos(ρ − lπ/2),

ul(r) ∝ sl(kr) cos δl + cl(kr) sin δl

which defines the phase shift δl. The lab integrates u outward to two radii R₁, R₂ beyond the potential and solves for tan δl = [u₁s(kR₂) − u₂s(kR₁)]/[u₂c(kR₁) − u₁c(kR₂)]. The observables follow:

f(θ) = (1/k) Σ (2l+1) eiδl sin δl Pl(cos θ), dσ/dΩ = |f(θ)|², σ = (4π/k²) Σ (2l+1) sin²δl

Each partial wave is bounded by the unitarity limit (4π/k²)(2l+1), reached when δl = π/2. Only l up to about kR contributes (the classical impact parameter b = l/k must be smaller than the range R). For identical spin-0 bosons the amplitude is symmetrized, f(θ) + f(π − θ), which keeps even l only and doubles their contribution.

Phase shifts are defined modulo π. The lab makes them continuous in energy and anchors them at the highest energy, so the curve can be compared with Levinson's theorem.

9. Low-Energy Scattering

As k → 0 the phase shifts obey the Wigner threshold law δl ∝ k2l+1 (for potentials falling faster than r−(2l+3)), so only s-waves survive. The s-wave is characterised by the effective-range expansion

k cot δ₀ = −1/a + ½ rek² + …, σ(E → 0) = 4πa² (8πa² for identical bosons)

with scattering length a and effective range re. For a hard sphere a = R. A large |a| signals a state close to threshold: a weakly bound state gives a large positive a ≈ 1/κ, and a virtual state gives a large negative a.

Levinson's theorem. For a short-range potential without a hard core, δ₀(0) − δ₀(∞) = Nbπ, where Nb is the number of s-wave bound states. Each bound state "uses up" π of phase. With a hard core, δ₀ decreases without limit (≈ −ka at high energy), and the theorem must be applied to the soft part.

The Ramsauer–Townsend effect (1921): if an attractive well makes δ₀ pass through π at a low energy while higher partial waves are still negligible, σ almost vanishes. Slow electrons pass through Ar, Kr and Xe atoms with almost no scattering near 0.7 eV, which is a purely wave-mechanical effect.

10. Coulomb Scattering

A 1/r tail never becomes negligible, so the free solutions are replaced by the Coulomb functions Fl(η, ρ) and Gl(η, ρ), with Sommerfeld parameter η = Z₁Z₂μ/k (negative for attraction). Asymptotically Fl ~ sin(ρ − η ln 2ρ − lπ/2 + σl), with Coulomb phase σl = arg Γ(l + 1 + iη). The pure Coulomb amplitude gives the Rutherford cross section

dσ/dΩ = η²/(4k² sin⁴(θ/2)), fC = −η e−iη ln sin²(θ/2) + 2iσ₀/(2k sin²(θ/2))

A short-range modification (an ion core) adds a phase shift δl relative to the Coulomb functions, and the full amplitude is fC + (1/k)Σ(2l+1)e2iσleiδl sin δl Pl. The lab computes F and G from their asymptotic series at large ρ and integrates the Coulomb equation inward to the matching radius. This is accurate to 10⁻⁹ and checked against the Wronskian F′G − FG′ = 1 and against arbitrary-precision values.

11. Resonances

A shape resonance appears when a barrier (centrifugal, or part of V itself) separates an inner well from the outside. A particle with energy below the barrier top can be trapped inside for a time τ = ħ/Γ before tunnelling out. (A Feshbach resonance, in contrast, arises from coupling to a closed channel; this one-channel lab describes shape resonances.) Near an isolated resonance

δl(E) = δbg + arctan[Γ/2 / (Er − E)], σl ≈ (4π/k²)(2l+1) (Γ²/4)/[(E − Er)² + Γ²/4]

(the Breit–Wigner form): the phase rises by π over a width Γ. The particle is delayed by the Wigner time delay 2ħ dδ/dE, which peaks at 4ħ/Γ.

Gamow states. The resonance is a pole of the S-matrix Sl = e2iδl at complex energy E = Er − iΓ/2, i.e. a solution of the radial equation that is regular at the origin and purely outgoing, u ~ eikr, with complex k. Its time dependence e−iEt/ħ decays as e−Γt/2ħ, and because Im k < 0 it grows at large r (the wave emitted long ago). The lab finds the pole by integrating at complex energy and matching to the outgoing Riccati–Hankel function hl+ = cl + isl.

For narrow resonances the WKB estimate is Γ ≈ (ħ/T) e−2S. Here T = 2∫μ dr/p is the classical period in the well (the "knocking rate" is 1/T) and S = ∫|p| dr/ħ is the tunnelling integral under the barrier, with the Langer replacement l(l+1) → (l + ½)². This is the formula Gamow used for α decay in 1928. Γ depends exponentially on the barrier, so resonances become rapidly narrower as E moves below the top.

12. Finding Resonances Numerically

A narrow resonance changes δ by π within Γ. Because phase shifts are only defined modulo π, a resonance narrower than the scan step is invisible in δ(E). The lab therefore combines two searches:

StepWhat it does
Quasi-bound levelsCloses the well with a wall at the outer turning point r₃(E) (iterated) and finds its levels by node counting. These are the resonance energies to about Γ, however narrow.
Phase-shift scanFinds broad structures, including those above the barrier, from maxima of dδ/dE, then zooms in until the resonance is resolved.
WidthComplex pole (short-range potentials) at Rm and 1.35 Rm; Breit–Wigner fit of δ(E) over ±6Γ; WKB.
Very narrow statesFor Γ/E < 2·10⁻⁹ the change of δ is below the precision of double arithmetic. Only E (quasi-bound level) and ΓWKB are given.

Agreement of the Breit–Wigner and pole widths (normally to 10⁻⁴ or better) is the quality check. WKB agrees to within tens of percent for states well below the barrier top and fails near the top.

14. Practice Problems

1. What is the ionization energy of positronium, and the wavelength of its Lyman-α line?
μ = 1/2: E₁ = −1/4 Eₕ = 6.803 eV. Lyman-α: (3/4)(6.803 eV) = 5.102 eV → 243.0 nm (twice the hydrogen value).
2. The sodium 3s quantum defect is 1.348. Estimate the 3s binding energy.
n* = 3 − 1.348 = 1.652; E = −1/(2 × 1.652²) Eₕ = −0.1832 Eₕ = −4.99 eV (measured 5.139 eV; δ is slightly n-dependent).
3. For a Morse oscillator with ωe = 2990.9 cm⁻¹ and ωexe = 52.82 cm⁻¹ (HCl), find the fundamental and the number of bound levels.
ν₀ = ωe − 2ωexe = 2885.3 cm⁻¹. Levels: ⌊2990.9/(2 × 52.82) − ½⌋ + 1 = ⌊27.81⌋ + 1 = 28.
4. What is the low-energy cross section for a hard sphere of radius R, and the high-energy limit?
a = R gives 4πR², four times the geometric cross section. At high energy σ → 2πR²: πR² from absorption-like removal plus πR² of diffraction (shadow scattering).
5. An s-wave has δ₀ = π − 0.01 rad at k = 0.05 a₀⁻¹. Estimate the scattering length.
tan δ₀ ≈ −0.01, so k cot δ₀ ≈ 0.05/(−0.01) = −5 → a = −1/(k cot δ₀) = 0.2 a₀ (assuming rek² is negligible). The "π" is one bound state (Levinson).
6. A resonance has Γ = 0.6 cm⁻¹. What is its lifetime?
Γ = 0.6/219 474.6 Eₕ = 2.73·10⁻⁶ Eₕ; τ = 2.4189·10⁻¹⁷/2.73·10⁻⁶ = 8.8 ps.
7. Why does the d series of potassium have a large quantum defect (0.28) while that of sodium is tiny (0.015)?
The empty 3d orbital of K lies at small radius and penetrates the [Ar] core strongly, and core polarization is large. In Na the 3d orbital is hydrogen-like and stays outside the small core.
8. How many partial waves are needed for Ar + Ar at 100 cm⁻¹ (σ ≈ 3.4 Å)?
μ = 19.98 u = 36 425 mₑ; E = 4.56·10⁻⁴ Eₕ; k = √(2μE) = 5.76 a₀⁻¹ = 10.9 Å⁻¹; lmax ≈ k·(1.5σ) ≈ 55. Use about 80 to be safe.

16. Limits of the Models in This Module

Single channel, central potential. No spin–orbit or fine structure, no coupling between electronic or rotational channels (so no Feshbach resonances, no predissociation by curve crossing), and no exchange with core electrons beyond the Pauli remark.

Potentials. The alkali model has a single parameter and no core polarization (−αd/2r⁴). Morse and Lennard-Jones are textbook forms: realistic ab initio or spectroscopically fitted (RKR) curves differ near dissociation and at long range. The He₂ LJ potential binds no state, unlike the true potential.

Numerics. At square edges the potential is averaged over the grid cell containing the discontinuity, which keeps the integration second-order accurate. Bound states sit in a box of radius rmax (the tail is reported). Phase shifts neglect the potential beyond Rm, which matters for r⁻⁶ tails at very low energy (check by increasing Rm). The complex-pole search is done only for potentials without a Coulomb tail. Widths below about 10⁻⁹E are from WKB only.
