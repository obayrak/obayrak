Bound, Quasi-bound and Scattering States — Theory

One equation, the radial Schrödinger equation, describes an electron in an atom, two nuclei vibrating in a molecule, and two atoms colliding. Its solutions fall into three classes: bound states at discrete negative energies, scattering states at every positive energy, and in between quasi-bound states (resonances), which are trapped for a finite time behind a barrier. This chapter treats all three for atomic and molecular systems and explains the numerical methods used in this lab.

1. The Radial Equation

For two particles interacting through a central potential V(r), separating the centre-of-mass motion leaves a single particle of reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂). Writing \(\psi = u(r)Y_{lm}(\theta, \varphi)/r\) gives

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\,u^{\prime\prime} + \Big[V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2l(l + 1)}{2\mu r^2}\Big]u = E\,u,\qquad u(0) = 0\]

The bracket is the effective potential. Its centrifugal term keeps particles with l > 0 away from the origin and, added to an attractive well, can form a barrier. The lab works in atomic units (ħ = mₑ = e = 4πε₀ = 1): lengths in bohr a₀ = 0.529177 Å, energies in hartree Eₕ = 27.2114 eV = 219 474.6 cm⁻¹ = 315 775 K, and μ in electron masses (1 u = 1822.888 mₑ).

2. Hydrogen-like Atoms

For V = −Z/r the levels depend only on n = nr + l + 1:

\[E_n = -\frac{\mu Z^2}{2n^2}\,E_h,\qquad \langle r\rangle_{nl} = \frac{3n^2 - l(l + 1)}{2\mu Z}\,a_0\]

The degeneracy in l is special to the 1/r potential (a hidden SO(4) symmetry, conservation of the Runge–Lenz vector). Any deviation from 1/r, such as screening or a finite core, lifts it. The reduced mass matters at the 10⁻⁴ level for hydrogen (the Rydberg constant for hydrogen is RH = 109 677.6 cm⁻¹ rather than R∞ = 109 737.3 cm⁻¹). For exotic atoms it is decisive. Positronium (μ = mₑ/2) has half the binding energy and twice the size of hydrogen. Muonic hydrogen (μ ≈ 186 mₑ) is 186 times smaller, so its levels are sensitive to the proton's charge radius, which is how the "proton radius puzzle" arose in 2010.

Why the levels are exact here. The lab solves the radial equation numerically for every potential, including the Coulomb one, so the exact formula is a test of the solver. On the logarithmic grid the error is about 10⁻¹² for the lowest levels.

3. Screened Coulomb Potentials

In a plasma, free charges screen a nucleus over the Debye length \(\lambda_D = \sqrt{\varepsilon_0k_BT/(n_ee^2)}\), giving the Debye–Hückel (Yukawa) potential \(V = -Z\,e^{-r/\lambda_D}/r\). Unlike the pure Coulomb potential it supports only a finite number of bound states. As λD shrinks, levels move up and disappear into the continuum one by one: the ionization potential is lowered (pressure ionization). For l = 0 the last bound state disappears at λc = 0.83990 a₀/(Zμ) (Rogers, Graboske and Harwood, 1970). Near λc the state becomes arbitrarily large and weakly bound, and its binding energy vanishes quadratically in (λ − λc).

For l > 0 the screened potential plus the centrifugal term has a barrier, so a level that leaves the bound spectrum first becomes a narrow shape resonance just above threshold. This is a clean example of a bound state turning into a quasi-bound one (Quasi-bound tab).

4. Alkali Atoms and Quantum Defects

The valence electron of an alkali atom sees the nuclear charge Z screened by the Z − 1 core electrons: −Z/r near the nucleus and −1/r outside. A one-parameter model \(V = -[1 + (Z - 1)e^{-r/d}]/r\) captures this (the presets fit d to the ionization energy). Its levels obey the Rydberg formula

\[E_{nl} = -\frac{1}{2(n - \delta_l)^2}\,E_h\]

with a quantum defect δl that is almost independent of n. Penetrating low-l orbits dive into the core, feel the larger charge and are bound more strongly (large δ), while high-l orbits stay outside (δ ≈ 0). For sodium, δs ≈ 1.35, δp ≈ 0.86 and δd ≈ 0.015, which is why the Na D lines (3p → 3s) lie at 589 nm instead of the hydrogenic position.

The model's deepest levels (1s, 2s, 2p for Na) are core orbitals. In the real atom they are occupied, so the Pauli principle makes the valence electron start at n = 3. The node count of the valence wavefunction reflects this: the 3s orbital has two radial nodes.

Seaton's theorem. Quantum-defect theory connects bound and continuum states. Just above threshold, an electron scattering from the ion core has a short-range phase shift (relative to Coulomb functions) \(\delta_l(E \to 0^+) = \pi\mu_l\), where μl is the quantum defect extrapolated to n → ∞. The Rydberg series and the low-energy scattering are one analytic function of energy. Compare the analysis panel of the Bound-states tab with the Scattering tab for the same atom.

5. Diatomic Molecules

In the Born–Oppenheimer approximation the electrons adjust instantly to the nuclear separation r, providing a potential energy curve V(r) in which the nuclei move with reduced mass μ ≈ 10³–10⁵ mₑ. The rotational quantum number J plays the role of l. The Morse potential

\[V(r) = D_e\Big[\big(1 - e^{-a(r - r_e)}\big)^2 - 1\Big]\]

is exactly solvable for J = 0: \(G(v) = \omega_e(v + \tfrac12) - \omega_ex_e(v + \tfrac12)^2 - D_e\), with \(\omega_e = a\sqrt{2D_e/\mu}\) and \(\omega_ex_e = a^2\hbar^2/(2\mu)\). The presets build the potential from the measured ωe, ωexe and re (Huber and Herzberg), so the lowest levels are right by construction. The highest ones are not, because Morse's \(D_e = \omega_e^2/4\omega_ex_e\) generally differs from the true well depth. Rotation adds

\[E(v, J) = G(v) + B_vJ(J + 1) - D_v\big[J(J + 1)\big]^2,\qquad B_v = B_e - \alpha_e\big(v + \tfrac12\big),\qquad B_e = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu r_e^2}\]

αe comes from vibration–rotation coupling (the bond lengthens in excited levels), and Dv from centrifugal stretching (Kratzer: \(D_e \approx 4B_e^3/\omega_e^2\)). The analysis panel extracts all of these from the computed energies, as a spectroscopist would from measured lines. The dissociation energy measured from the lowest level is D₀ = De − ZPE.

For high J the centrifugal term creates a barrier on the outer side of the well. Levels above the dissociation limit but below the barrier are quasi-bound. They decay by tunnelling (rotational predissociation), with lifetimes from picoseconds to longer than the age of the universe.

6. van der Waals Dimers

Closed-shell atoms attract each other through the dispersion force V ≈ −C₆/r⁶ at long range and repel strongly at short range, modelled by the Lennard-Jones potential \(4\varepsilon[(\sigma/r)^{12} - (\sigma/r)^6]\). The wells are shallow (ε/kB ≈ 10–200 K) and the dimers are extremely floppy. Ar₂ has a handful of vibrational levels. He₂ is the extreme case: its true potential holds a single level bound by about 1 mK, with a size of about 50 Å, the largest known ground-state diatomic. A near-threshold state shows up in scattering as a very large scattering length, so the low-energy cross section 8πa² is huge.

At thermal energies atom–atom collisions have hundreds of partial waves. Particles with the right l can be temporarily trapped behind the centrifugal barrier, giving orbiting resonances: narrow peaks in σ(E) that are seen in molecular-beam experiments and affect transport coefficients at low temperature. For identical spin-0 bosons (⁴He, ⁴⁰Ar) only even l exist, which removes half of the resonances.

7. Numerical Method for Bound States

The substitution \(r = e^x\), \(u = r^{1/2}\phi(x)\) turns the radial equation exactly into

\[\phi^{\prime\prime}(x) = \Big[\big(l + \tfrac12\big)^2 + 2\mu r^2\big(V(r) - E\big)\Big]\phi(x)\]

A uniform grid in x is fine near the nucleus and coarse far away, which is what atomic and molecular wavefunctions need (hydrogen 1s and 20s on one grid of a few thousand points). It is integrated with the Numerov method (error O(Δx⁴) per unit length).

Finding every level. By the Sturm oscillation theorem, the number of nodes of the outward solution increases by one each time E passes an eigenvalue. Bisection on the node count therefore finds level n directly, with no initial guess and no risk of skipping a level. The wavefunction is then built from an outward and an inward solution joined at the outer classical turning point. The Numerov recursion produces spurious sign changes if Δx²f/12 > 1, so the search starts above the energy where that would happen. The fraction of each level's amplitude left at rmax is reported: a large tail means the box is too small.

8. Partial-Wave Scattering

Outside the range of the potential the radial solution is a combination of the free solutions. With Riccati functions \(s_l(\rho) = \rho j_l(\rho) \sim \sin(\rho - l\pi/2)\) and \(c_l(\rho) = -\rho y_l(\rho) \sim \cos(\rho - l\pi/2)\),

\[u_l(r) \propto s_l(kr)\cos\delta_l + c_l(kr)\sin\delta_l\]

which defines the phase shift δl. The lab integrates u outward to two radii R₁, R₂ beyond the potential and solves for \(\tan\delta_l = [u_1s(kR_2) - u_2s(kR_1)]/[u_2c(kR_1) - u_1c(kR_2)]\). The observables follow:

\[f(\theta) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_l (2l + 1)\,e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l\,P_l(\cos\theta),\qquad \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(\theta)|^2,\qquad \sigma = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_l (2l + 1)\sin^2\delta_l\]

Each partial wave is bounded by the unitarity limit (4π/k²)(2l+1), reached when δl = π/2. Only l up to about kR contributes (the classical impact parameter b = l/k must be smaller than the range R). For identical spin-0 bosons the amplitude is symmetrized, f(θ) + f(π − θ), which keeps even l only and doubles their contribution.

Phase shifts are defined modulo π. The lab makes them continuous in energy and anchors them at the highest energy, so the curve can be compared with Levinson's theorem.

9. Low-Energy Scattering

As k → 0 the phase shifts obey the Wigner threshold law \(\delta_l \propto k^{2l+1}\) (for potentials falling faster than \(r^{-(2l+3)}\)), so only s-waves survive. The s-wave is characterised by the effective-range expansion

\[k\cot\delta_0 = -\frac{1}{a} + \frac12\,r_ek^2 + \dots,\qquad \sigma(E \to 0) = 4\pi a^2\quad (8\pi a^2 \text{ for identical bosons})\]

with scattering length a and effective range re. For a hard sphere a = R. A large |a| signals a state close to threshold: a weakly bound state gives a large positive a ≈ 1/κ, and a virtual state gives a large negative a.

Levinson's theorem. For a short-range potential without a hard core, \(\delta_0(0) - \delta_0(\infty) = N_b\pi\), where Nb is the number of s-wave bound states. Each bound state "uses up" π of phase. With a hard core, δ₀ decreases without limit (≈ −ka at high energy), and the theorem must be applied to the soft part.

The Ramsauer–Townsend effect (1921): if an attractive well makes δ₀ pass through π at a low energy while higher partial waves are still negligible, σ almost vanishes. Slow electrons pass through Ar, Kr and Xe atoms with almost no scattering near 0.7 eV, which is a purely wave-mechanical effect.

10. Coulomb Scattering

A 1/r tail never becomes negligible, so the free solutions are replaced by the Coulomb functions Fl(η, ρ) and Gl(η, ρ), with Sommerfeld parameter \(\eta = Z_1Z_2\mu/k\) (negative for attraction). Asymptotically \(F_l \sim \sin(\rho - \eta\ln 2\rho - l\pi/2 + \sigma_l)\), with Coulomb phase \(\sigma_l = \arg\Gamma(l + 1 + i\eta)\). The pure Coulomb amplitude gives the Rutherford cross section

\[\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{\eta^2}{4k^2\sin^4(\theta/2)},\qquad f_C = -\frac{\eta\,e^{-i\eta\ln\sin^2(\theta/2) + 2i\sigma_0}}{2k\sin^2(\theta/2)}\]

A short-range modification (an ion core) adds a phase shift δl relative to the Coulomb functions, and the full amplitude is \(f_C + (1/k)\sum(2l+1)e^{2i\sigma_l}e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l\,P_l\). The lab computes F and G from their asymptotic series at large ρ and integrates the Coulomb equation inward to the matching radius. This is accurate to 10⁻⁹ and checked against the Wronskian \(F^{\prime}G - FG^{\prime} = 1\) and against arbitrary-precision values.

11. Resonances

A shape resonance appears when a barrier (centrifugal, or part of V itself) separates an inner well from the outside. A particle with energy below the barrier top can be trapped inside for a time τ = ħ/Γ before tunnelling out. (A Feshbach resonance, in contrast, arises from coupling to a closed channel; this one-channel lab describes shape resonances.) Near an isolated resonance

\[\delta_l(E) = \delta_{bg} + \arctan\frac{\Gamma/2}{E_r - E},\qquad \sigma_l \approx \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l + 1)\,\frac{\Gamma^2/4}{(E - E_r)^2 + \Gamma^2/4}\]

(the Breit–Wigner form): the phase rises by π over a width Γ. The particle is delayed by the Wigner time delay \(2\hbar\,d\delta/dE\), which peaks at \(4\hbar/\Gamma\).

Gamow states. The resonance is a pole of the S-matrix \(S_l = e^{2i\delta_l}\) at complex energy \(E = E_r - i\Gamma/2\), i.e. a solution of the radial equation that is regular at the origin and purely outgoing, \(u \sim e^{ikr}\), with complex k. Its time dependence \(e^{-iEt/\hbar}\) decays as \(e^{-\Gamma t/2\hbar}\), and because Im k < 0 it grows at large r (the wave emitted long ago). The lab finds the pole by integrating at complex energy and matching to the outgoing Riccati–Hankel function \(h_l^+ = c_l + is_l\).

For narrow resonances the WKB estimate is \(\Gamma \approx (\hbar/T)\,e^{-2S}\). Here \(T = 2\int\mu\,dr/p\) is the classical period in the well (the "knocking rate" is 1/T) and \(S = \int|p|\,dr/\hbar\) is the tunnelling integral under the barrier, with the Langer replacement \(l(l+1) \to (l + \tfrac12)^2\). This is the formula Gamow used for α decay in 1928. Γ depends exponentially on the barrier, so resonances become rapidly narrower as E moves below the top.

12. Finding Resonances Numerically

A narrow resonance changes δ by π within Γ. Because phase shifts are only defined modulo π, a resonance narrower than the scan step is invisible in δ(E). The lab therefore combines two searches:

StepWhat it does
Quasi-bound levelsCloses the well with a wall at the outer turning point r₃(E) (iterated) and finds its levels by node counting. These are the resonance energies to about Γ, however narrow.
Phase-shift scanFinds broad structures, including those above the barrier, from maxima of dδ/dE, then zooms in until the resonance is resolved.

Very narrow statesFor Γ/E < 2·10⁻⁹ the change of δ is below the precision of double arithmetic. Only E (quasi-bound level) and ΓWKB are given.

Agreement of the Breit–Wigner and pole widths (normally to 10⁻⁴ or better) is the quality check. WKB agrees to within tens of percent for states well below the barrier top and fails near the top.

14. Practice Problems

1. What is the ionization energy of positronium, and the wavelength of its Lyman-α line?
μ = 1/2: E₁ = −1/4 Eₕ = 6.803 eV. Lyman-α: (3/4)(6.803 eV) = 5.102 eV → 243.0 nm (twice the hydrogen value).
2. The sodium 3s quantum defect is 1.348. Estimate the 3s binding energy.
n* = 3 − 1.348 = 1.652; E = −1/(2 × 1.652²) Eₕ = −0.1832 Eₕ = −4.99 eV (measured 5.139 eV; δ is slightly n-dependent).
3. For a Morse oscillator with ωe = 2990.9 cm⁻¹ and ωexe = 52.82 cm⁻¹ (HCl), find the fundamental and the number of bound levels.
ν₀ = ωe − 2ωexe = 2885.3 cm⁻¹. Levels: ⌊2990.9/(2 × 52.82) − ½⌋ + 1 = ⌊27.81⌋ + 1 = 28.
4. What is the low-energy cross section for a hard sphere of radius R, and the high-energy limit?
a = R gives 4πR², four times the geometric cross section. At high energy σ → 2πR²: πR² from absorption-like removal plus πR² of diffraction (shadow scattering).
5. An s-wave has δ₀ = π − 0.01 rad at k = 0.05 a₀⁻¹. Estimate the scattering length.
tan δ₀ ≈ −0.01, so k cot δ₀ ≈ 0.05/(−0.01) = −5 → a = −1/(k cot δ₀) = 0.2 a₀ (assuming rek² is negligible). The "π" is one bound state (Levinson).
6. A resonance has Γ = 0.6 cm⁻¹. What is its lifetime?
Γ = 0.6/219 474.6 Eₕ = 2.73·10⁻⁶ Eₕ; τ = 2.4189·10⁻¹⁷/2.73·10⁻⁶ = 8.8 ps.
7. Why does the d series of potassium have a large quantum defect (0.28) while that of sodium is tiny (0.015)?
The empty 3d orbital of K lies at small radius and penetrates the [Ar] core strongly, and core polarization is large. In Na the 3d orbital is hydrogen-like and stays outside the small core.
8. How many partial waves are needed for Ar + Ar at 100 cm⁻¹ (σ ≈ 3.4 Å)?
μ = 19.98 u = 36 425 mₑ; E = 4.56·10⁻⁴ Eₕ; k = √(2μE) = 5.76 a₀⁻¹ = 10.9 Å⁻¹; lmax ≈ k·(1.5σ) ≈ 55. Use about 80 to be safe.

16. Resonances Behind a Coulomb Barrier

When two positive ions are bound by a short-range chemical force, the long-range Coulomb repulsion Z₁Z₂/r forms a barrier. If the well lies above the separated-ion limit, as for many doubly charged diatomics (He₂²⁺, N₂²⁺, CO²⁺), every vibrational level is quasi-bound: the molecule is metastable and eventually explodes into two ions (Coulomb explosion).

A Gamow state behind a Coulomb barrier must be matched to the outgoing Coulomb function \(H^+_l = G_l + iF_l\) rather than to a spherical Hankel function. Only its logarithmic derivative is needed, and Steed's continued fraction gives it for complex η and complex ρ = kr:

\[\frac{H^{+\prime}}{H^+} = i\Big(1 - \frac{\eta}{\rho}\Big) + \frac{i}{\rho}\,\cfrac{a_1}{b_1 + \cfrac{a_2}{b_2 + \dots}},\qquad a_n = (l + n + i\eta)(n - 1 - l + i\eta),\qquad b_n = 2(\rho - \eta + ni)\]

The pole condition \(u'(R_m)/u(R_m) = k\,H^{+\prime}/H^+\) at complex E then works exactly as for short-range potentials. The matching radius is moved, when necessary, outside the classical turning point of the pure Coulomb + centrifugal potential, \(r_t = [\eta + \sqrt{\eta^2 + l(l+1)}]/k\), because inside it F is exponentially small and cannot be integrated inward. The tunnelling probability through a pure Coulomb barrier is governed by the Gamow factor \(\exp(-2\pi\eta)\), \(\eta = Z_1Z_2\mu/(\hbar^2k)\) (in atomic units Z₁Z₂μ/k). The lifetimes therefore range from picoseconds for the top levels to far beyond the age of the universe for the lowest ones (analysis panel of the dication preset).

17. Quantum-Defect Theory and Seaton's Theorem

Outside the ionic core an alkali valence electron moves in the pure Coulomb field of charge 1. The core only shifts the phase of the radial wave, which defines the energy-dependent quantum defect μl(E):

\[E_{nl} = -\frac{\mu}{2(n - \mu_l)^2}\,E_h\ \ (\text{bound}),\qquad \delta_l(E) = \pi\mu_l(E)\ \ (\text{continuum, short-range phase relative to the Coulomb functions})\]

Seaton's theorem states that one smooth function μl(E) describes both sides of the ionization threshold: the defects of the high Rydberg levels tend to δl(E → 0⁺)/π. The Scattering tab plots both for the alkali presets ("quantum defect across threshold"), and the Validation tab checks the continuity for the Li s series to 10⁻³. The physical reason is that near E = 0 the Coulomb functions vary smoothly with energy on the scale of the core, so the core phase cannot tell a bound electron from a slow free one.

Integer ambiguity. Only μ mod 1 is fixed by the continuum phase. The integer part depends on how the core orbitals are counted: the model potential here also binds the 1s (Li) or 1s–2p (Na) core levels, so the plotted defects count every level of the model.

18. RKR Potentials from Spectroscopic Constants

The Rydberg–Klein–Rees method inverts measured vibrational and rotational energies into a potential. With \(G(v) = \omega_e(v+\tfrac12) - \omega_ex_e(v+\tfrac12)^2 + \omega_ey_e(v+\tfrac12)^3\) and \(B(v) = B_e - \alpha_e(v+\tfrac12) + \gamma_e(v+\tfrac12)^2\), the two classical turning points at energy G(v) are

\[f = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2\mu}}\int_{-1/2}^{v}\frac{dv^{\prime}}{\sqrt{G(v) - G(v^{\prime})}},\qquad g = \frac{\sqrt{2\mu}}{\hbar}\int_{-1/2}^{v}\frac{B(v^{\prime})\,dv^{\prime}}{\sqrt{G(v) - G(v^{\prime})}},\qquad r_\pm = \sqrt{f/g + f^2} \pm f\]

f is half the width of the well, fixed by G alone; g fixes where the well sits, through the rotational constants. The integrable singularity at v′ = v is removed by the substitution v′ = v − w², and the turning points (every 0.05 in v) are joined by a cubic spline. Beyond the last fitted level the curve approaches the dissociation limit De exponentially.

RKR is a first-order semiclassical construction: the WKB levels of the curve are exactly G(v). The exact quantum levels differ by Dunham's constant \(Y_{00} = B_e/4 + \alpha_e\omega_e/12B_e + \alpha_e^2\omega_e^2/144B_e^3 - \omega_ex_e/4\) plus higher-order terms. For CO the difference is a constant 0.19 cm⁻¹. For HCl it grows to about 12 cm⁻¹ at v ≈ 10, a reminder that light hydrides need beyond-RKR (inverse perturbation) refinements. Unlike Morse, the RKR curve has the correct dissociation energy.

19. Polarization Potentials and Halo States

Electron–atom scattering. A slow electron polarizes a neutral atom, which attracts it as \(-\alpha_d/2r^4\) at long range (αd the dipole polarizability). With l = 1 the centrifugal barrier and this attraction can hold the electron for a few femtoseconds: a shape resonance of the temporary negative ion. Mg⁻ ²P near 0.15 eV is a classic case. Because the tail falls off as r⁻⁴, the effective-range expansion is modified at low energy (O'Malley–Spruch–Rosenberg): k cot δ₀ acquires a term linear in k, so the fitted "effective range" of this preset is only indicative.

The helium dimer. The He–He well is only 11 K deep and He is light, so the zero-point energy nearly cancels the well. On the accurate HFD-B potential exactly one level survives, bound by about 1.7 mK, with a scattering length of 88 Å and a mean separation of about 46 Å: a quantum halo, far larger than the potential itself (rm ≈ 3 Å). Such states obey universal relations independent of the potential's details:

\[E_b \approx -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu a^2},\qquad \langle r\rangle \approx \frac{a}{2},\qquad \psi(r) \approx \frac{e^{-r/a}}{r}\ \ \text{outside the well}\]

The measured bond length 52 ± 4 Å (Grisenti et al. 2000) confirms the halo. The simple Lennard-Jones preset binds no state at all, which shows how sensitive threshold physics is to the potential.

20. Rovibrational Spectra

A diatomic molecule in vibrational level v and rotational level J has energy EvJ, computed here by solving the radial equation with the centrifugal term \(J(J+1)/2\mu r^2\). In an infrared transition the dipole moment \(\mu(r) = \mu_0 + \mu_1(r - r_e)\) changes, and for a ¹Σ state ΔJ = ±1 only: the R branch (J → J+1) and the P branch (J → J−1), separated by a gap of about 2(B′ + B″) where the forbidden Q branch would be.

\[I(J^{\prime\prime} \to J^{\prime}) \propto \nu\,\frac{N_{J^{\prime\prime}}}{2J^{\prime\prime} + 1}\,S_{J^{\prime\prime}J^{\prime}}\,\big|\langle v^{\prime}J^{\prime}|\,\mu(r)\,|v^{\prime\prime}J^{\prime\prime}\rangle\big|^2\big(1 - e^{-h\nu/kT}\big),\qquad N_J \propto g_J(2J + 1)\,e^{-E_J/kT},\qquad S_R = J^{\prime\prime} + 1,\ S_P = J^{\prime\prime}\]

Because the matrix element is computed with the exact J-dependent wavefunctions, the Herman–Wallis effect appears by itself. The vibrational and rotational motions mix, so the transition dipole depends on J and the R branch is stronger than the P branch when μ₀ and μ₁ have the same sign (HCl). Raman transitions (ΔJ = 0, ±2: Q, O and S branches, Placzek–Teller factors) also appear for homonuclear molecules, which have no infrared spectrum. Their intensities alternate with the nuclear-spin weights gJ, for example 1 : 3 for ortho/para H₂, and odd J only for ¹⁶O₂.

21. Franck–Condon Factors and Photodissociation

An electronic transition is fast compared with nuclear motion, so the nuclei keep their positions and momenta (the Franck–Condon principle). With a constant electronic transition moment the band intensities are proportional to the Franck–Condon factors

\[q(v^{\prime}, v^{\prime\prime}) = \Big|\int u_{v^{\prime}}(r)\,u_{v^{\prime\prime}}(r)\,dr\Big|^2,\qquad \sum_{v^{\prime}} q(v^{\prime}, v^{\prime\prime}) + \int \big|\langle E^{\prime}|v^{\prime\prime}\rangle\big|^2\,dE^{\prime} = 1\]

The sum rule (closure) holds because the bound and continuum levels of the upper state form a complete set. Above the dissociation limit of the upper state the absorption is a continuum: the molecule falls apart (photodissociation). By the reflection principle, the continuum spectrum is roughly the |uv″|² profile reflected off the upper potential curve. The I₂ B ← X system (visible violet colour, a convergence limit near 20 000 cm⁻¹) is the classic example. The continuum functions are energy-normalised, \(u \sim \sqrt{2\mu/\pi k}\,\sin(kr - l\pi/2 + \delta)\), and the closure is checked in the Validation tab.

22. Photoionization

In the independent-electron picture an atom absorbs a photon of energy ħω and its electron goes from the bound state |nl⟩ to a continuum state |εl′⟩ with l′ = l ± 1 and ε = ħω − I. In the length gauge and atomic units:

\[\sigma(\omega) = \frac{4\pi^2}{3}\,\alpha\,\hbar\omega\sum_{l^{\prime} = l\pm1}\frac{\max(l, l^{\prime})}{2l + 1}\,\Big|\int u_{\varepsilon l^{\prime}}(r)\,r\,u_{nl}(r)\,dr\Big|^2\]

For hydrogen 1s the threshold value is 6.30 Mb (1 Mb = 10⁻¹⁸ cm²) and σ falls off roughly as \(\omega^{-8/3}\) near threshold. The calculation reproduces the exact Stobbe formula (Validation tab), including the reduced-mass factor \((m_e/\mu)^2\). In alkali atoms the s → εp radial integral changes sign just above threshold because of the nodes that the core imposes on the valence orbital: the Cooper minimum, which the Na preset shows. Electron correlation, which the one-electron picture leaves out, modifies the real cross sections near threshold.

23. Cold Collisions: Scattering Length and Threshold Laws

As a well is deepened (V → λV), a new s-wave bound state appears each time the zero-energy wavefunction gains a node. At that point the scattering length passes through ±∞. Just beyond it a is large and positive, and the new state is a halo with \(E_b \approx -\hbar^2/2\mu a^2\). The thresholds λc are located where 1/a = 0, with no box needed. For a square well they reproduce \(\lambda_c = \pi^2/(8\mu V_0R^2)\) to 10⁻⁷. This is the principle behind tuning a with Feshbach resonances in ultracold gases.

\[\text{short range: } \delta_l \propto k^{2l+1} \Rightarrow \sigma_l \propto E^{2l}\ \ (\text{Wigner});\qquad \text{tail } -C_n/r^n:\ \sigma_l \propto E^{\min(2l,\,n-3)}\ \text{ for } l \ge 1\]

The anomalous term comes from distances r ~ 1/k, far beyond any matching radius. The lab adds the potential beyond Rm in the first Born approximation (\(\tan\delta \approx -(2\mu/k)\int V s_l(kr)^2\,dr\)), which is accurate there because the tail is weak. The threshold-law table then shows E³ for van der Waals dimers (n = 6, l ≥ 2) and E¹ for electron–atom polarization (n = 4, l ≥ 1). The unitarity limit 4π(2l+1)/k² bounds every partial cross section.

24. Rydberg Atoms in Static Fields

In an electric field F along z, the Hamiltonian H₀ + Fz couples states with Δl = ±1 and the same m. The lab diagonalises it in the basis of the computed field-free states |nlm⟩, using the angular factor \(\sqrt{[(l+1)^2 - m^2]/[(2l+1)(2l+3)]}\) and numerical radial integrals. In hydrogen the n-manifold splits linearly, \(E = -1/2n^2 + \tfrac32\,n(n_1 - n_2)F\) (parabolic quantum numbers), and levels of different manifolds cross. In alkali atoms the core breaks the hidden symmetry: low-l states with large quantum defects shift quadratically, and crossings become avoided (the Stark maps of Zimmerman et al., 1979). Two benchmark fields:

\[\text{Inglis–Teller: } F_{IT} = \frac{1}{3n^5},\qquad \text{saddle-point ionization: } F_c = \frac{1}{16n^4},\qquad E_{\text{saddle}} = -2\sqrt{ZF}\qquad (1\ \text{a.u.} = 5.142\times10^9\ \text{V/cm})\]

In a magnetic field B along z the paramagnetic term mB/2 shifts each m, and the diamagnetic term \((B^2/8)\,r^2\sin^2\theta\) couples Δl = 0, ±2. For Rydberg states (⟨r²⟩ ∝ n⁴) it becomes comparable to the level spacing already at a few tesla: the quadratic Zeeman effect, the gateway to quantum chaos in the hydrogen atom. The basis contains bound states only, so states above the saddle point, which really ionize, are not described.

25. Wave Packets, Revivals and the Decay of Quasi-bound States

A superposition \(\psi(t) = \sum c_n u_n e^{-iE_nt}\) of levels around \(\bar n\) moves like a classical particle at first, with period \(T_{cl} = 2\pi/|E^{\prime}(\bar n)|\). Because the levels are not equally spaced it spreads, and at the revival time \(T_{rev} = 4\pi/|E^{\prime\prime}(\bar n)|\) it re-forms, with fractional revivals at rational fractions of Trev. For hydrogen \(T_{rev}/T_{cl} = 2\bar n/3\). For a Morse oscillator \(T_{rev} = 2\pi/(2\omega_ex_e)\). The autocorrelation \(|\langle\psi(0)|\psi(t)\rangle|^2\) shows both, as in femtosecond pump–probe experiments on I₂ and Rydberg atoms.

For a quasi-bound state the lab solves the time-dependent equation directly: Crank–Nicolson steps \((1 + iH\Delta t/2)\,\psi_{n+1} = (1 - iH\Delta t/2)\,\psi_n\) with a complex absorbing potential beyond the barrier. The population left inside the barrier decays as \(e^{-\Gamma t/\hbar}\) after a short transient (the non-resonant part leaves first). The Crank–Nicolson map damps an eigenvector with energy E − iΓ/2 by exactly \(|(1 - iz\Delta t/2)/(1 + iz\Delta t/2)|\) per step, and that relation is inverted to correct the fitted rate. The result is a third, independent width, which agrees with the pole and the Breit–Wigner fit.

26. Matrix Methods and Complex Scaling

The shooting method (Numerov plus node counting) is cross-checked against diagonalisation of the Hamiltonian matrix on a mesh, which shares no code with it:

\[T_{ij} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu\Delta^2}\,(-1)^{i-j}\begin{cases}\dfrac{\pi^2}{3} - \dfrac{1}{2i^2}, & i = j\\[6pt] \dfrac{2}{(i - j)^2} - \dfrac{2}{(i + j)^2}, & i \ne j\end{cases}\qquad (\text{sinc DVR, Colbert and Miller 1992})\]

For potentials with a Coulomb singularity the regularized Lagrange–Laguerre mesh (Baye 2015) is used instead. Its mesh points are h times the zeros of LN, and it gives hydrogen levels to 10⁻¹³. The cross-check has already caught a real defect: for very large rmax the deep core orbitals of the alkali models fell below the stability limit of Numerov's method. Numerov now refines its grid automatically when that happens.

Complex scaling replaces r by \(r_0 + (r - r_0)e^{i\theta}\). The scaled Hamiltonian is non-Hermitian: its continuum rotates into the lower half-plane by −2θ, while a resonance is an isolated, θ-independent eigenvalue Er − iΓ/2 (Aguilar, Balslev and Combes). The lab diagonalises the complex matrix at two angles (Householder reduction to Hessenberg form, shifted QR) and keeps only θ-stable eigenvalues off the rotated continuum. It needs a potential that is analytic in r, so square wells, tabulated and HFD-B potentials are excluded.

27. Limits of the Models in This Module

Single channel, central potential. No spin–orbit or fine structure, no coupling between electronic or rotational channels (so no Feshbach resonances, no predissociation by curve crossing), and no exchange with core electrons beyond the Pauli remark.

Potentials. The alkali model has a single parameter and no core polarization (−αd/2r⁴). Morse and Lennard-Jones are textbook forms; the RKR and HFD-B presets are the realistic alternatives (RKR only up to the highest fitted level, with an extrapolated approach to De). The electron–atom model is tuned to one measured resonance position and ignores exchange with the target electrons. A tabulated potential is only as good as its points: the spline cannot add structure between them.

Numerics. At square edges the potential is averaged over the grid cell containing the discontinuity, which keeps the integration second-order accurate. Bound states sit in a box of radius rmax (the tail is reported). Phase shifts neglect the potential beyond Rm, which matters for r⁻⁶ tails at very low energy (check by increasing Rm). The complex-pole search uses spherical Hankel functions for short-range potentials and outgoing Coulomb functions for Coulomb tails; for states with Γ/E ≈ 10⁻⁶ its double-precision accuracy is about 0.3 %. Widths below about 10⁻⁹E are from WKB only.
