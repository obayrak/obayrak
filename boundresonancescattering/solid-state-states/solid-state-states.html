Bound, Quasi-bound and Scattering States in Solid State Physics — Theory

A crystal is a sea of electrons in a periodic potential. Its band structure is the scattering problem solved once and for all; the interesting physics is what happens when something breaks the periodicity. An impurity or an electron–hole pair binds states inside the gap (donors, excitons). A heterostructure confines electrons to a layer or a dot, or lets them tunnel through barriers. A resonance in the conduction band becomes a quasi-bound state: a resonant-tunnelling level or a virtual bound state of a transition-metal impurity. And every defect scatters the conduction electrons, which is where electrical resistance comes from. This chapter treats all three classes and explains the numerical methods used in this lab.

The same mathematical tools apply as in atoms and nuclei: the radial Schrödinger equation for impurities and excitons, partial-wave phase shifts for scattering in metals, and S-matrix poles for resonances. Two additions are specific to solids: the effective mass and dielectric constant that dress the particles, and the transfer matrix for layered structures.

2. The Effective-Mass Approximation

Near a band extremum the energy is quadratic in the crystal momentum, \(E(\mathbf k) \approx E_0 + \hbar^2k^2/2m^*\). If a perturbation \(U(\mathbf r)\) varies slowly on the scale of the lattice constant, the electron wavefunction is a Bloch function at the band edge times a slowly varying envelope \(F(\mathbf r)\) that obeys

\[\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m^*}\nabla^2 + U(\mathbf r)\right]F(\mathbf r) = (E - E_0)\,F(\mathbf r)\]

The crystal enters only through \(m^*\) and, for Coulomb interactions, through the dielectric constant \(\varepsilon\) that screens charges at distances larger than a few lattice constants. In these units \(\hbar^2/2m_e = 3.810\ \text{eV Å}^2\) and \(e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 14.40\ \text{eV Å}\). The approximation is good when the bound state extends over many unit cells. That is true for shallow donors in GaAs (\(a^* \approx 100\) Å) and fails in the central cell of a deep impurity.

3. Hydrogenic Donors and Acceptors

A phosphorus atom in silicon has one more electron than the atom it replaces. The extra electron is bound to the P⁺ ion by \(-e^2/\varepsilon r\), and the effective-mass equation becomes a scaled hydrogen atom:

\[E_n = -\frac{\mathrm{Ry}^*}{n^2},\qquad \mathrm{Ry}^* = \frac{m^*}{\varepsilon^2}\times 13.606\ \text{eV},\qquad a^* = \frac{\varepsilon}{m^*}\times 0.5292\ \text{Å}\]

Because the binding energy is comparable to \(k_BT\) at room temperature (25.9 meV), donors are almost fully ionised, which makes doping work. They "freeze out" only at low temperature.

Central-cell correction. Real donors in silicon are bound more strongly than effective-mass theory predicts: P binds by 45.6 meV, As by 53.8 meV, Sb by 42.7 meV, against about 31 meV from the anisotropic effective-mass theory of Kohn and Luttinger. The difference comes from the first few Å, where the potential is not \(-e^2/\varepsilon r\). The excited p states, which vanish at the origin, agree with effective-mass theory. The lab models this with a short-range Gaussian fitted to the 1s energy. In silicon the six conduction-band valleys also split 1s into A₁, T₂ and E (valley–orbit splitting), and the mass anisotropy splits 2p into 2p₀ and 2p±. Acceptors are the hole analogue, complicated by degenerate valence bands.

4. Wannier–Mott Excitons and the Elliott Formula

When a photon lifts an electron across the gap, the electron and the hole attract each other. In semiconductors with small \(m^*\) and large \(\varepsilon\) the pair is large (Wannier–Mott exciton) and obeys the hydrogenic equation with the reduced mass \(\mu = m_e m_h/(m_e + m_h)\). In ionic crystals and molecular solids it is localised on one site (Frenkel exciton).

The optical absorption near the gap (Elliott 1957) contains a series of lines below \(E_g\) and an enhanced continuum above it:

\[\alpha(\hbar\omega) \propto \sum_n \frac{4\pi}{n^3}\,\mathrm{Ry}^{*3/2}\,\delta\!\left(\hbar\omega - E_g + \frac{\mathrm{Ry}^*}{n^2}\right) + \theta(\hbar\omega - E_g)\,\frac{\pi\sqrt{\mathrm{Ry}^*}\,e^{\pi x}}{\sinh \pi x},\qquad x = \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Ry}^*}{\hbar\omega - E_g}}\]

The line strengths follow \(|\psi_n(0)|^2 \propto 1/n^3\): only s excitons are bright in an allowed transition. Above the gap the Sommerfeld factor makes the absorption jump to a finite value at \(E_g\) instead of rising as \(\sqrt{\hbar\omega - E_g}\). The lines merge continuously into the continuum, so absorption is smooth across \(E_g\). In GaAs \(\mathrm{Ry}^* \approx 4.2\) meV, and only \(n \le 3\) is resolved.

5. Rydberg Excitons in Cu₂O

Cuprous oxide has a direct gap of 2.172 eV and an exciton Rydberg of about 90 meV, twenty times that of GaAs. Its highest valence band and lowest conduction band have the same (positive) parity. The direct band-to-band dipole transition is therefore forbidden, and the optically allowed excitons are the P states, with strength

\[f_n \propto |R_n'(0)|^2 \propto \frac{n^2 - 1}{n^5}\]

In 2014 Kazimierczuk et al. resolved this "yellow" series up to \(n = 25\) in a natural crystal; later experiments at millikelvin temperatures reached \(n \approx 28\)–29. The size grows as \(n^2a^*\): the \(n = 25\) exciton spans about 2 μm, more than 10 billion unit cells and larger than the wavelength of the light that creates it. Like Rydberg atoms, such excitons interact strongly: one exciton blocks the creation of another nearby (Rydberg blockade).

6. Excitons in Two Dimensions

For a strictly 2D Coulomb problem the radial equation has the centrifugal term \((m^2 - \tfrac14)/r^2\), and the spectrum is

\[E_n = -\frac{\mathrm{Ry}^*}{\left(n - \tfrac12\right)^2},\qquad n = 1, 2, \dots\]

The 1s state is bound four times more strongly than in 3D, and its radius is half as large. Narrow quantum wells approach this limit.

In an atomically thin layer (a transition-metal dichalcogenide monolayer such as WS₂ or MoS₂) the field lines between electron and hole run mostly outside the layer, where screening is weak. The result is the Rytova–Keldysh potential:

\[V(r) = -\frac{\pi e^2}{2\bar\varepsilon r_0}\left[H_0\!\left(\frac{r}{r_0}\right) - Y_0\!\left(\frac{r}{r_0}\right)\right],\qquad r_0 = 2\pi\chi_{2D}\]

Here \(H_0\) is the Struve function, \(Y_0\) the Neumann function, \(\bar\varepsilon\) the average dielectric constant of the surroundings, and \(\chi_{2D}\) the 2D polarisability of the sheet. For \(r \gg r_0\) it is \(-e^2/\bar\varepsilon r\). For \(r \ll r_0\) it is only logarithmic, which binds the 1s exciton much less strongly than the Coulomb law would and the excited states relatively more.

Chernikov et al. (2014) measured a binding of 0.32 eV for the A exciton in monolayer WS₂ and found that the s states do not follow the hydrogenic series. Such large binding energies make excitons stable at room temperature.

7. Quantum Dots

A nanocrystal smaller than the exciton Bohr radius confines the electron and hole like particles in a spherical box. With an infinite barrier the levels are \(E_{nl} = \hbar^2x_{nl}^2/2m^*R^2\), with \(x_{nl}\) the zeros of the spherical Bessel functions (\(\pi\), 4.493, 5.763… for 1S, 1P, 1D). The optical gap is approximately (Brus 1984)

\[E \approx E_g + \frac{\hbar^2\pi^2}{2R^2}\left(\frac{1}{m_e} + \frac{1}{m_h}\right) - 1.786\,\frac{e^2}{\varepsilon R}\]

Smaller dots are bluer: CdSe nanocrystals span the visible spectrum between \(R \approx 1\) and 4 nm. A finite barrier (the electron affinity, a few eV) lowers the levels and lets the wavefunction leak out. Ekimov, Brus and Bawendi received the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery and synthesis of quantum dots.

8. Heterostructures and Transfer Matrices

Epitaxy grows layers of different semiconductors with atomically sharp interfaces. The conduction-band edge then becomes a piecewise-constant potential, and so does the effective mass. For GaAs/AlxGa1−xAs the band offset \(\Delta E_c\) is about \(0.67\,\Delta E_g \approx 0.83x\) eV (\(x \lt 0.45\)), and \(m^* \approx 0.067 + 0.083x\).

With a position-dependent mass the kinetic energy must be written \(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2}\,\partial_z \frac{1}{m^*}\,\partial_z\) to stay Hermitian. The matching conditions at an interface are then (BenDaniel and Duke 1966):

\[\psi \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{1}{m^*}\frac{d\psi}{dz}\quad\text{continuous}\]

Inside a layer of constant \(V\) and \(m^*\) the state vector \((\psi, \psi'/m^*)\) is propagated by a 2 × 2 matrix:

\[P = \begin{pmatrix} \cos kd & \dfrac{m^*}{k}\sin kd \\[4pt] -\dfrac{k}{m^*}\sin kd & \cos kd \end{pmatrix},\qquad \hbar^2k^2 = 2m^*(E - V)\]

(cosh and sinh in barriers). The matrix for the whole structure is the product \(M = P_N \cdots P_1\), with \(\det M = 1\). Bound states, transmission resonances, minibands and complex resonance poles all follow from \(M\) with different boundary conditions in the leads. A linear potential (applied field) is cut into thin slices.

9. Quantum Wells and the Stark Effect

A layer of GaAs between AlGaAs barriers is a finite square well. Its levels lie below the infinite-well values \(\hbar^2\pi^2n^2/2m^*L^2\) because the wavefunction leaks into the barriers over a decay length \(1/\kappa\). A symmetric 1D well always has at least one bound state. In the absorption spectrum each level pair produces a step (2D density of states), topped by a strong exciton line.

Quantum-confined Stark effect. An electric field perpendicular to the well tilts it. The electron level moves down and the hole level up, so the absorption edge shifts to lower energy by tens of meV. The barriers keep the exciton bound even in fields that would ionise it in bulk (Miller et al. 1984). This is the principle of the electro-absorption modulators used in optical communication.

Coupled wells. Two wells separated by a thin barrier form "molecular" bonding and antibonding states. The splitting \(\Delta = 2t\) measures the tunnel coupling: an electron placed in one well oscillates between them with period \(h/\Delta\).

10. Superlattices and Minibands

For a periodic structure of period \(d\), Bloch's theorem requires \(\psi(z + d) = e^{iqd}\psi(z)\). This gives

\[\cos qd = \tfrac12\,\mathrm{Tr}\,M(E)\]

Energies with \(|\tfrac12\,\mathrm{Tr}\,M| \le 1\) form allowed bands, the others gaps. With free electrons and square barriers this is the Kronig–Penney model (1931), the simplest model of band structure. In semiconductor superlattices (Esaki and Tsu 1970) the bands are narrow minibands, with widths set by tunnelling through the barriers. Their effective masses are heavy, because the electron must tunnel to move along the growth axis.

A finite stack of \(N\) wells has \(N\) transmission resonances in each miniband, which merge into the band as \(N \to \infty\). In a field \(F\) an electron in a miniband would perform Bloch oscillations with period \(T_B = h/eFd\): about 0.6 ps for \(d = 70\) Å and 10 kV/cm. That is short enough to be observed before scattering, unlike in natural crystals.

11. Tunnelling and Resonant Tunnelling

A particle below a barrier of height \(V\) and width \(d\) is transmitted with probability

\[T = \left[1 + \frac{V^2\sinh^2(\kappa d)}{4E(V - E)}\right]^{-1} \approx \frac{16E(V - E)}{V^2}\,e^{-2\kappa d},\qquad \hbar\kappa = \sqrt{2m^*(V - E)}\]

Two barriers enclose a well whose level is no longer bound: it can leak out through either barrier with rates \(\Gamma_L/\hbar\) and \(\Gamma_R/\hbar\). It is a quasi-bound state. Near its energy the transmission has the Breit–Wigner form

\[T(E) = \frac{\Gamma_L\Gamma_R}{(E - E_r)^2 + (\Gamma_L + \Gamma_R)^2/4},\qquad T_{\max} = \frac{4\Gamma_L\Gamma_R}{(\Gamma_L + \Gamma_R)^2}\]

For symmetric barriers \(T\) reaches 1 at resonance, however opaque each barrier is alone: the wave builds up inside the well until its leakage through the second barrier matches the incoming flux. The width \(\Gamma = \Gamma_L + \Gamma_R\) gives the lifetime \(\tau = \hbar/\Gamma\). It is also \(-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,E\) of the S-matrix pole, which the lab finds independently by solving the transfer-matrix problem with outgoing waves on both sides at complex energy. The phase delay \(\hbar\,d\varphi/dE\) at the peak is \(2\tau\). Thicker or higher barriers give exponentially narrower resonances and longer-lived states.

12. The Tsu–Esaki Current and Negative Differential Resistance

For coherent tunnelling the current density through a layered structure is (Tsu and Esaki 1973)

\[J = \frac{e\,m^*k_BT}{2\pi^2\hbar^3}\int T(E_z, V)\,\ln\!\left[\frac{1 + e^{(\mu - E_z)/k_BT}}{1 + e^{(\mu - eV - E_z)/k_BT}}\right]dE_z\]

where the logarithm counts the electrons with longitudinal energy \(E_z\) in the emitter, minus those in the collector. Under bias the resonance moves down relative to the emitter. Current flows while it lies between the emitter conduction-band edge and the Fermi level, and drops sharply once it falls below the band edge. This gives a peak, a valley and negative differential resistance (\(dJ/dV \lt 0\)) between them.

Chang, Esaki and Tsu observed this in 1974. Sollner et al. (1983) showed that double-barrier diodes oscillate at terahertz frequencies, and resonant-tunnelling diodes remain among the fastest electronic devices. The model here neglects space charge in the well, scattering, and the electrons' parallel motion beyond the 2D phase-space factor, so peak positions and peak-to-valley ratios are idealised.

13. Impurity Scattering in Metals

A conduction electron at the Fermi energy scattered by a spherical impurity is described by phase shifts \(\delta_l(E_F)\). Two results follow from them.

Friedel sum rule. A metal screens any charge completely. An impurity with valence difference \(Z\) (Zn, Ga, Ge, As in Cu: \(Z = 1\)–4) must therefore accumulate exactly \(Z\) extra electrons around itself. By Levinson's theorem and the Friedel–Lloyd formula the number of states added below \(E_F\) is (the factor 2 is for spin)

\[\Delta N(E_F) = \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_l (2l + 1)\,\delta_l(E_F) = Z\]

This condition fixes the strength of any model potential, so the resistivity follows without further parameters.

Residual resistivity. The resistivity at \(T \to 0\) comes from the transport cross section, which weights backscattering:

\[\sigma_{tr} = \frac{4\pi}{k_F^2}\sum_l (l + 1)\sin^2(\delta_l - \delta_{l+1}),\qquad \Delta\rho = \frac{n_{imp}}{n}\,\frac{\hbar k_F\,\sigma_{tr}}{e^2}\]

For Cu with 1 at.% of impurities the prefactor \(4\pi\hbar/(e^2k_F)\) is 3.8 μΩ cm, the value reached if all the screening were done by the s wave with \(\delta_0 = \pi/2\). Linde's rule states that the impurity-induced increase in residual resistivity is proportional to the square of the valence difference between host and solute. In the Born limit the phase shifts are small and proportional to \(Z\), so \(\Delta\rho \propto Z^2\). The Friedel calculation explains both the rule and the departures from it once the phase shifts are no longer small.

14. Virtual Bound States

A transition-metal impurity (Mn, Fe, Co…) in a simple metal such as Al has a 3d level that lies inside the conduction band. Kept apart from the band, it would be bound. Coupled to it, it becomes a resonance in the \(l = 2\) channel, trapped behind the centrifugal barrier: a virtual bound state (Friedel 1956, Anderson 1961). The phase shift \(\delta_2\) rises through \(\pi/2\) at the resonance energy \(E_d\). The density of states gains a Lorentzian of width \(\Gamma\) holding \(2(2l + 1) = 10\) states:

\[\Delta\rho(E) = \frac{2}{\pi}(2l + 1)\frac{d\delta_l}{dE} \approx \frac{10}{\pi}\,\frac{\Gamma/2}{(E - E_d)^2 + \Gamma^2/4}\]

When \(E_d\) is close to \(E_F\) the resistivity is large and changes strongly along the 3d series (the "double hump" of 3d impurities in Al). With strong Coulomb repulsion on the d level the impurity can carry a local moment (Anderson model), which leads to the Kondo effect at low temperature. That many-body physics is beyond this one-electron model.

15. Friedel Oscillations

Because the Fermi sea has a sharp edge at \(k_F\), the screening charge around an impurity does not decay smoothly. It oscillates with wavelength \(\pi/k_F\):

\[\Delta n(r) \approx \frac{1}{2\pi^2r^3}\sum_l (2l + 1)(-1)^l\sin\delta_l\,\cos(2k_Fr + \delta_l)\]

These Friedel oscillations are seen around adatoms with the scanning tunnelling microscope, as standing waves of surface electrons. They give the indirect RKKY exchange between magnetic impurities, and their \(2k_F\) singularity drives the Kohn anomaly in phonon spectra.

16.1 Bound states: Numerov, finite differences and DVR

Numerov. The radial equation is written as \(u'' = f(r)\,u\) with \(f = 2\mu[V(r) - E]/\hbar^2 + l(l+1)/r^2\). On a uniform grid of step \(h\) the Numerov recurrence

\[\left(1 - \frac{h^2}{12}f_{i+1}\right)u_{i+1} = 2\left(1 + \frac{5h^2}{12}f_i\right)u_i - \left(1 - \frac{h^2}{12}f_{i-1}\right)u_{i-1} + O(h^6)\]

has a local error \(O(h^6)\) and a global error \(O(h^4)\). A Coulomb state has structure on the scale \(a^*/n^2\) near the origin and a tail out to \(\sim n^2a^*\), so the lab uses a logarithmic grid \(r = e^x\). With \(u(r) = r^{1/2}\phi(x)\) the equation becomes exactly

\[\phi''(x) = \left[\left(l + \tfrac12\right)^2 + \frac{2\mu r^2}{\hbar^2}\,\big(V(r) - E\big)\right]\phi(x),\]

which Numerov integrates in \(x\) with uniform \(\Delta x\). The \(n\)-th level is the energy at which the outward solution acquires its \(n\)-th node (Sturm oscillation theorem), found by bisection; the wavefunction joins an outward and an inward solution at the outermost turning point. The method needs no matrix and costs \(O(N)\) per energy.

Finite differences replace \(u''\) by \((u_{i+1} - 2u_i + u_{i-1})/h^2\). The Hamiltonian becomes a tridiagonal matrix whose eigenvalues are counted by a Sturm sequence. The error is \(O(h^2)\). Sinc-DVR (Colbert–Miller) represents the kinetic energy exactly in a basis of sinc functions on a uniform grid; for smooth potentials it converges exponentially, but it needs a dense \(N \times N\) diagonalisation (\(O(N^3)\)).

Numerov on the logarithmic grid reaches \(10^{-8}\) with a few hundred points and then levels off at a floor of about \(10^{-8}\), far below anything that matters physically. Finite differences on a uniform grid show the expected \(h^2\) law (the error falls 16-fold per factor 4 in \(N\)) and need \(\sim 10^4\) points for \(10^{-7}\). DVR, excellent for smooth potentials, converges slowly for s states here: the Coulomb cusp \(u \propto r(1 - r/a^*)\) at the origin is not smooth, and a uniform grid wastes points in the tail. The p state, which vanishes as \(r^2\), converges much faster with every method.

16.2 Layered structures: transfer matrices against finite differences

Inside a layer of constant \(V\) and \(m^*\) the solution is known exactly (plane waves or exponentials), so the transfer matrix introduces no discretisation error: the only errors are round-off and, for layers in an electric field, the staircase approximation of the slope (2 Å slices). Finite differences need the BenDaniel–Duke operator \(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2}\frac{d}{dz}\frac{1}{m^*}\frac{d}{dz}\) with the masses taken at the half points:

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2h^2}\left[\frac{\psi_{i+1} - \psi_i}{m^*_{i+1/2}} - \frac{\psi_i - \psi_{i-1}}{m^*_{i-1/2}}\right] + V_i\psi_i = E\psi_i\]

16.4 Quasi-bound states: three ways to the width

A resonance can be characterised (i) by the peak of \(T(E)\) and its half-maximum points, (ii) as a pole of the S-matrix, found by solving the transfer-matrix problem at complex energy \(E = E_r - i\Gamma/2\) with only outgoing waves in both leads (Newton iteration), or (iii) semiclassically, as an attempt frequency times the barrier transmission:

\[\Gamma \approx \sum_{j = L,R}\hbar\,\frac{v}{2L_{\text{eff}}}\,T_j,\qquad v = \frac{\hbar k}{m^*},\quad L_{\text{eff}} = L + \frac{2}{\kappa}\]

The pole and the line shape agree to \(4 \times 10^{-5}\) because the resonance is isolated and narrow (\(\Gamma/E_r \approx 4 \times 10^{-3}\)); for broad or overlapping resonances the line shape is no longer Lorentzian, and only the pole remains well defined. The semiclassical estimate gives the right order of magnitude, which is what it is for. Opening the well shifts the level down by 35 μeV, a small fraction of \(\Gamma\).

16.5 Scattering phase shifts and continuum states

For the metal impurities the radial equation is integrated outward with Numerov and matched at \(R_m\) to \(u = A[\hat{\jmath}_l(kr)\cos\delta_l - \hat{n}_l(kr)\sin\delta_l]\) (Riccati–Bessel functions). Numerov's \(O(h^4)\) holds only for smooth potentials: at a step of the square well the error drops to \(O(h^2)\), even with the potential averaged over the cell that contains the step.

Phase shifts are defined only modulo \(\pi\). The branch is fixed by following \(\delta_l(E)\) continuously from \(E \to 0\), where Levinson's theorem gives \(\delta_l(0) = n_b\pi\) for \(n_b\) bound states; the Friedel sum depends on this choice.

For the Coulomb continuum the same integration is matched to Coulomb functions. The density at the origin relative to a free wave reproduces the Sommerfeld factor \(2\pi\eta/(1 - e^{-2\pi\eta})\), \(\eta = 1/(ka^*)\), to 0.1 % or better (12.555 vs 12.566 at \(E = \mathrm{Ry}^*/4\), 6.2941 vs 6.2949 at \(E = \mathrm{Ry}^*\)); this enhancement is what lifts the absorption above the gap in the Elliott formula.

18. Practice Problems

1. Find the binding energy and Bohr radius of a donor in GaAs (\(m^* = 0.067\), \(\varepsilon = 12.9\)). Is it ionised at room temperature?
\(\mathrm{Ry}^* = 13.606 \times 0.067/166.4 = 5.48\) meV; \(a^* = 0.5292 \times 12.9/0.067 = 102\) Å, about 18 lattice constants. \(k_BT = 25.9\) meV \(\gg\) 5.5 meV at 300 K, so practically all donors are ionised; at 4 K (\(k_BT = 0.34\) meV) electrons freeze out onto them.
2. Estimate the ground level of a 100 Å GaAs well with infinite barriers.
\(E_1 = \hbar^2\pi^2/2m^*L^2 = 3.810 \times 9.870/(0.067 \times 10^4)\) eV = 56.1 meV. With 0.25 eV AlGaAs barriers the lab finds 30.5 meV: the wavefunction leaks about 14 Å into each barrier, so the effective width is larger.
3. Estimate the transmission of a 50 meV electron (\(m^* = 0.067\)) through a 50 Å, 0.3 eV barrier.
\(\kappa = \sqrt{0.067 \times 0.25/3.810}\) Å⁻¹ = 0.0663 Å⁻¹, so \(2\kappa d = 6.63\) and \(e^{-2\kappa d} = 1.32 \times 10^{-3}\). The prefactor \(16E(V - E)/V^2 = 2.22\) gives \(T \approx 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\), equal to the exact value to two figures.
4. A double-barrier resonance has \(\Gamma = 0.27\) meV. What is the lifetime of the quasi-bound state?
\(\tau = \hbar/\Gamma = 6.582 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{eV s}/2.7 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{eV} = 2.4\) ps. The electron bounces about 20 times between the barriers before escaping. \(\Gamma\) shrinks exponentially with barrier thickness.
5. What is the Bloch-oscillation period in a superlattice with \(d = 70\) Å in a field of 10 kV/cm?
\(eFd = 10^6\ \text{V/m} \times 7 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m} \times e = 7\) meV. \(T_B = h/eFd = 4.136 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{eV s}/7 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{eV} = 0.59\) ps, shorter than typical scattering times, so the oscillation can be seen (as THz emission).
6. How large is the \(n = 25\) P exciton in Cu₂O (\(a^* \approx 11\) Å)?
\(\langle r\rangle = a^*[3n^2 - l(l + 1)]/2 = 10.9\ \text{Å} \times (1875 - 2)/2 \approx 1.0\) μm, so its diameter is about 2 μm.
7. A Zn impurity (\(Z = 1\)) in Cu screened entirely by the s wave: what residual resistivity follows?
The Friedel sum gives \(\delta_0 = \pi/2\), so \(\sin^2(\delta_0 - \delta_1) = 1\). \(\Delta\rho = c \times 4\pi\hbar/(e^2k_F) = 0.01 \times 3.8 \times 10^{-6}\ \Omega\,\text{m}\) = 3.8 μΩ cm per at.%, more than ten times the measured 0.3. In reality the screening charge is shared among s, p and d waves with small phase shifts, which the square-well calculation reproduces.
8. Estimate the confinement energy of an electron–hole pair in a CdSe dot of radius 2 nm (\(m_e = 0.13\), \(m_h = 0.45\)).
\(\frac{\hbar^2\pi^2}{2R^2}\left(\frac{1}{m_e} + \frac{1}{m_h}\right) = (3.810 \times 9.870/400)\ \text{eV} \times (7.69 + 2.22) = 0.094\ \text{eV} \times 9.91 = 0.93\) eV. The Coulomb term \(1.786\,e^2/\varepsilon R = 1.786 \times 14.40/(9.6 \times 20)\) eV = 0.13 eV. Optical gap ≈ 1.74 + 0.93 − 0.13 = 2.54 eV: blue-green instead of near-infrared for bulk CdSe.

19. History

1931: Kronig and Penney's model of bands; Frenkel's exciton. 1937–38: Wannier and Mott describe large-radius excitons. 1950–52: the hydrogen-like exciton series is observed in Cu₂O (Hayashi; Gross). 1952–58: Friedel's sum rule, virtual bound states and oscillations. 1955: Kohn and Luttinger's effective-mass theory of donors in Si and Ge. 1957: Elliott's theory of exciton absorption; Esaki's tunnel diode (Nobel Prize 1973). 1961: Anderson's model of localised moments. 1966: BenDaniel–Duke boundary conditions. 1970: Esaki and Tsu propose semiconductor superlattices. 1974: resonant tunnelling observed (Chang, Esaki and Tsu); quantum-well optical spectra (Dingle, Wiegmann and Henry). 1981–84: semiconductor nanocrystals (Ekimov, Efros, Brus; Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2023). 1983: THz resonant-tunnelling diodes (Sollner et al.). 1984: quantum-confined Stark effect (Miller et al.). 2010: monolayer MoS₂ is found to be a direct-gap semiconductor. 2014: giant Rydberg excitons in Cu₂O (\(n = 25\)) and the non-hydrogenic exciton series of monolayer WS₂.

20. Limits of the Models

Effective-mass models. Isotropic, parabolic, single band: no valley degeneracy or valley–orbit splitting (silicon donors), no mass anisotropy, no valence-band mixing (acceptors and holes), no non-parabolicity. The dielectric constant is a constant: polaron effects (the choice between \(\varepsilon_0\) and \(\varepsilon_\infty\) for excitons) and exchange splittings are absent. The exciton absorption uses the Elliott formula with a phenomenological broadening.

Heterostructures. Conduction band only (Γ valley), constant masses in each layer, abrupt interfaces, no strain. The bias drops linearly, without charge accumulation in the well or depletion and accumulation layers, and transport is coherent: no phonon or interface-roughness scattering, which in real devices fill the valley current. AlAs barriers involve the X valley, not included.

Metals. Free electrons, a spherical Fermi surface and a single-site model potential whose radius is a free parameter; no lattice relaxation around the impurity and no many-body effects (Kondo). The Friedel oscillation formula is the asymptotic large-\(r\) form.

02

Quantum wells and superlattices

Describe the conduction-band profile as layers (thickness, band edge, effective mass, optionally a field), with semi-infinite leads on both sides. In bound-state mode the levels are the zeros of the transfer-matrix condition for decaying waves in both leads; the plot shows the wavefunctions on the band profile. In superlattice mode the layers form one period: the dispersion follows from cos(qd) = ½ Tr M, with the miniband edges, widths and the effective mass at the band bottom, and the transmission through a finite stack of N periods; the potential plot shows |ψ|² at the transmission resonances of the lowest miniband. In both modes the scattering-state plot draws Re ψ and |ψ| for a wave incident from the left at the energy given.

The transmission T(E) of the structure for electrons incident from the emitter, at an optional bias. An impurity with valence difference Z in a free-electron host (Cu, Ag, Al).
