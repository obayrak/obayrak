I. Quantum Foundations
The Central Problem
A crystal is a periodic arrangement of atoms in space. The potential energy felt by a conduction electron satisfies translation symmetry: shifting by any lattice vector \(\mathbf{R}\) leaves the Hamiltonian unchanged.
\[ V(\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{R}) = V(\mathbf{r}), \qquad \mathbf{R} = n_1\mathbf{a}_1 + n_2\mathbf{a}_2 + n_3\mathbf{a}_3, \quad n_i \in \mathbb{Z} \]
The time-independent Schrödinger equation to be solved is:
\[ \hat{H}\,\psi(\mathbf{r}) = \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla^2 + V(\mathbf{r})\right]\psi(\mathbf{r}) = E\,\psi(\mathbf{r}) \]
Bloch's Theorem
Because \(\hat{H}\) commutes with every lattice translation \(\hat{T}_{\mathbf{R}}\), their eigenstates can be chosen as simultaneous eigenstates of all translations. This forces the wavefunction into a unique factored form — Bloch's theorem:
\[ \boxed{\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}) = e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\,u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})} \qquad u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R}) = u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}) \]
Crystal Momentum k
The wavevector \(\mathbf{k}\) labels states within the first Brillouin zone. It is a conserved quantum number analogous to linear momentum, but defined only modulo a reciprocal lattice vector \(\mathbf{G}\).
Band Index n
At each \(\mathbf{k}\) there are infinitely many solutions \(E_n(\mathbf{k})\) labeled by a discrete index \(n = 1,2,3,\ldots\) Each forms a continuous energy band as \(\mathbf{k}\) varies through the BZ.
Periodic Part u
The function \(u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\) carries the same periodicity as the lattice. It encodes how electrons redistribute within the unit cell under the ionic potential.
Brillouin Zone
The first BZ is the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice — the set of \(\mathbf{k}\) closer to \(\mathbf{G}=0\) than to any other reciprocal lattice point. It contains exactly one k-state per unit cell.
The Reciprocal Lattice
For each real-space lattice with primitive vectors \(\{\mathbf{a}_i\}\) there is a dual reciprocal lattice with primitive vectors \(\{\mathbf{b}_j\}\) satisfying \(\mathbf{a}_i \cdot \mathbf{b}_j = 2\pi\delta_{ij}\). Any lattice-periodic function — including \(V(\mathbf{r})\) and \(u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\) — is exactly represented by a Fourier series over reciprocal lattice vectors \(\mathbf{G} = m_1\mathbf{b}_1 + m_2\mathbf{b}_2 + m_3\mathbf{b}_3\):
\[ V(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{\mathbf{G}} \tilde{V}_{\mathbf{G}}\,e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}, \qquad \tilde{V}_{\mathbf{G}} = \frac{1}{\Omega}\int_{\text{cell}} V(\mathbf{r})\,e^{-i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\,d^d\mathbf{r} \]
Here \(\Omega\) is the unit-cell volume (area in 2D, length in 1D). The Fourier coefficients \(\tilde{V}_{\mathbf{G}}\) are the fundamental coupling matrix elements between plane waves — their magnitude directly controls the size of band gaps.
II. 1D Band Structure — Step by Step
The one-dimensional case is the cleanest illustration of the plane-wave method. We solve for electrons in a chain of atoms with lattice constant \(a\), using the Kronig–Penney model as the worked example throughout.
1
Define and Fourier-Expand the Periodic Potential
Choose \(V(x)\) periodic with period \(a\). Expand it as a Fourier series over reciprocal lattice vectors \(G_m = 2\pi m/a\):
\[ V(x) = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} \tilde{V}_m\,e^{iG_m x}, \qquad \tilde{V}_m = \frac{1}{a}\int_0^a V(x)\,e^{-iG_m x}\,dx \]
For real, even potentials: \(\tilde{V}_{-m} = \tilde{V}_m \in \mathbb{R}\). The Kronig–Penney model places square barriers of height \(V_0\) and width \(b\) on every site, giving:
\[ \tilde{V}_0 = V_0\frac{b}{a}, \qquad \tilde{V}_{m\neq0} = V_0\frac{b}{a}\,\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{mb}{a}\right), \qquad \operatorname{sinc}(u) \equiv \frac{\sin(\pi u)}{\pi u} \]
Unlike the cosine potential (only \(\tilde{V}_{\pm1} \neq 0\)), the KP model has contributions from all harmonics, decaying algebraically as \(1/m\). This opens band gaps at every BZ boundary \(k = n\pi/a\).
Numerical example — \(V_0 = 5\,\text{eV}\), \(b/a = 0.20\), \(a = 3.5\,\text{Å}\):

\(\tilde{V}_0 = 1.000\,\text{eV}\),  \(\tilde{V}_1 = 0.935\,\text{eV}\) (\(\operatorname{sinc}(0.20)\)),  \(\tilde{V}_2 = 0.757\,\text{eV}\) (\(\operatorname{sinc}(0.40)\)),  \(\tilde{V}_3 = 0.505\,\text{eV}\) (\(\operatorname{sinc}(0.60)\)).

The slow \(\operatorname{sinc}\) decay means one must include \(N \geq 8\) harmonics for sub-percent accuracy at higher bands.
2
Apply the Bloch Ansatz and Choose a Plane-Wave Basis
Restrict \(\mathbf{k}\) to the first BZ: \(k \in [-\pi/a,\,+\pi/a]\). Since \(u_k(x)\) is periodic it admits a Fourier expansion, giving:
\[ \psi_k(x) = \sum_{m=-N}^{N} c_m\,e^{i(k+G_m)x} \]
We truncate at \(|m| \leq N\), yielding a basis of \(2N+1\) shifted plane waves. The free-electron kinetic energy of each basis state is:
\[ \varepsilon_m^{(0)}(k) = \frac{\hbar^2(k+G_m)^2}{2m_e} = E_0\!\left(\frac{ka}{\pi} + 2m\right)^2, \qquad E_0 = \frac{\hbar^2\pi^2}{2m_e a^2} = \frac{3.81\;\text{eV·Å}^2}{a^2} \]
\(E_0\) is the characteristic energy scale of the lattice. With \(a = 3.5\,\text{Å}\): \(E_0 = 0.311\,\text{eV}\). All band gaps and effective masses scale with this reference energy.
3
Derive the Central Equation
Substitute the plane-wave expansion into \(\hat{H}\psi = E\psi\). Using orthogonality of plane waves \(\langle e^{iG_m x}|e^{iG_{m'} x}\rangle = a\,\delta_{mm'}\), each Fourier component must independently satisfy:
\[ \frac{\hbar^2(k+G_m)^2}{2m_e}\,c_m + \sum_{m'} \tilde{V}_{m-m'}\,c_{m'} = E\,c_m \]
This is the Central Equation of band theory. The kinetic term is diagonal in the plane-wave basis; the potential \(\tilde{V}_{m-m'}\) couples two plane waves whose momenta differ by the reciprocal lattice vector \(G_{m-m'}\) — physically, Bragg scattering by the lattice.
4
Assemble the Hamiltonian Matrix H(k)
Collect all \(2N+1\) central equations into a single matrix eigenvalue problem \(\mathbf{H}(k)\,\mathbf{c} = E\,\mathbf{c}\):
\[ H_{mm'}(k) = \underbrace{E_0\!\left(\frac{ka}{\pi}+2m\right)^{\!2}\delta_{mm'}}_{\text{kinetic diagonal}} +\; \underbrace{\tilde{V}_{m-m'}}_{\text{potential off-diagonal}} \]
Written explicitly for \(N=2\) (a \(5\times5\) example, rows/columns ordered \(m\in\{-2,-1,0,+1,+2\}\)):
\[ \mathbf{H}(k) = \begin{pmatrix} \varepsilon_{-2} & \tilde{V}_1 & \tilde{V}_2 & \tilde{V}_3 & \tilde{V}_4 \\ \tilde{V}_1 & \varepsilon_{-1}& \tilde{V}_1 & \tilde{V}_2 & \tilde{V}_3 \\ \tilde{V}_2 & \tilde{V}_1 & \varepsilon_0 & \tilde{V}_1 & \tilde{V}_2 \\ \tilde{V}_3 & \tilde{V}_2 & \tilde{V}_1 & \varepsilon_1 & \tilde{V}_1 \\ \tilde{V}_4 & \tilde{V}_3 & \tilde{V}_2 & \tilde{V}_1 & \varepsilon_2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \varepsilon_m = E_0\!\left(\tfrac{ka}{\pi}+2m\right)^2 \]
Matrix structure: \(\mathbf{H}\) is real and symmetric for real even potentials (all eigenvalues are real). It is also centrosymmetric: \(H_{mm'} = H_{(-m)(-m')}\). The off-diagonal elements form a Toeplitz pattern — \(H_{mm'}\) depends only on the difference \(m-m'\), reflecting that the potential can only scatter electrons by a reciprocal lattice vector.
5
Diagonalize at Each k-point Across the Brillouin Zone
Scan \(k\) through \(N_k = 120\) equally-spaced points across the first BZ. At each \(k\), diagonalize \(\mathbf{H}(k)\) to obtain \(2N+1\) eigenvalues. Sorting in ascending order gives the \(n\)-th band:
\[ \mathbf{H}(k)\,\mathbf{c}^{(n)}(k) = E_n(k)\,\mathbf{c}^{(n)}(k), \qquad n = 1,2,\ldots,2N+1 \]
The resulting curves \(E_n(k)\) are the band structure. The eigenvectors \(\mathbf{c}^{(n)}\) give the plane-wave coefficients of the Bloch wavefunction, which this simulator reconstructs in real space on the POTENTIAL tab.
6
Interpret the Band Diagram — Gap Opening Mechanism
At the BZ boundary \(k = \pm\pi/a\), two free-electron states \(e^{+i\pi x/a}\) and \(e^{-i\pi x/a}\) are degenerate with energy \(E_0\). The potential \(\tilde{V}_1\) couples them, splitting the degeneracy by degenerate perturbation theory:
\[ \psi_{\pm}(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\!\left(e^{+i\pi x/a} \pm e^{-i\pi x/a}\right) = \begin{cases} \sqrt{2}\cos(\pi x/a) \\ \sqrt{2}\,i\sin(\pi x/a) \end{cases}, \qquad \Delta E_\text{gap}^{(1)} = 2|\tilde{V}_1| \]
The cosine state \(\psi_+\) concentrates charge at potential minima (lower energy); the sine state \(\psi_-\) concentrates at potential maxima (higher energy). This charge redistribution is the physical origin of every band gap. For the KP model with the numbers above: \(\Delta E_\text{gap}^{(1)} = 2\times 0.935 = 1.87\,\text{eV}\).
Nearly-Free Electron Limit \((V_0 \ll E_0)\)
When the potential is weak, first-order degenerate perturbation theory gives the gap at the \(n\)-th BZ boundary \(k = n\pi/a\):
\[ E_\pm = n^2 E_0 \pm |\tilde{V}_n|, \qquad \Delta E_\text{gap}^{(n)} = 2|\tilde{V}_n| \]
Higher-order gaps are smaller because \(|\tilde{V}_n|\) decays with \(n\). The undistorted free-electron parabola \(E = \hbar^2k^2/2m_e\) (toggle Show free electron) is the \(V_0\to0\) limit — visible as faint dashed curves in the band diagram.
Tight-Binding Limit \((V_0 \gg E_0)\)
When wells are very deep, electrons are nearly localized on atomic sites. Bandwidth is small and the lowest band follows a cosine dispersion:
\[ E(k) \approx E_0^{\text{atom}} - 2t\cos(ka) \]
where \(t\) is the hopping integral (wavefunction overlap between neighboring sites). Bandwidth \(= 4t\); gap grows as \(V_0\) increases. The plane-wave method reproduces this limit automatically when \(V_0\) is large — no separate formulation is needed.
III. Periodic Potential Types
The shape of \(V(x)\) determines the Fourier coefficients \(\tilde{V}_m\) and hence the number and magnitude of band gaps. Every potential model implemented in this simulator is listed below with its exact Fourier spectrum.
① Cosine — \(V(x) = V_0\cos(2\pi x/a)\)
\[ \tilde{V}_{\pm1} = \frac{V_0}{2}, \qquad \tilde{V}_m = 0\;\text{ for } |m|\neq1 \]
Only the first Fourier harmonic is non-zero. Equivalent to the Mathieu differential equation. Opens exactly one gap \(\Delta E = V_0\) at the first BZ boundary; all higher-order gaps vanish identically. Fastest-converging plane-wave expansion: \(N=1\) is already exact.
② Kronig–Penney — Rectangular Barrier Array
\[ V(x) = \begin{cases}V_0 & x\bmod a < b\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}, \qquad \tilde{V}_m = V_0\frac{b}{a}\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{mb}{a}\right) \]
The foundational textbook model (Kronig & Penney, 1931). All Fourier harmonics contribute; amplitude decays as \(\operatorname{sinc}(mb/a)\), opening gaps at every BZ boundary. Width ratio \(b/a\) controls relative gap sizes. Requires \(N \geq 8\) for accurate higher bands.
③ Square Wells — Attractive Well Array
\[ \tilde{V}_m = -V_0\frac{b}{a}\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{mb}{a}\right) \]
Identical to KP but with \(V_0 \to -V_0\) — attractive wells instead of repulsive barriers. Models bound states at ionic cores. At large \(V_0\), the deepest band collapses toward bound atomic levels.
④ Gaussian Lattice — Ionic Model
\[ V(x) = V_0\sum_n e^{-(x-na)^2/2\sigma^2}, \qquad \tilde{V}_m = \frac{V_0\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}{a} \exp\!\left(-\frac{2\pi^2m^2\sigma^2}{a^2}\right) \]
Models the smoothed Coulomb potential of an ionic core. Fourier coefficients decay as a Gaussian in \(m\), so the plane-wave expansion converges very rapidly. Narrow spread \(\sigma\) → many significant harmonics; wide \(\sigma\) → approaches the cosine limit.
⑤ Optical Lattice — \(V(x) = V_0\cos^2(\pi x/a)\)
\[ V(x) = \frac{V_0}{2}\!\left[1+\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)\right], \qquad \tilde{V}_0 = \frac{V_0}{2},\quad \tilde{V}_{\pm1} = \frac{V_0}{4} \]
Standard potential for ultracold atoms trapped in laser standing waves. The constant offset \(\tilde{V}_0\) shifts all energies uniformly without affecting band gaps. Coupling strength is half that of a cosine of the same amplitude.
⑥ Mathieu — \(V(x) = V_0\!\left[\cos(2\pi x/a) + \tfrac{1}{2}\cos(4\pi x/a)\right]\)
\[ \tilde{V}_{\pm1} = \frac{V_0}{2},\quad \tilde{V}_{\pm2} = \frac{V_0}{4},\quad \tilde{V}_m = 0\;\text{ for }|m|\geq3 \]
The added second harmonic opens an independent gap at the second BZ boundary \(k = 2\pi/a\). Demonstrates directly how each higher Fourier component controls the gap at the corresponding BZ boundary.
⑦ Sin² — \(V(x) = V_0\sin^2(\pi x/a)\)
\[ \tilde{V}_0 = \frac{V_0}{2},\quad \tilde{V}_{\pm2} = -\frac{V_0}{4},\quad \tilde{V}_{\pm1} = 0 \]
The first harmonic vanishes; the leading coupling is at \(n=2\), so the primary gap opens at the second BZ boundary instead of the first. Illustrates that gap location is controlled by which harmonic dominates — not the amplitude alone.
IV. 2D Band Structure — Step by Step
In two dimensions the plane-wave method is identical in structure to 1D, but the geometry is richer: the BZ shape depends on the lattice type, and the k-path must be chosen to trace the most informative high-symmetry lines.
1
Choose the 2D Bravais Lattice and Construct the Reciprocal Lattice
The two most important 2D lattices are the square and hexagonal (triangular). Their primitive vectors and the resulting BZ shape are:
Lattice Real-space \(\mathbf{a}_1,\,\mathbf{a}_2\) Reciprocal \(\mathbf{b}_1,\,\mathbf{b}_2\) BZ shape
Square a(1,0),  a(0,1) (2π/a)(1,0),  (2π/a)(0,1) Square
Hexagonal a(1,0),  a(½,√3/2) (2π/a)(1,−1/√3),  (2π/a)(0,2/√3) Regular hexagon
The reciprocal primitive vectors satisfy \(\mathbf{b}_i\cdot\mathbf{a}_j = 2\pi\delta_{ij}\). All 2D G-vectors are \(\mathbf{G}_{mn} = m\mathbf{b}_1 + n\mathbf{b}_2\).
2

Rather than mapping the full 2D BZ, the band structure is plotted along lines connecting the special symmetry points at corners, edge midpoints, and center.

3
Generate the 2D Plane-Wave Basis
Enumerate all G-vectors up to cutoff \(|m|,|n| \leq N_\text{cut} = 3\). Convert each to Cartesian coordinates:
\text{Square:}\quad \mathbf{G}_{mn} = \frac{2\pi}{a}(m,\,n) \qquad \text{Hexagonal:}\quad \mathbf{G}_{mn} = \frac{2\pi}{a}\!\left(m,\;\frac{2n-m}{\sqrt{3}}\right)
With \(N_\text{cut}=3\): \((2\cdot3+1)^2 = 49\) basis states. This is sufficient for accurate computation of the first 6–8 bands.
4
Build and Diagonalize the 2D Hamiltonian
The \(49\times49\) Hamiltonian at each 2D k-point \(\mathbf{k}=(k_x,k_y)\) is:
\[ H_{ij}(\mathbf{k}) = \frac{\hbar^2|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}_i|^2}{2m_e}\,\delta_{ij} + \tilde{V}_{\mathbf{G}_i - \mathbf{G}_j} \]
In dimensionless units (k and G in \(2\pi/a\), energies in eV), the kinetic term is \(4E_0\,|(k_x+G_{ix})^2+(k_y+G_{iy})^2|\).
2D Fourier Coupling Structure
Square: Separable
For a separable potential \(V(x,y)=f(x)+f(y)\), coupling is non-zero only when \(m=0\) or \(n=0\) (but not both). Cross-coupling terms vanish; x and y directions decouple.
Hexagonal: 6-fold
The 6 nearest-neighbor G-vectors form a shell with \(m^2-mn+n^2=1\). All 6 couple with the same amplitude \(\tilde{V}_1\), enforcing the 6-fold rotational symmetry of the BZ.
Shell Criterion
Coupling strength depends only on \(|\Delta\mathbf{G}|^2\). For hex: nearest shell at \(|\mathbf{G}|^2 = \tfrac{4}{3}(2\pi/a)^2\). For square: nearest at \(|\mathbf{G}|^2 = (2\pi/a)^2\).
5
Signature Features of 2D Band Structures
The 2D band structure reveals physical phenomena absent in 1D:
Γ Point — Band Extremum
At \(\Gamma\), \(\mathbf{k}=0\) and all bands have extrema. Curvature in all directions gives the isotropic effective mass. The lowest band minimum is always at \(\Gamma\) for weak potentials.
X Point — Charge Density Wave
At X in the square lattice, two free-electron states degenerate at \(E_0\) split by \(\Delta E = 2|\tilde{V}_{10}|\). The resulting charge-density wave has wavevector \(\mathbf{Q}=(2\pi/a,0)\).
K Point — Dirac Cone
At K in the hexagonal lattice, three states are degenerate. With a honeycomb sublattice, one pair forms a linear touching \(E \propto |\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{K}|\) — a massless Dirac cone, as in graphene.
M Point — Saddle Point
At M, the dispersion is a saddle point — concave along \(\Gamma\text{–}M\) and convex along \(M\text{–}K\). This creates a logarithmic Van Hove singularity in the 2D DOS.

Three-dimensional band structure uses the identical plane-wave formalism, but the BZ geometry is more complex and the matrix is significantly larger: \((2N_\text{cut}+1)^3\times(2N_\text{cut}+1)^3\). This simulator uses \(N_\text{cut}=1\), giving a compact \(27\times27\) matrix that captures the first four bands and the zone-boundary gaps correctly.

Unique Phenomena in 3D Band Structures
Band Sticking
Along certain high-symmetry lines, crystal symmetry (group of the wavevector) forces multiple bands to be degenerate regardless of potential strength. No avoided crossing occurs.
Anisotropic Effective Mass
In 3D the effective mass is a tensor: \((m^*)^{-1}_{ij} = \hbar^{-2}\partial^2E/\partial k_i\partial k_j\). Longitudinal and transverse masses differ, e.g., Si has \(m^*_l = 0.92\,m_e\) but \(m^*_t = 0.19\,m_e\).
Indirect Band Gap
The conduction band minimum need not occur at the same k as the valence band maximum. Si and Ge are indirect-gap semiconductors; optical transitions require a phonon to conserve crystal momentum.
Spin–Orbit Coupling
In heavy elements the relativistic term \(\propto(\nabla V)\times\mathbf{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\) lifts spin degeneracy at high-symmetry points and along lines, producing band splittings essential for topological effects.
VI. Numerical Eigenvalue Method
At each k-point we solve the real symmetric eigenvalue problem \(\mathbf{H}(k)\,\mathbf{c} = E\,\mathbf{c}\). This simulator uses the Jacobi iterative algorithm, optimal for dense symmetric matrices up to dimension \(\sim\!100\).
Jacobi Rotation Algorithm
The algorithm applies a sequence of plane (Givens) rotations \(\mathbf{J}(p,q,\theta)\) that annihilate one off-diagonal element at a time while preserving all eigenvalues (orthogonal transformations leave the spectrum unchanged):
\[ \mathbf{H}' = \mathbf{J}^T\mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{J}, \qquad \text{choose } \theta \text{ so that } H'_{pq} = 0 \]
The rotation angle for each pair \((p,q)\) satisfies:
\[ \tau = \frac{H_{qq}-H_{pp}}{2H_{pq}}, \qquad t = \frac{\operatorname{sign}(\tau)}{|\tau|+\sqrt{1+\tau^2}}, \qquad c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^2}},\quad s = tc \]
Numerical stability: The formula \(t = \operatorname{sign}(\tau)/(|\tau|+\sqrt{1+\tau^2})\) always gives \(|t|\leq1\), avoiding the cancellation error in the naive \(\tan(2\theta) = 2H_{pq}/(H_{pp}-H_{qq})\) expression when \(H_{pp}\approx H_{qq}\).
Algorithm
function jacobi_eigen(H[n×n], needVectors, maxSweeps = 60):
  V ← Identity(n) // accumulate rotations → eigenvectors
  for sweep = 1 to maxSweeps:
    maxOff ← max|H[i,j]| for i ≠ j
    if maxOff < 1e-12: break // converged
    for p = 0 to n−2:
      for q = p+1 to n−1:
        if |H[p,q]| < 1e-15: continue // already small
        τ ← (H[q,q] − H[p,p]) / (2·H[p,q])
        t ← sign(τ) / (|τ| + √(1+τ²)) // numerically stable tan θ
        c ← 1/√(1+t²), s ← t·c
        apply Givens rotation J(p,q) to H and V
  eigenvalues ← sort( diag(H) ) // diagonal entries after convergence
  if needVectors: eigenvectors ← columns of V reordered by energy
  return eigenvalues, eigenvectors
Convergence and Computational Cost
Each sweep visits all \(n(n-1)/2\) off-diagonal pairs. The off-diagonal Frobenius norm decreases each sweep by a factor \(\leq 1 - 2/(n^2-n)\), giving quadratic ultimate convergence. In practice 5–30 sweeps suffice.

Plane-Wave Cutoff and Convergence
The number of plane waves controls accuracy. For smooth potentials (cosine, Gaussian) convergence is exponential in \(N\). For discontinuous potentials (Kronig–Penney, square wells), convergence is algebraic — more waves are required.
\[ E_\text{cut} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\!\left(\frac{2\pi N_\text{cut}}{a}\right)^2 \qquad \text{(kinetic energy of the highest included plane wave)} \]
Production DFT codes (VASP, Quantum ESPRESSO, Abinit) use \(E_\text{cut} = 300\text{–}600\,\text{eV}\) for transition metals, corresponding to \(10^4\text{–}10^5\) plane waves. Diagonalization uses iterative Krylov methods (Davidson, LOBPCG) that find only the \(N_\text{occ}\) lowest eigenpairs in \(\mathcal{O}(N_\text{PW}\cdot N_\text{occ}^2)\) time — far more efficient than full diagonalization.
VII. Physical Observables from Band Structure
1. Band Gap
The fundamental band gap \(E_g\) separates the valence band maximum (VBM) from the conduction band minimum (CBM). It is the most critical quantity for electronic and optical device applications:
\[ E_g = \min_\mathbf{k} E_{n+1}(\mathbf{k}) - \max_\mathbf{k} E_n(\mathbf{k}) \]
Metal (\(E_g = 0\))
Bands overlap or Fermi level crosses a band. Free carriers at \(T=0\). Examples: Cu (overlap at L), Al, Na.
Semiconductor (\(E_g \lesssim 3\,\text{eV}\))
Thermally activated carriers. Si: 1.12 eV (indirect), GaAs: 1.42 eV (direct), Ge: 0.67 eV.
Insulator (\(E_g \gtrsim 3\,\text{eV}\))
Negligible conductivity at RT. SiO₂: 9 eV, diamond: 5.5 eV, MgO: 7.8 eV, AlN: 6.2 eV.
Direct vs indirect gap: If VBM and CBM occur at the same \(\mathbf{k}\), the gap is direct (GaAs, InP, GaN) — efficient optical transitions, ideal for LEDs and lasers. If at different \(\mathbf{k}\) points, it is indirect (Si, Ge, AlAs) — optical transitions require a phonon to conserve crystal momentum, making them inefficient light emitters but excellent solar-cell absorbers.
2. Effective Mass
Near a band extremum at \(\mathbf{k}_0\), the dispersion is approximately parabolic. The effective mass \(m^*\) captures how the crystal potential modifies electron inertia, controlling carrier mobility, optical absorption onset, and transport:
\[ E_n(\mathbf{k}) \approx E_n(\mathbf{k}_0) + \frac{\hbar^2(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}_0)^2}{2m^*}, \qquad \frac{1}{m^*} = \frac{1}{\hbar^2}\frac{d^2E_n}{dk^2} \]
Computed numerically in this simulator by a centred second difference at the zone boundary index \(i_0\):
\[ \left.\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}\right|_{k_0} \approx \frac{E_{i_0+1} - 2E_{i_0} + E_{i_0-1}}{(\delta k)^2} \]
\(m^* > 0\) at band bottoms (electron-like carriers); \(m^* < 0\) at band tops (hole-like). \(|m^*| \ll m_e\) implies highly mobile carriers: GaAs conduction band \(m^* = 0.067\,m_e\); GaN: \(0.20\,m_e\); Si: \(0.19\,m_e\) (transverse), \(0.92\,m_e\) (longitudinal).
3. Density of States
The DOS \(g(E)\) counts the number of electronic states per unit energy per unit volume. It is obtained by integrating a delta function over the BZ:
\[ g(E) = \frac{2}{(2\pi)^d}\sum_n \int_\text{BZ} \delta\!\left(E - E_n(\mathbf{k})\right)d^d\mathbf{k} \]
In this simulator the delta function is replaced by a Gaussian of width \(\sigma_\text{dos} = E_\text{max}/60\) (Gaussian smearing):
\[ g(E) \approx \frac{2}{N_k}\sum_{n,k} \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(E-E_n(k))^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]
Van Hove singularities appear wherever \(\nabla_\mathbf{k}E_n(\mathbf{k})=0\) (band edges, saddle points). Their form depends on dimension: 1D — inverse square-root divergences; 2D — logarithmic divergences; 3D — square-root onsets with jumps at saddle points. These singularities directly determine optical absorption spectra and are visible as sharp features in the DOS panel.
4. Group Velocity and Semiclassical Transport
The semiclassical velocity of a Bloch wavepacket (averaged over the unit cell) is the gradient of the band dispersion:
\[ \mathbf{v}_n(\mathbf{k}) = \frac{1}{\hbar}\nabla_\mathbf{k} E_n(\mathbf{k}) \]
At band extrema \(\mathbf{v} = 0\). At the BZ boundary \(k=\pi/a\), \(dE/dk = 0\) for any periodic potential — this is the Bragg reflection condition: the electron cannot propagate at that wavevector. Conductivity is proportional to \(\sum_n \int (v_{n,x})^2 (-\partial f/\partial E) d^dk\) where \(f\) is the Fermi–Dirac distribution.
5. Fermi Level and Band Filling
At \(T=0\), electrons fill all states up to the Fermi energy \(E_F\), determined by the total number of valence electrons per unit cell \(N_e\):
\[ N_e = 2\int_{-\infty}^{E_F} g(E)\,dE \]
One electron per cell half-fills the first band (metal). Two electrons per cell can exactly fill the first band — yielding an insulator if \(E_g > 0\), or a semimetal/metal if bands overlap. This counting rule explains why monovalent Na is metallic while divalent Ca can be either metallic (d-band overlap) or insulating (MgO). Diamond-structure C, Si, Ge each have 4 valence electrons filling exactly two bands, but only become insulators/semiconductors because the four-fold-degenerate first four bands split into two pairs with a gap between.
Complete Plane-Wave Band Structure Algorithm — Summary
Step 1 — Define the lattice (1D/2D/3D, Bravais type) and potential \(V(\mathbf{r})\). Compute Fourier coefficients \(\tilde{V}_\mathbf{G}\) analytically or by numerical quadrature.

Step 2 — Generate the G-vector basis set to energy cutoff \(E_\text{cut}\). Construct the index table mapping \((l,m,n)\to\)Cartesian coordinates.

Step 3 — Define the high-symmetry k-path for the lattice type. Parameterize by cumulative arc length so segment widths are proportional to their reciprocal-space lengths.

Step 4 — For each k-point: build \(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{k})\) (diagonal kinetic + off-diagonal potential), diagonalize by Jacobi (or LAPACK DSYEV for large matrices), sort eigenvalues, store \(E_n(\mathbf{k})\) and \(\mathbf{c}^{(n)}\).

Step 5 — Plot \(E_n(\mathbf{k})\) vs arc length; overlay the folded free-electron dispersion to identify which Bragg scattering event opens each gap.

Step 6 — Post-process: compute DOS by Gaussian smearing, extract \(E_g\) and \(m^*\) by finite difference, reconstruct Bloch wavefunctions from eigenvectors, identify Van Hove singularities.
