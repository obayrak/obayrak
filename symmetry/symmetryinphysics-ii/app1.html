Foundation

What is Symmetry in Physics?

A symmetry is a transformation you can perform on a system that leaves the laws of physics — or the measurable outcomes — unchanged.

"If a system has a continuous symmetry property, then there are corresponding quantities whose values are conserved in time."

— Emmy Noether, 1918

Symmetry is not merely aesthetic. It is the skeletal architecture of modern physics. From the Standard Model of particle physics to general relativity, the deepest equations are expressions of symmetry principles.

Geometric Symmetry

A geometric (or mathematical) symmetry is a transformation that leaves a shape or pattern visually invariant.

  • A snowflake has 6-fold rotational symmetry
  • A sphere looks the same from every direction
  • A sine wave is translation-periodic
$f(x) = f(x + a) \quad \text{(periodic function)}$

Geometric symmetry lives in the structure of mathematical objects — shapes, functions, equations.

Physical Symmetry

A physical symmetry is a transformation that leaves the laws of physics or physical observables invariant.

  • Moving a lab to another city doesn't change the laws
  • Rotating an experiment doesn't change its outcome
  • Running physics forward or backward in time (mostly)
$\mathcal{L}(q, \dot{q}, t) \to \mathcal{L}(q', \dot{q}', t')$

Physical symmetry is deeper: it constrains what the universe can do, and directly implies conservation laws.

Key Distinction

The Critical Difference

A snowflake has geometric symmetry, but this tells us nothing about physics. When we say "the laws of physics are translationally symmetric," we mean an experiment performed in Paris and one performed in Tokyo — under identical initial conditions — must yield identical results. This is a claim about nature itself, not about the shape of equations.

The Lagrangian (or Hamiltonian) of a system encodes these physical symmetries. When the Lagrangian is invariant under a continuous transformation, Emmy Noether's theorem guarantees a conserved quantity emerges.

$\text{Symmetry of } \mathcal{L} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{Conservation Law}$

The Lagrangian Formalism

Modern physics expresses symmetry through the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = T - V$ (kinetic minus potential energy). A symmetry is a transformation $q \to q'$ under which:

$$\delta \mathcal{L} = 0 \qquad \text{or more generally} \qquad \delta \mathcal{L} = \frac{d}{dt}F(q,t)$$

The Euler–Lagrange equations of motion are:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}_i} - \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial q_i} = 0$$

These equations, plus the symmetries of $\mathcal{L}$, contain essentially all of classical mechanics.

Continuous vs. Discrete

Continuous Symmetries

Parametrised by a continuously varying number (e.g. angle, distance, time). These are described by Lie groups — groups with a smooth manifold structure.

Examples: SO(3) for 3D rotations, U(1) for phase, the Poincaré group for spacetime.

Noether's theorem applies — every continuous symmetry yields a conservation law.

Discrete Symmetries

Only a finite number of operations (or a countably infinite set). Cannot be labelled by a continuous parameter.

Examples: P (parity), C (charge conjugation), T (time reversal), the symmetry group of a crystal.

Noether's theorem does not directly apply, but discrete symmetries still impose powerful constraints on allowed interactions.

Symmetry Breaking

When Symmetry is Broken

Not all symmetries are exact. The universe sometimes breaks them:

  • Spontaneous symmetry breaking: The Lagrangian is symmetric but the ground state is not (e.g. a ferromagnet, the Higgs mechanism). This gives particles mass.
  • Explicit breaking: A term in the Lagrangian directly violates the symmetry (e.g. quark masses break chiral symmetry).
  • Anomalous breaking: A classical symmetry is violated by quantum corrections.
  • Parity violation: The weak force is the only force that distinguishes left from right — experimentally confirmed by Chien-Shiung Wu in 1957.
Continuous Spatial Symmetry

Translational Symmetry

The laws of physics are the same everywhere in space. No location in the universe is physically special. This seemingly simple statement is the origin of the conservation of momentum.

The Principle

If we shift every coordinate by a constant vector $\mathbf{a}$:

$\mathbf{r}_i \;\to\; \mathbf{r}_i + \mathbf{a}$

The Lagrangian of an isolated system must be invariant: $\delta\mathcal{L} = 0$. This means $\mathcal{L}$ cannot depend on the absolute position of the centre of mass, only on relative positions and velocities.

From Lagrangian to Momentum

The Lagrangian for $N$ particles with pairwise interactions:

$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{1}{2}m_i \dot{\mathbf{r}}_i^2 - \sum_{i < j} V(|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|)$$

The potential $V$ depends only on relative displacements, so $\mathcal{L}$ is invariant under $\mathbf{r}_i \to \mathbf{r}_i + \mathbf{a}$. Noether's theorem then gives:

$$\mathbf{p}_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i \dot{\mathbf{r}}_i = \text{constant}$$

SO(3) Symmetry

Rotational Symmetry

Space has no preferred direction. Rotating an entire experiment by any angle produces the same physics. This symmetry group is SO(3) and its conserved quantity is angular momentum.

The Rotation Group SO(3)

A rotation by angle $\theta$ about axis $\hat{n}$ maps $\mathbf{r} \to R_{\hat{n},\theta}\,\mathbf{r}$. For a rotationally symmetric Lagrangian (like a central force):

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{r},\dot{\mathbf{r}}) = \mathcal{L}(R\mathbf{r}, R\dot{\mathbf{r}}) \quad \forall R \in SO(3)$$

This requires that $V(\mathbf{r})$ depends only on $|\mathbf{r}|$ — a central potential — which describes gravity and the Coulomb force.

Angular Momentum Conservation

$$\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p} = \mathbf{r} \times m\dot{\mathbf{r}} = \text{const}$$

Noether's theorem gives us this directly. The infinitesimal rotation $\delta\mathbf{r} = \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \times \mathbf{r}$ leads to the conserved Noether charge:

$$\mathbf{L} = \sum_i \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{\mathbf{r}}_i} \times \mathbf{r}_i = \sum_i m_i \dot{\mathbf{r}}_i \times \mathbf{r}_i$$

Kepler's Second Law — A Consequence

The conservation of angular momentum directly explains Kepler's second law: a line joining the planet to the sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times.

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{|\mathbf{L}|}{2m} = \text{constant}$$

Watch the elliptical orbit: the planet moves faster near the star (perihelion) and slower far away (aphelion) — exactly what's needed to sweep equal areas.

Temporal Translation Invariance

Time Translation Symmetry

The laws of physics today are the same as they were a billion years ago and will be a billion years from now. This temporal homogeneity is the origin of energy conservation.

Time Translation

The symmetry: shift the time origin $t \to t + \tau$. If the Lagrangian has no explicit time dependence:

$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial t} = 0$

Then the Hamiltonian (total energy) is conserved.

Time Reversal ($T$)

A separate concept: $T: t \to -t$. This reverses} velocities and momenta. Newton's equations are invariant under $T$ for conservative forces. The movie plays equally well forwards and backwards.

$T: (t,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p}) \to (-t,\mathbf{r},-\mathbf{p})$

Energy Conservation from Noether's Theorem

Define the Hamiltonian from the Lagrangian via a Legendre transform:

$$H = \sum_i \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}_i}\dot{q}_i - \mathcal{L} = T + V = E$$

Using the Euler–Lagrange equations, the time derivative is:

$$\frac{dH}{dt} = -\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial t} \;=\; 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad E = \text{constant}$$

When Time Symmetry Breaks Down

The arrow of time emerges not from fundamental laws (which are mostly time-symmetric) but from statistical mechanics: entropy increases because there are vastly more disordered states than ordered ones.

Additionally, CP violation (observed in kaons and B mesons) implies, through CPT invariance, that T symmetry is slightly violated in the weak interaction at the microscopic level.

$S_{\text{Boltzmann}}: k_B \ln \Omega \;\nearrow\; \text{thermodynamics}$
Discrete Symmetry · P-Symmetry

Parity (Reflection) Symmetry

Parity $P$ inverts all spatial coordinates: $\mathbf{r} \to -\mathbf{r}$. This is equivalent to a mirror reflection plus a 180° rotation. For most of physics, the mirror image obeys the same laws. But not always.

The Parity Operation
$$P: (x, y, z) \to (-x, -y, -z)$$

Vectors (like position $\mathbf{r}$, momentum $\mathbf{p}$) flip sign under $P$. These are called polar vectors.

Pseudovectors (like angular momentum $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}$, magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$) do not flip — they pick up an extra minus sign from the cross product.

$$P(\mathbf{L}) = P(\mathbf{r}) \times P(\mathbf{p}) = (-\mathbf{r}) \times (-\mathbf{p}) = +\mathbf{L}$$
Wu Experiment (1957)

Chien-Shiung Wu cooled Cobalt-60 nuclei and aligned their spins with a magnetic field. She measured the direction of emitted electrons (beta decay via weak force).

Result: Electrons emerged preferentially opposite to the spin direction. In the mirror world, they would emerge in the same direction — which never happens.

The weak force violates parity maximally — it couples only to left-handed particles (and right-handed antiparticles).

Helicity and the Weak Force

Maximal Parity Violation

The weak force couples only to left-handed fermions (spin anti-parallel to momentum) and right-handed anti-fermions. Under parity, left-handed becomes right-handed — but the resulting right-handed fermion doesn't interact with the weak force!

$$W^\pm \text{ bosons couple only to: }\; \psi_L = \tfrac{1}{2}(1-\gamma^5)\psi$$

This is described by the V−A (vector minus axial-vector) structure of weak interactions, a cornerstone of the Standard Model.

Local Symmetry & Discrete Operations

Gauge Symmetry & CPT

Gauge symmetry is the profound idea that local unobservable freedoms in a quantum field, when demanded to be exact, require the existence of force-carrying particles.

Global U(1) Gauge Symmetry

A quantum field $\psi$ can be multiplied by a global phase without changing any observable:

$\psi \to e^{i\alpha}\psi, \quad |\psi|^2 \to |\psi|^2$

The global phase $\alpha$ is physically meaningless. By Noether's theorem, this gives us conservation of electric charge!

$Q = \int j^0 d^3x = \text{const}$
Local Gauge Symmetry

Now allow $\alpha$ to vary with position and time: $\alpha \to \alpha(x^\mu)$. The Dirac Lagrangian is not invariant under this. To restore invariance, we must introduce a new field $A_\mu$ (the gauge field) that transforms as:

$A_\mu \to A_\mu - \frac{1}{e}\partial_\mu \alpha$

This gauge field is the photon. The electromagnetic force is the direct consequence of demanding local U(1) invariance!

CPT Symmetry

The most fundamental discrete symmetry theorem in physics: the combination of Charge conjugation, Parity, and Time reversal is an exact symmetry of every quantum field theory consistent with special relativity and locality.

C — Charge Conjugation

Replaces every particle with its antiparticle. Flips all internal charges (electric, color, lepton number).

$C: \psi \to \psi^c$

Violated by weak force (neutrinos are only left-handed).

P — Parity

Inverts spatial coordinates. Flips handedness. Mirrors the universe.

$P: \mathbf{r} \to -\mathbf{r}$

Violated maximally by the weak force (Wu 1957).

T — Time Reversal

Reverses the direction of time. Flips momenta and spins.

$T: t \to -t$

Violated slightly via CP violation (CPT theorem).

The CPT Theorem

Proved by Lüders and Pauli (1954): any quantum field theory that is (1) Lorentz invariant, (2) local, and (3) has a Hermitian Hamiltonian is automatically invariant under CPT. This means:

  • Particles and antiparticles have exactly equal masses and lifetimes
  • Their magnetic moments differ only in sign
  • A violation of CPT would require new physics beyond the Standard Model

Current experimental bounds place CPT violation at less than $10^{-18}$ GeV, among the most precisely tested laws in all of physics.

The Master Theorem

Noether's Theorem

Proved by Emmy Noether in 1915, this theorem is arguably the most beautiful and important result in theoretical physics: every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a conservation law.

Emmy Noether (1882–1935)

Noether was a German mathematician who made transformative contributions to abstract algebra and theoretical physics. Einstein called her "the most significant creative mathematical genius thus far produced." Her theorem, published in 1918 in "Invariante Variationsprobleme," unified the understanding of symmetry and conservation in one elegant stroke.

The Mathematical Statement

Consider a system with generalised coordinates $q_i$ and Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}(q_i, \dot{q}_i, t)$. Suppose there is a one-parameter family of transformations $q_i \to q_i + \epsilon \,\delta q_i$ that leaves the action $S = \int \mathcal{L}\,dt$ invariant. Then there exists a conserved quantity:

$$\boxed{J = \sum_i \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}_i}\,\delta q_i = \text{const}}$$

This is the Noether charge associated with the symmetry. Its time derivative is zero on every solution of the equations of motion:

$$\frac{dJ}{dt} = 0 \quad \text{(on solutions of Euler-Lagrange)}$$
NOETHER CORRESPONDENCE — INTERACTIVE SYMMETRY MAP

The Three Great Correspondences

Space Translation → Momentum

Transformation: $\delta q_i = \hat{n}$ (constant shift in direction $\hat{n}$)

$$\mathbf{p} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{\mathbf{r}}} = m\dot{\mathbf{r}}$$

The law: $\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt} = 0$

This is Newton's first law in disguise — and it applies to quantum mechanics, relativity, and field theory equally.

Time Translation → Energy

The action shifts by $\delta t$; $\delta q_i = \dot{q}_i \delta t$

$$H = \sum_i p_i \dot{q}_i - \mathcal{L} = E$$

The law: $\frac{dE}{dt} = 0$

In cosmology: the universe's expansion can break time translation symmetry — which is why energy is not globally conserved in an expanding universe!

Rotation → Angular Momentum

Infinitesimal rotation: $\delta \mathbf{r}_i = \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \times \mathbf{r}_i$

$$\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p} = \mathbf{r} \times m\dot{\mathbf{r}}$$

The law: $\frac{d\mathbf{L}}{dt} = 0$

Explains everything from spinning gyroscopes to the accretion discs of black holes to the stability of atoms.

Extended Correspondences

Gauge Symmetry → Charge Conservation

In quantum field theory, global U(1) phase invariance ($\psi \to e^{i\alpha}\psi$) gives via Noether's theorem:

$$j^\mu = \bar{\psi}\gamma^\mu\psi, \qquad \partial_\mu j^\mu = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad Q = \int j^0 d^3x = \text{const}$$

This is the exact conservation of electric charge. The fact that you cannot create or destroy net electric charge is a consequence of a symmetry of nature.
