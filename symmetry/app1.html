Geometrical Symmetry

A figure or pattern is geometrically symmetric if it is invariant under one or more geometric transformations — rotations, reflections, translations, or their combinations. Described by group theory (point groups, space groups), geometric symmetry classifies crystals, molecules, and patterns in nature.

Physical Symmetry

A physical law possesses symmetry if its mathematical form — the Lagrangian or Hamiltonian — is unchanged under some transformation. Noether's theorem guarantees that every continuous physical symmetry corresponds to an exactly conserved quantity. Discrete symmetries (C, P, T) constrain particle interactions.

Core Taxonomy

Types of Symmetry

Both branches share the same group-theoretic language. A symmetry group is a set of transformations closed under composition, with an identity and inverses.

Geometrical Symmetry

Group Theory
Rotational Symmetry
Cₙ — cyclic group of order n

Invariance under rotation by 360°/n. C₂ = half-turn; C₆ = snowflake / benzene. The order n determines the degree of symmetry. Generators: Rₙ = rotation by 2π/n.

Reflection (Mirror) Symmetry
σ — reflection plane; Cs point group

Invariance under reflection through a plane (3D) or line (2D). Bilateral symmetry in biology. In molecules: σh (horizontal), σv (vertical), σd (dihedral).

Translational Symmetry
T(a) — translation by lattice vector a

Invariance under displacement by a fixed vector a (or multiples). Crystals have discrete translational symmetry with lattice vectors a₁, a₂, a₃. Infinite patterns have continuous translation symmetry.

Inversion Symmetry
i — parity / centrosymmetry

Invariance under r → −r through a centre. 11 of the 32 crystallographic point groups possess an inversion centre (centrosymmetric). Absent in chiral molecules and non-centrosymmetric crystals (e.g. piezoelectrics).

Glide & Screw Symmetry
a,b,c,n,d glide planes; nₘ screw axes

Combined translation + reflection (glide plane) or translation + rotation (screw axis). Present only in space groups (230 total in 3D), not point groups. Produces helical patterns — e.g. DNA double helix (pseudo-screw axis).

Dihedral Symmetry
Dₙ = Cₙ + n reflection planes

Contains n rotational axes AND n mirror planes. D₆h is the full symmetry of the snowflake and benzene ring. D∞h is the symmetry of a homonuclear diatomic (N₂, O₂). Order of Dₙ = 2n.

Physical Symmetry

Lagrangian Invariance
Time-Translation Symmetry
t → t + τ  ·  Conserved: Energy E

The laws of physics are the same at all times. The Lagrangian has no explicit time dependence: ∂L/∂t = 0. By Noether's theorem this gives energy conservation. Violated if Hamiltonian is explicitly time-dependent.

Spatial-Translation Symmetry
x → x + a  ·  Conserved: Momentum p

Physics is the same everywhere in space. The Lagrangian does not depend on absolute position. The resulting conserved charge is linear momentum. Broken by gravitational fields and boundaries.

Rotational Symmetry
SO(3)  ·  Conserved: Angular Momentum L

Physics is the same in all directions. The Lagrangian is invariant under SO(3) rotations. Noether gives three components of angular momentum L = r × p. Broken by anisotropic media or preferred directions.

Global Phase Symmetry
U(1)  ·  Conserved: Electric Charge Q

The wave-function ψ → e^{iα}ψ leaves all observable probabilities |ψ|² unchanged. Noether's theorem gives conservation of electric charge. Promoting α to a local function α(x) forces introduction of the photon.

Lorentz / Poincaré Symmetry
ISO(3,1)  ·  Conserved: 4-momentum, J^{μν}

The laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames. The Poincaré group = Lorentz boosts + rotations + spacetime translations. Gives conservation of 4-momentum and angular momentum tensor. Foundation of special relativity and QFT.

Scale (Dilatation) Symmetry
x → λx  ·  Conserved: Dilatation current D^μ

Massless theories are often invariant under rescaling of coordinates. Conformal symmetry (15-parameter group in 4D) extends scale invariance. Exact in massless QED and N=4 SYM. Broken by mass and quantum anomalies (RG running).

Discrete Symmetries

C, P and T

Unlike continuous symmetries, discrete symmetries do not yield conserved charges via Noether. Instead they constrain the form of interactions and, when violated, reveal fundamental asymmetries in nature.

C
Charge Conjugation
q → −q  ·  particle ↔ antiparticle

Replaces every particle with its antiparticle and vice versa. If C were exact, matter and antimatter would behave identically. The Universe would contain equal amounts of each — but it doesn't.

VIOLATED by weak interaction (left-handed W bosons couple only to left-handed fermions)
P
Parity (Spatial Inversion)
r → −r  ·  mirror image of physics

Flips all spatial coordinates: (x,y,z)→(−x,−y,−z). Discovered to be violated by Wu (1956): Co-60 beta decay showed preferred handedness. Weak interactions maximally violate P.

VIOLATED by weak interaction — Wu experiment, 1956 (C.S. Wu)
T
Time Reversal
t → −t  ·  run film backward

Reverses the direction of time. At microscopic level T-violation was directly observed in kaon system (CPLEAR, 1998) and B-mesons (BaBar 2012). Not to be confused with thermodynamic time-irreversibility.

VIOLATED in weak kaon & B-meson decays (subtle, measured via CP violation + CPT)

CP Violation

The combined CP symmetry is violated in weak decays of K and B mesons. CP violation is the mechanism by which the Universe developed a matter–antimatter asymmetry (baryogenesis). In the Standard Model it enters via a complex phase δ in the CKM matrix.

CPT

CPT Theorem

The combination CPT is believed to be an exact symmetry of any local, Lorentz-invariant quantum field theory. It is the deepest discrete symmetry in physics. CPT invariance implies particle and antiparticle have the same mass and lifetime.

Local Symmetry

Gauge Symmetry

Promoting a global symmetry to a local one (α → α(x)) requires introducing a gauge field — a force-carrying boson — to maintain invariance. This is the origin of all fundamental forces.

From Symmetry to Force

Each fundamental interaction arises from demanding local gauge invariance under a specific Lie group. The gauge bosons are the quanta of the corresponding gauge fields.

When Symmetry Fails

Symmetry Breaking

Some of the most important physics arises precisely when a symmetry of the Hamiltonian is not reflected in the ground state or the solutions — giving rise to mass, phase transitions, and Goldstone bosons.

Explicit Symmetry Breaking

The Lagrangian/Hamiltonian itself is not invariant under the transformation. The symmetry is broken "by hand" — e.g. by a mass term, an external field, or a perturbation. No Goldstone bosons result; the symmetry was never exact.

Examples: Quark masses explicitly break chiral symmetry in QCD. Gravitational field breaks translational symmetry in a lab. An external magnetic field breaks spin-rotation symmetry.
Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking

The Hamiltonian IS invariant, but the ground state (vacuum) is not. The system "chooses" a particular ground state that breaks the symmetry. Goldstone's theorem: each broken continuous symmetry → a massless Goldstone boson (or a massive gauge boson via Higgs mechanism).

Examples: Higgs field giving mass to W/Z (Higgs mechanism). Ferromagnetism: spin-rotation symmetry broken below Curie temperature. Superconductivity (U(1) breaking). Chiral condensate in QCD.

Goldstone's Theorem

For every spontaneously broken continuous global symmetry, there exists a massless scalar Goldstone boson. If the original symmetry has N generators and the unbroken residual subgroup has M, there are (N−M) Goldstone bosons.

N_Goldstone = dim(G) − dim(H)

Higgs Mechanism

When a local (gauge) symmetry is spontaneously broken, the Goldstone bosons are "eaten" by the gauge bosons, which acquire mass. The Higgs field has a non-zero vacuum expectation value v ≈ 246 GeV, breaking SU(2)_L × U(1)_Y → U(1)_EM.

m_W = gv/2  ·  m_Z = gv/(2cosθ_W)
