Defining Geometric Symmetry

A geometric figure is symmetric with respect to an operation if the operation maps the figure back onto itself — leaving it visually indistinguishable from the original. Formally, a symmetry is an isometry: a distance-preserving transformation that sends every point of the figure to another point (or the same point) within the figure.

The complete collection of symmetries of an object, closed under composition, forms a symmetry group — a central object in abstract algebra and modern physics (Noether's theorem connects symmetry groups directly to conservation laws).

Sym(X) = { f : X → X | f is an isometry, f(X) = X }
Reflection

Reflection Symmetry

Also called line symmetry or mirror symmetry. One half of the figure is the exact mirror image of the other. The dividing line is the line of symmetry.

\[ R_x(x,y) = (x,\,-y) \qquad R_y(x,y) = (-x,\,y) \] \[ R_{y=x}(x,y) = (y,\,x) \qquad R_{y=-x}(x,y) = (-y,\,-x) \]

The reflection matrix across a line at angle \(\theta\) through the origin:

\[ M_\theta = \begin{pmatrix}\cos 2\theta & \sin 2\theta \\ \sin 2\theta & -\cos 2\theta\end{pmatrix} \]
  • Square — 4 lines (horizontal, vertical, 2 diagonals)
  • Equilateral triangle — 3 lines of symmetry
  • Isosceles / Kite — 1 line of symmetry
  • Scalene triangle — no lines of symmetry
  • Circle — infinitely many lines of symmetry

Rotation

Rotational Symmetry

A shape has rotational symmetry of order n when it maps to itself after a rotation of \(360°/n\). The cyclic group \(C_n\) is its symmetry group.

\[ R_\theta = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix} \]
\[ C_n = \left\{\,R_{k\cdot 360°/n}\;\middle|\; k = 0,1,\ldots,n-1\,\right\} \]
  • Square — order 4 (90°, 180°, 270°, 360°)
  • Equilateral triangle — order 3 (120° steps)
  • Regular hexagon — order 6 (honeycomb structure)
  • Kite — order 1 (no non-trivial rotation)
  • Circle — infinite order (any angle works)

Translation

Translational Symmetry & Bravais Lattices

A pattern has translational symmetry if shifting it by a lattice vector \(\mathbf{a}\) leaves it unchanged. The 14 Bravais lattices describe every periodic 3D arrangement in crystallography.

\[ T_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{r}) = \mathbf{r} + \mathbf{a}, \quad \mathbf{a} = n_1\mathbf{a}_1 + n_2\mathbf{a}_2, \quad n_i \in \mathbb{Z} \]

Scaling

Scaling (Dilation) Symmetry

A shape has scaling symmetry — self-similarity — if it looks identical at different magnifications. This is the hallmark of fractals.

\[ S_s(\mathbf{r}) = s\cdot\mathbf{r}, \quad s \in \mathbb{R}^{+} \]

Fractal (Hausdorff) dimension with scale factor \(s\) and \(N\) self-similar pieces:

\[ D = \frac{\log N}{\log(1/s)} \] \[ \text{Koch snowflake:}\; D = \tfrac{\log 4}{\log 3} \approx 1.26 \] \[ \text{Sierpiński:}\; D = \tfrac{\log 3}{\log 2} \approx 1.585 \]
  • Each iteration produces \(N\) copies at scale \(1/s\)
  • Scale symmetry also appears in power laws: \(f(\lambda x)=\lambda^k f(x)\)
  • Turbulence, stock prices, earthquakes exhibit scale-free behavior

Inversion

Point Inversion — Centrosymmetry

A figure is centrosymmetric if every point \((x,y)\) has a corresponding point \((-x,-y)\) on the opposite side of a fixed center of inversion. In 2D, inversion through the origin is equivalent to a 180° rotation, but in 3D it involves a handedness change.

Shapes with inversion symmetry include: parallelograms, circles, regular polygons with even number of sides, and all centrosymmetric crystal structures.

◇ Parallelogram○ Circle ⬡ Regular hexagon⬛ Square
\[ I(\mathbf{r}) = -\mathbf{r} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad (x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y) \]
\[ I = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix} = R_{180°} \quad (\text{in 2D}) \]

In 3D crystallography, the inversion center is a distinct symmetry element from \(C_2\) rotation. Combined with other operations it generates the 32 crystallographic point groups.
