01 — Introduction

What is Symmetry in Physics?

A symmetry is a transformation that leaves the laws of physics unchanged.

In everyday language, symmetry suggests balance and beauty. In physics it has a precise, operational meaning: a symmetry is a transformation applied to a physical system such that the system's equations of motion, its predictions, and all observable outcomes remain completely identical after the transformation is applied.

This idea—deceptively simple—is one of the most powerful organizing principles in all of science. Emmy Noether proved in 1915 that every continuous symmetry of the laws of physics corresponds exactly to a conserved quantity, a number that never changes with time.

The Core Idea:
$$\text{Symmetry of }\mathcal{L}\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{Noether's Theorem}\;}\;\text{Conservation Law}$$ If the action $S = \int \mathcal{L}\,dt$ is invariant under a continuous transformation, there exists a corresponding conserved charge $Q$ with $\dot{Q}=0$.

The symmetries of the Standard Model predict conservation of energy, momentum, angular momentum, electric charge, baryon number, and lepton number. Understanding symmetry means understanding the deep structure of physical reality itself.

Symmetry as Invariance

We say system $S$ has symmetry $T$ if applying $T$ gives $T(S) \equiv S$ in the sense that every measurable quantity is unchanged. Mathematically, symmetry transformations form a group $G$ under composition.

$g_1, g_2 \in G \Rightarrow g_1 \circ g_2 \in G$,   identity $e \in G$,   $\exists\,g^{-1} \in G$

Continuous vs Discrete

Continuous symmetries (parameterized by a real number, like rotation angle) generate conservation laws via Noether's theorem. Discrete symmetries (like reflection) do not generate conserved quantities directly, but are still fundamental constraints.

Continuous: SO(3), U(1), SU(2), SU(3)...
Discrete: $\mathbb{Z}_2$ (parity), $\mathcal{T}$ (time reversal)

02 — Distinction

Geometric vs. Physical Symmetry

🔷 Geometric Symmetry

A shape or object appears identical after a geometric transformation. This is the classical mathematical notion from Euclidean geometry and group theory.

Example: A regular hexagon looks identical after a 60° rotation about its center. A circle is symmetric under any rotation.

Symmetry groups: $D_6$, $C_{4v}$, $\text{SO}(2)$...
The object is unchanged.

⚛️ Physical Symmetry

The laws of physics themselves are unchanged under a transformation, even when the system appears different. This is far more profound.

Example: Moving a physics experiment from Paris to Tokyo changes its position, but Newton's laws are identical at both locations.

$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{a},\dot{\mathbf{r}}) = \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{r},\dot{\mathbf{r}})$
The Lagrangian is unchanged.

Physical symmetries operate on the action functional $S[\mathbf{q}(t)] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}},t)\,dt$. When $S$ is invariant under a continuous group of transformations, Noether's theorem guarantees a conservation law. This is why physical symmetry is the deeper, more fundamental notion: it dictates the very structure of what quantities nature preserves.

04a — Translational Symmetry

Translational Symmetry

The laws of physics are the same at every point in space.

If we take a physical system and move it bodily from one location to another — translating every particle by the same vector $\mathbf{a}$ — the dynamics are completely unchanged. The forces between particles depend only on their relative positions $\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j$, not on their absolute positions.

$$\text{Translation: }\quad \mathbf{r}_i \;\mapsto\; \mathbf{r}_i + \mathbf{a}$$ $$V(\mathbf{r}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{r}_N) = V(\mathbf{r}_1+\mathbf{a},\ldots,\mathbf{r}_N+\mathbf{a})$$ $$\Rightarrow\quad \sum_i \mathbf{F}_i = \frac{d}{dt}\sum_i m_i\dot{\mathbf{r}}_i = \frac{d\mathbf{P}}{dt} = 0$$

Because the Lagrangian is independent of the center-of-mass coordinate, the total momentum $\mathbf{P} = \sum_i m_i \dot{\mathbf{r}}_i$ is conserved. This is Newton's first law elevated to a fundamental principle.

04b — Rotational Symmetry

Rotational Symmetry

The laws of physics are the same in every direction in space.

Rotating the entire universe — or equivalently, rotating our measuring apparatus — leaves all physical predictions unchanged. Central force laws like gravity and electrostatics depend only on $|\mathbf{r}|$, making them spherically symmetric. Rotating a planetary orbit by any angle gives an equally valid orbit.

$$V(\mathbf{r}) = V(|\mathbf{r}|) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta} = 0$$ $$\Rightarrow\quad L = m r^2 \dot{\theta} = \text{const} \qquad\text{(angular momentum conserved)}$$

04c — Time Symmetry

Time Symmetry (Time-Translation Invariance)

The laws of physics are the same at every moment in time.

If an experiment yields a particular result today, the same experiment performed tomorrow (or a billion years from now) yields the same result. Mathematically, the Lagrangian has no explicit time dependence: $\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial t = 0$. This single condition is sufficient to guarantee conservation of energy.

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial t} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad H = \sum_i \dot{q}_i \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}_i} - \mathcal{L} = E = \text{const}$$

Time-reversal symmetry is a related but distinct concept: for conservative systems, if $\mathbf{q}(t)$ is a valid trajectory, then $\mathbf{q}(-t)$ (with $\dot{\mathbf{q}}\to -\dot{\mathbf{q}}$) is also valid. Watch an elastic collision forward and backward — you cannot tell which direction is "physical."

04d — Reflection Symmetry

Parity (Reflection) Symmetry

A mirror image of a physical process is also a valid physical process — usually.

Parity transformation $P$ sends every spatial coordinate to its negative: $(x,y,z) \mapsto (-x,-y,-z)$. This is equivalent to a full spatial inversion (mirror reflection plus a 180° rotation). Under parity, polar vectors like position and momentum flip: $\mathbf{r} \to -\mathbf{r}$, $\mathbf{p} \to -\mathbf{p}$. Axial vectors (pseudovectors) like angular momentum and magnetic field do not flip: $\mathbf{L} \to +\mathbf{L}$, $\mathbf{B} \to +\mathbf{B}$.

$$P:\; (x,y,z) \to (-x,-y,-z)$$ $$\mathbf{p} \to -\mathbf{p},\quad \mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p} \to \mathbf{L},\quad \mathbf{B} \to \mathbf{B}$$

Electromagnetism and gravity are parity-symmetric. But in 1956, Lee and Yang predicted (and Wu confirmed) that the weak force violates parity — nature has a preferred handedness at the fundamental level!

04e — Gauge Symmetry

Gauge Symmetry

A global (and local) phase redefinition of quantum fields leaves all observables unchanged.

In quantum mechanics, a particle is described by a complex wavefunction $\psi(\mathbf{r},t)$. Physical observables depend on $|\psi|^2$, not on $\psi$ itself. Multiplying $\psi$ by a global phase factor $e^{i\alpha}$ (where $\alpha$ is a real constant) has absolutely no observable consequence:

$$\psi \;\mapsto\; e^{i\alpha}\psi \quad\Rightarrow\quad |\psi'|^2 = |e^{i\alpha}\psi|^2 = |\psi|^2 \quad \text{(unchanged)}$$

This global U(1) gauge symmetry leads via Noether's theorem to conservation of electric charge. When we demand the symmetry be local — $\alpha \to \alpha(\mathbf{r},t)$ — we are forced to introduce a gauge field (the electromagnetic four-potential $A_\mu$), and this naturally generates Maxwell's equations and the electromagnetic interaction. The entire theory of electrodynamics emerges from gauge invariance alone!

Local gauge invariance:
$\psi \to e^{i\alpha(\mathbf{r},t)}\psi$ requires $A_\mu \to A_\mu - \frac{1}{e}\partial_\mu\alpha$
→ This generates the photon and electromagnetic force.

04f — CPT Symmetry

CPT Symmetry

The combined transformation of Charge conjugation, Parity, and Time reversal is an exact symmetry of all known physics.

$C$: Particles ↔ Antiparticles (flip charge, spin, flavor)
$P$: Spatial inversion $(x,y,z)\to(-x,-y,-z)$
$T$: Time reversal $t \to -t$, reversing momenta and spins

Individual symmetries C, P, and T may be violated. The weak force violates P (Wu experiment 1956). It also violates C and CP (Cronin–Fitch 1964, B-factory experiments). But the CPT theorem guarantees the combined operation is always preserved in any Lorentz-invariant local quantum field theory. A violation of CPT would be revolutionary physics beyond the Standard Model.

05 — Noether's Theorem

Noether's Theorem

Emmy Noether (1915): Every differentiable symmetry of the action of a physical system corresponds to a conservation law.

This is arguably the most profound theorem in all of physics. Noether proved that the connection between symmetry and conservation is not a coincidence or an approximation — it is a mathematical inevitability. The theorem works in both classical and quantum mechanics (via the path integral).

Noether's Theorem — Formal Statement:
If the action $S = \int \mathcal{L}\,dt$ is invariant under the infinitesimal transformation
$q_i \to q_i + \varepsilon K_i(q,\dot{q},t)$,
then the quantity
$$Q = \sum_i \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}_i} K_i$$ is conserved: $\dot{Q} = 0$.

The Three Great Conservation Laws

Space is uniform
→
Translation symmetry
⟹
$\mathbf{P} = \sum m_i \dot{\mathbf{r}}_i = \text{const}$
Space is isotropic
→
Rotation symmetry
⟹
$\mathbf{L} = \sum \mathbf{r}_i \times m_i \dot{\mathbf{r}}_i = \text{const}$
Time is uniform
→
Time-translation symmetry
⟹
$E = T + V = \text{const}$

These are not independent assumptions — they are consequences of the deep structure of space-time. The homogeneity and isotropy of space, and the uniformity of time, are the most fundamental experimental facts in all of physics, and they directly produce the three most important conservation laws.
