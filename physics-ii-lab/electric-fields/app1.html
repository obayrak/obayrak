Physics Reference
Gradient & Electric Field
The electric field is the negative gradient of the scalar potential: E = −∇V. It points in the direction of steepest decrease of V. Field lines are always perpendicular to equipotential surfaces, since moving along an equipotential does no work against the field (∇V is normal to level sets of V).
Superposition & Surface Integral
Each element of charged surface dA′ with density σ(r′) acts as a point charge dq = σ dA′. The total potential is the superposition (integral) of all these contributions: V(r) = (1/4πε₀) ∫ σ(r′)/|r−r′| dA′. This tool discretizes the surface into flat panels and numerically sums the contribution of every panel — a boundary-element / Riemann-sum approximation to the exact surface integral.
Gauss's Law & Flux
Gauss's law states ∮ E·dA = Q_enclosed/ε₀ — the net electric flux through any closed surface is proportional to the enclosed charge. For highly symmetric shapes (sphere, infinite cylinder) it gives the field directly; for general quadrics like the ones here, symmetry is broken by finite size or shape eccentricity, so the field must instead be found by direct integration as done in this simulator.
Surface Charge Density & Boundary Conditions
At a conductor/charged sheet, the field is discontinuous across the surface: E_outside,⊥ − E_inside,⊥ = σ/ε₀, while the tangential component of E is continuous. σ can be uniform (as on an isolated charged conductor of simple shape) or position-dependent (as it would be on a conductor sitting in an external field, or on a surface with prescribed non-uniform charging).
Equipotentials & Streamlines
Equipotential contours trace curves of constant V; stacking them across parallel slicing planes gives a visual impression of the 3-D equipotential surfaces. Field lines (streamlines of E) are generated here by numerically integrating dr/ds = E/|E| (RK4) starting from seed points near the charged surface.
Numerical Method
Each quadric is meshed into quadrilateral panels with position, area (from the cross product of edge vectors) and outward normal. σᵢ is assigned per panel (uniform, or evaluated from a custom formula and renormalized to match a requested total charge). V and E at any field point are then a direct sum over panels: E(r) ≈ k Σᵢ σᵢ Aᵢ (r−rᵢ)/|r−rᵢ|³, clamped near the surface to avoid the 1/r² singularity.

Starting point — Coulomb's law

Every calculation in this tool ultimately comes from Coulomb's law: the force between two point charges q₁ and q₂ separated by r is

$$\mathbf{F}_{12}=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1q_2}{|\mathbf{r}_1-\mathbf{r}_2|^2}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{12}$$

The constant 1/4πε₀ = k ≈ 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C² comes from the permittivity of free space, ε₀ ≈ 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m. The electric field is defined as force per unit test charge, E ≡ F/q₀, so the field of a single point charge q at the origin, measured at position r, is:

$$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|^2}\,\hat{\mathbf{u}},\qquad \hat{\mathbf{u}}=\frac{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}$$

where r′ is the source (charge) location and r is the field (observation) point. Writing the unit vector out explicitly, $\hat{\mathbf u}/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^2 = (\mathbf r-\mathbf r')/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3$, which is the form used everywhere else in this tool since it avoids computing a square root twice.

The scalar potential

Because a point-charge field is conservative (curl-free — see Tab 3), it can always be written as the gradient of a scalar function V, with a minus sign by convention:

$$\mathbf{E}=-\nabla V \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad V(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}+\text{const.}$$

The additive constant is fixed by choosing V → 0 as r → ∞, which is the convention used throughout this explorer (and is why isolated finite charge distributions have a well-defined V at every point in space).

Superposition principle

Maxwell's equations are linear in the charge and field, so if charge distribution 1 alone produces E₁ and charge distribution 2 alone produces E₂, the two together produce E₁ + E₂ (vector sum), and likewise V₁ + V₂ for potentials (ordinary scalar sum). This is not obvious from Coulomb's law alone — it is an experimentally verified property of electromagnetism at the classical level — but it is what makes the entire "sum over lots of point charges" strategy valid.

From points to continuous distributions

A continuous charge distribution is treated as an uncountably large collection of infinitesimal point charges. Depending on how the charge is smeared out, dq is written using a different density:

  • Line charge: dq = λ(r′) dl′, λ in C/m — used for the Line Charge, Ring Charge shapes.
  • Surface charge: dq = σ(r′) dA′, σ in C/m² — used for every quadric surface in this tool.
  • Volume charge: dq = ρ(r′) dV′, ρ in C/m³ — not used here, but the same idea one dimension up.

Substituting dq = σ(r′) dA′ into the point-charge V and E formulas and integrating (summing) over the whole surface S gives the exact field and potential of any charged surface — a sphere, a torus, a bipolar cylinder, all follow the identical recipe, just with a different S.

Key idea: a charged quadric surface is nothing more than a very large number of point charges glued together. Once you can integrate a point charge correctly, every surface in this explorer is "just" bookkeeping over that same integral with a different domain of integration and a different σ(r′).

Surface integral for V

Replacing dq = σ(r′) dA′ in the point-charge potential and integrating over the entire surface S gives the exact electric potential at any field point r:

$$V(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_S \frac{\sigma(\mathbf{r}')}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}\,dA'$$

V is a scalar field — a single number at every point in space, with no direction to track — which is usually the easier quantity to compute first, and why equipotential surfaces / contour lines (toggle "Equipotential lines" in the left panel) are a convenient way to visualize it: they are literally the level sets of this integral.

Path independence and work

Because E = −∇V, the work done by the field moving a test charge q₀ from point a to point b along any path C is a pure difference of the scalar V at the endpoints:

$$W_{a\to b}=q_0\int_a^b \mathbf{E}\cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell}=-q_0\int_a^b \nabla V\cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell}=q_0\big(V(a)-V(b)\big)$$

This is exactly the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus for line integrals applied to a gradient field — the reason electrostatic forces are called conservative. If you carry a test charge around any closed loop, the net work is zero: $\oint \mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = 0$, which (by Stokes' theorem) is equivalent to $\nabla\times\mathbf E=\mathbf 0$ everywhere in electrostatics.

Properties used elsewhere in this tool

  • Equipotential surfaces are always perpendicular to field lines, since moving along one does no work (E·dℓ = 0 requires E ⊥ dℓ).
  • For any distribution with a nonzero net charge Q, V(r) → kQ/r as r → ∞, regardless of the exact shape — a monopole approximation valid far from the object (see Tab 6).
  • The potential energy stored in an assembled charge distribution is $U=\tfrac12\int_S \sigma(\mathbf r')V(\mathbf r')\,dA'$ — half the "self-energy" double integral, used conceptually for the capacitance discussion in Tab 4.

Deriving E = −∇V for a point charge

Starting from $V(\mathbf r)=\frac{k q}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}$, differentiate component-wise. With $R=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|=\sqrt{(x-x')^2+(y-y')^2+(z-z')^2}$, the x-derivative is $\partial R/\partial x = (x-x')/R$, so:

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}=kq\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{R^2}\right)\frac{\partial R}{\partial x}=-\frac{kq(x-x')}{R^3} \;\Longrightarrow\; \nabla V = -kq\,\frac{\mathbf r-\mathbf r'}{R^3}$$

and therefore $\mathbf E=-\nabla V = kq\,(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')/R^3$, matching the direct Coulomb's-law expression from Tab 1 — a useful consistency check that the sign convention (E points away from positive charge) is correct.

Surface integral for E

Differentiating the potential integral under the integral sign (valid wherever σ is bounded and r is off the surface) carries the same derivative through the sum over source points, giving the field directly as a vector surface integral:

$$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})=-\nabla V=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_S \sigma(\mathbf{r}')\,\frac{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|^3}\,dA'$$

Unlike V, E is a vector field — each infinitesimal patch of charge contributes a vector pointing away from (positive σ) or toward (negative σ) that patch, scaled by 1/|r−r′|². All those vectors are summed component-by-component. This is exactly what the field arrows in the 3-D view represent, sampled on a grid around the surface, colored by |E| on a log scale.

Gauss's law from the divergence theorem

Taking the divergence of the point-charge field and using the identity $\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(\hat{\mathbf u}/R^2\big)=4\pi\delta^3(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')$ (the 3-D Dirac delta) gives the local, differential form of Gauss's law:

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}=\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}$$

Integrating both sides over a volume V bounded by a closed surface ∂V and applying the divergence theorem $\int_V \nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf E\,dV=\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A$ recovers the familiar integral form:

$$\oint_{\partial V} \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{A}=\frac{Q_{\text{enclosed}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$

For highly symmetric shapes (sphere, infinite cylinder) this gives E directly without integrating — a useful independent check on the numerical result. Most quadrics in this tool (ellipsoids, paraboloids, hyperboloids, tori, spheroids...) don't have enough symmetry for Gauss's law alone to pin down E's direction and magnitude everywhere, which is why direct integration (Tab 5) is needed in general.

Boundary condition at a charged surface

Consider a thin "pillbox" straddling a surface carrying density σ, with faces of area δA just above and below the surface. Gauss's law applied to the pillbox (the thin side walls contribute negligible flux as their height → 0) gives:

$$E_{\text{above},\perp}\,\delta A - E_{\text{below},\perp}\,\delta A=\frac{\sigma\,\delta A}{\varepsilon_0} \;\Longrightarrow\; E_{\text{above},\perp}-E_{\text{below},\perp}=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$$

So the normal component of E jumps discontinuously by σ/ε₀ across any charged surface, while the tangential component is continuous (it follows from $\nabla\times\mathbf E=0$ applied to a thin rectangular loop straddling the surface). This is exactly why the field arrows in this tool visibly change direction/magnitude as they cross a charged shell.

Conductors in electrostatic equilibrium

Inside a perfect conductor in equilibrium, free charges rearrange until E = 0 everywhere inside (otherwise charges would keep accelerating). Since E = −∇V, this forces V to be constant throughout the conductor's interior and on its surface — an isolated conductor is always an equipotential body. Combined with the boundary condition above (and E = 0 just inside), the field just outside a conductor's surface is purely normal:

$$\mathbf{E}_{\text{just outside}}=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\hat{\mathbf n}$$

This is precisely the physics the "Conductor" charging mode implements numerically: it solves for the σ(r′) that makes V constant over the whole discretized surface (Tab 5's linear system), rather than letting you prescribe σ directly.

Capacitance and stored energy

For any isolated conductor carrying charge Q at potential V (relative to V=0 at infinity), the capacitance is defined as C = Q/V, and the energy stored in the resulting field is:

$$C=\frac{Q}{V},\qquad U=\frac{1}{2}CV^2=\frac{1}{2}QV=\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int_{\text{all space}}|\mathbf E|^2\,dV$$

The two-conductor shapes in this tool — Parallel Plates, Dipole, Quadrupole pairs — behave as simple capacitors when set to "Conductor" mode: each conductor sits at an equal and opposite potential, and Q/ΔV for the pair is the system's capacitance.

From integral to sum — Boundary Element Method

The integrals in Tabs 2–3 have no closed form for most of these shapes, so this tool evaluates them numerically using a first-order Boundary Element Method (BEM) / panel method. The surface is meshed into N small quadrilateral panels. For panel i, built from four corner points p₀₀, p₁₀, p₀₁, p₁₁ of the parametric grid, we compute:

  • Centroid ri = ¼(p₀₀+p₁₀+p₀₁+p₁₁) — the average of the four corners.
  • Area Ai — the quadrilateral is split into two triangles along each diagonal pairing, each triangle's area from ½|e₁×e₂| of its edge vectors, and the two triangle areas are averaged for a more accurate estimate on non-planar (curved) panels.
  • Outward normal n̂i — from the (normalized) sum of the two triangles' cross products, so it stays well-defined even for slightly warped panels.
  • Charge qi = σi Ai — each panel is treated as an effective point charge located at its centroid.

Substituting this into the surface integrals turns them into discrete Riemann sums — exactly a direct point-charge superposition over all panels:

$$V(\mathbf{r})\approx k\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\sigma_i A_i}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i|}, \qquad \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})\approx k\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_i A_i\,\frac{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i|^3}$$

with k = 1/4πε₀ ≈ 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C². As N grows and each panel shrinks, this converges to the exact surface integral (it is literally a Riemann sum in the surface's parametric u,v coordinates). The rendering mesh uses a finer grid (for a smooth-looking surface); a coarser, independent "calculation mesh" (controlled by "Mesh / charge grid size") is used for the O(N) or O(N²) physics sums so the app stays responsive while you drag sliders.

Regularizing the near-field singularity

As r → ri the 1/R and 1/R² factors blow up — a real artifact of collapsing a finite panel to a single point charge at its centroid. This tool clamps the minimum separation distance to 1 mm before evaluating any 1/R term, which keeps arrows and probes well-behaved right up to the surface without materially affecting the field anywhere the mesh is reasonably fine relative to the probe distance.

Isolated / dual conductors — solving for σ

When "Conductor" charging is selected, σ isn't prescribed — it's solved for so that V is constant everywhere on the discretized surface (Tab 4's equilibrium condition). Demanding $V(\mathbf r_i)=1$ (an arbitrary reference unit — the physical scale is fixed afterward) at every panel centroid turns the continuous equilibrium condition into an N×N linear system:

$$\sum_{j=1}^N A_{ij}\,\sigma_j = 1,\qquad A_{ij}=\begin{cases} k\cdot 2\pi r_i & i=j\\[4pt] \dfrac{k A_j}{|\mathbf{r}_i-\mathbf{r}_j|} & i\ne j\end{cases}$$

The diagonal (self-interaction) term can't use the point-charge formula since r → 0 there; instead each panel is approximated as a flat disk of the same area, radius $r_i=\sqrt{A_i/\pi}$, whose exact potential at its own center is the standard result $V_{\text{disk center}}=k\cdot 2\pi\sigma r_i = k\cdot 2\pi r_i$ (per unit σ), giving a consistent, non-singular diagonal entry.

This dense linear system is solved once per recompute using Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting (O(N³) — practical for the coarse calculation mesh sizes used here), and the resulting relative σ pattern is then uniformly rescaled so the surface's total (or, for two-body shapes like Parallel Plates / Dipole, each group's) charge matches the requested Q.

Performance notes

  • Field-arrow and equipotential-contour sampling use the coarse calculation mesh, since each sample point requires an O(N) sum over every panel.
  • Geometry/parameter changes are debounced (~160 ms) before the full BEM recompute fires, so dragging sliders stays smooth.
  • Increasing "Mesh / charge grid size" trades recompute speed for numerical accuracy — useful when checking a shape against an exact formula (Tab 7).

Far-field expansion of 1/|r−r′|

For an observation point far from a bounded charge distribution (r ≫ r′, i.e. r much larger than the size of the object), 1/|r−r′| can be Taylor-expanded in powers of r′/r — the standard multipole expansion. In terms of the angle γ between r and r′ (using Legendre polynomials Pℓ):

$$\frac{1}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{r'}{r}\right)^{\!\ell} P_\ell(\cos\gamma)$$

Substituting into $V=k\int_S \sigma(\mathbf r')/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|\,dA'$ and integrating term by term gives the multipole series for V:

$$V(\mathbf r)\approx \frac{k}{r}\underbrace{\int_S\sigma\,dA'}_{Q\ (\text{monopole})} +\frac{k}{r^2}\underbrace{\int_S\sigma\,r'\cos\gamma\,dA'}_{\text{dipole term}} +\frac{k}{r^3}\big(\text{quadrupole term}\big)+\cdots$$

Monopole term

The leading 1/r term depends only on the total charge Q = ∫σ dA′ — every detail of the shape washes out. This is why, viewed from far enough away, the Sphere, Ellipsoid, Torus, or any other shape with the same net charge all look identical: $V\to kQ/r$, $E\to kQ/r^2\,\hat{\mathbf r}$. This is the check used in the worked example in Tab 7.

Dipole term

When Q = 0 (equal and opposite charges, as in the Dipole, Quadrupole (Linear/Square), and Parallel Plates shapes here), the monopole term vanishes and the leading behavior is set by the dipole moment vector:

$$\mathbf p=\int_S \sigma(\mathbf r')\,\mathbf r'\,dA' \;\;(\text{for two point charges } \pm q \text{ separated by } d:\ p=qd)$$
$$V_{\text{dipole}}(\mathbf r)=\frac{k\,\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}, \qquad \mathbf E_{\text{dipole}}(\mathbf r)=\frac{k}{r^3}\Big(3(\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})\hat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf p\Big)$$

Notice the dipole field falls off as 1/r³ — one power of r faster than a monopole's 1/r² — and is no longer spherically symmetric: it's strongest along the dipole axis and weakest broadside. Try the Dipole shape with equal ± charges and compare the far-field arrow pattern to this formula.

Quadrupole and beyond

If both Q and p vanish (as for the Quadrupole (Linear) shape's +q,−2q,+q arrangement, or the alternating Charge Lattice), the leading term is the quadrupole moment (a rank-2 tensor), and V falls off as 1/r³, E as 1/r⁴ — falling off faster still. Each successive multipole order is more sensitive to fine structure and less important far away, which is the general pattern: far from any bounded charge distribution, only its lowest surviving multipole moment matters.

Worked check 1 — uniformly charged spherical shell (monopole, exact)

Pick the Sphere surface with uniform density. Every closed shape in this tool — Sphere, Ellipsoid, Torus, Prolate/Oblate Spheroid, and so on — is a hollow shell: charge only ever lives on the 2-D surface (σ, in C/m²), never spread through a 3-D volume (there is no ρ, in C/m³, anywhere in this tool). For radius R and total charge Q, the exact textbook results (Gauss's law, Tab 3) are:

$$V_{\text{surface}}=\frac{kQ}{R},\qquad E_{\text{just outside}}=\frac{kQ}{R^2},\qquad V(r>R)=\frac{kQ}{r},\qquad E(r>R)=\frac{kQ}{r^2}, \qquad E_{\text{inside}}=0$$

Set "Total Q" mode, hover the "Measure" probe just outside the sphere, and compare the readout to kQ/r and kQ/r² computed by hand — they should agree closely (small differences come from the panel mesh resolution and the 1 mm minimum-distance clamp near the surface). Probe well inside the sphere and you should read V ≈ constant (equal to the surface value) and E ≈ 0 — all the field lines terminate on the shell itself, none reach the interior.

Shell vs. solid: outside a closed shape, it makes no difference whether the same total charge Q sits on a thin shell or is spread uniformly through a solid volume — both give the identical external field kQ/r² by Gauss's law. Inside is where they differ: a shell gives E = 0 throughout the interior, while a uniformly charged solid ball would instead give E = kQr/R³, rising linearly from the center. Since this tool only ever models surface charge, it always reproduces the shell (E = 0 inside) behavior, not the solid-ball one.

Worked check 2 — charged ring on axis (exact, non-trivial shape)

For a ring of radius a carrying total charge Q, uniformly distributed, the field and potential on the symmetry axis (distance z from the ring's plane) have a simple closed form even though the ring isn't spherically symmetric:

$$V(z)=\frac{kQ}{\sqrt{z^2+a^2}},\qquad E(z)=\frac{kQz}{(z^2+a^2)^{3/2}}$$

Select the Ring Charge shape, probe along the z-axis at a few different heights, and compare — this is a good test of the discretization accuracy away from the high-symmetry sphere case, since the ring's field varies non-monotonically along its axis (E peaks near z ≈ a/√2, not at z = 0).

Worked check 3 — ideal parallel-plate capacitor

For two oppositely charged infinite plates separated by distance d, each with surface density ±σ, the field between the plates is uniform:

$$E_{\text{between}}=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}=\frac{4\pi k\sigma}{1},\qquad V_{\text{between}}(y)=E\cdot y \ (\text{linear ramp}),\qquad C=\frac{\varepsilon_0 A_{\text{plate}}}{d}$$

Select Parallel Plates with finite (but large) plate half-size a ≫ separation d — the finite-size version approximates the infinite-plate idealization near the center, where fringing fields are smallest. Away from the center, you'll see the field lines curve outward — the "fringing field" effect the infinite-plate formula ignores.

Tip: increasing "Mesh / charge grid size" in the Rendering panel refines the panel mesh and pushes every numerical result closer to its exact analytic answer, at the cost of slower recomputation. For the sharpest comparisons, also increase "Arrow density" so you have more sample points to check against.
