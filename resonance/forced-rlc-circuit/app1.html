Forced Series RLC Circuit

Equation Derivation · Analytical & Numerical Solution · Phasor · Frequency Response

📐   Theory — How is the RLC equation derived and solved? ▲
01Kirchhoff's Voltage Law & the ODE

In a series RLC circuit driven by a sinusoidal source Vscos(Ωt), a single loop current i(t) flows through all three components. Applying KVL — the sum of all voltage drops equals the source voltage:

V_L + V_R + V_C = V_s·cos(Ωt)

Substituting the component voltage–current relations:

  • L
    Inductor: V_L = L·di/dt  (opposes change in current)
  • R
    Resistor: V_R = R·i  (instantaneous Ohm's law)
  • C
    Capacitor: V_C = q/C,  where i = dq/dt

Writing everything in terms of charge q (since i = q̇):

L·q̈ + R·q̇ + (1/C)·q = V_s·cos(Ωt)

This is the governing ODE — a linear 2nd-order system identical in structure to the mass–spring–damper equation, with the electro-mechanical analogy: L↔m, R↔c, 1/C↔k.

02Standard Form & Key Parameters

Dividing by L converts to the canonical form with two characteristic quantities:

q̈ + (R/L)·q̇ + (1/LC)·q = (V_s/L)·cos(Ωt)
SymbolNameFormula
ω₀Resonant frequency1/√(LC)
ζDamping ratioR/(2√(L/C))
QQuality factorω₀L/R = 1/(2ζ)
BW−3 dB BandwidthR/L = ω₀/Q rad/s

The Q factor measures how sharply the circuit resonates — a high-Q circuit stores energy much more efficiently than it dissipates it. At resonance, V_C and V_L can be Q times larger than V_s!

Impedance: Z(Ω) = R + j(ΩL − 1/ΩC)

At resonance Ω = ω₀, the reactive terms cancel: Z = R (purely resistive, minimum impedance, maximum current).

03Analytical Solution (Closed Form)

The general solution is the sum of the homogeneous (transient) and particular (steady-state) parts:

q(t) = q_h(t) + q_p(t)

The transient response q_h solves the unforced equation. Its character depends on ζ:

RegimeConditionq_h(t)
Under-dampedζ < 1e−ζω₀t[A cos(ωd t)+B sin(ωd t)]
Critically dampedζ = 1(A + Bt)·e−ω₀t
Over-dampedζ > 1A·es₁t + B·es₂t

The steady-state response (particular solution) for sinusoidal forcing:

q_p(t) = (V_s/L) · [P·cos(Ωt) + Q·sin(Ωt)] / (…)

where P = (ω₀²−Ω²) and Q = 2ζω₀Ω. Constants A, B are fixed by initial conditions q(0) = q₀, q̇(0) = i₀. The transient decays as e−ζω₀t, leaving only q_p at steady state.

04Numerical Solution — RK4

The 2nd-order ODE is split into two coupled 1st-order equations using state vector [q, i]:

q̇ = i
i̇ = [V_s cos(Ωt) − R·i − q/C] / L

The 4th-order Runge-Kutta method evaluates four slope estimates per time-step h = 1/500 s:

k₁ — start of interval
f(tₙ, yₙ)
k₂ — midpoint estimate 1
f(tₙ+h/2, yₙ+h·k₁/2)
k₃ — midpoint estimate 2
f(tₙ+h/2, yₙ+h·k₂/2)
k₄ — end of interval
f(tₙ+h, yₙ+h·k₃)
yₙ₊₁ = yₙ + (h/6)(k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄)

Local truncation error is O(h⁵). The simulation updates physics 500 times per second using a fixed-step accumulator, so the displayed waveform stays accurate regardless of monitor frame rate.

05Phasor Analysis & Impedance

At steady state, every voltage and current in a linear circuit oscillates at the driving frequency Ω. Phasors represent these sinusoids as rotating vectors in the complex plane:

I = V_s / Z_total  (complex division)
  • R
    Resistor — Z_R = R (real). V_R in phase with current.
  • L
    Inductor — Z_L = jΩL. V_L leads current by 90°.
  • C
    Capacitor — Z_C = −j/ΩC. V_C lags current by 90°.
|Z| = √[R² + (ΩL − 1/ΩC)²]
φ = arctan[(ΩL − 1/ΩC) / R]

The phasor diagram in the simulation shows V_R, V_L, V_C, and V_total as live rotating arrows. Watch how V_L and V_C (90° apart) can be many times larger than V_s near resonance — they cancel each other in the KVL sum, but individually they are enormous.

06Frequency Response & Resonance

The steady-state current amplitude as a function of driving frequency Ω:

|I(Ω)| = V_s / √[R² + (ΩL − 1/ΩC)²]

Three frequency regimes:

  • ▸
    Ω ≪ ω₀ — capacitor dominates (high Z_C). Current is small, lags source by ≈ 90°.
  • ★
    Ω = ω₀ — resonance. Z_L = Z_C cancel. |I| = V_s/R (maximum). Phase = 0°.
  • ▸
    Ω ≫ ω₀ — inductor dominates (high Z_L). Current is small, leads source by ≈ 90°.

The bandwidth is the frequency range where |I| ≥ |I_max|/√2 (−3 dB power points):

BW = R/L    Q = ω₀/BW = ω₀L/R

Try sweeping Ω toward ω₀ with small R to see resonant amplification. Lower R → sharper peak → higher Q → narrower bandwidth. This selectivity is the basis of radio tuning circuits.
