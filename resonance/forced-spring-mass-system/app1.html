Mass – Spring – Damper System

Equation Derivation  ·  Analytical & Numerical Solution  ·  Interactive Simulation

📖   Theory: How is the equation derived and solved? ▲
01 Free-Body Diagram & Newton's 2nd Law

Consider a mass m constrained to move in one direction (x), attached to a spring of stiffness k and a viscous damper with coefficient c. An external harmonic force F·cos(Ωt) may also be applied.

Three forces act on the mass at any instant:

  • 1
    Spring force — opposes displacement: F_s = −kx
  • 2
    Damping force — opposes velocity: F_d = −cẋ
  • 3
    External force: F(t) = F·cos(Ωt)

Applying ΣF = ma:

mẍ = −kx − cẋ + F·cos(Ωt)

Rearranging to the standard form (all terms on the left):

m·ẍ + c·ẋ + k·x = F·cos(Ωt)

This is a linear, second-order, ordinary differential equation with constant coefficients. Every term has a clear physical meaning: inertia, dissipation, and stiffness.

02 Dimensionless Standard Form

Dividing the entire equation by m introduces two fundamental parameters that fully characterise the system's dynamics:

ẍ + 2ζω₀ẋ + ω₀²x = (F/m)·cos(Ωt)

ζ alone determines the qualitative character of the free response — whether the system oscillates, decays exponentially, or returns without oscillation.

The critical damping coefficient is:

c_cr = 2√(mk) = 2m·ω₀

At ζ = 1 the system returns to rest as fast as possible without oscillating.

03 Analytical Solution (Closed Form)

For the free vibration case (F = 0) the solution is the homogeneous part only. The characteristic equation is:

ms² + cs + k = 0  →  s = −ζω₀ ± ω₀√(ζ²−1)

Three distinct cases arise depending on ζ:

CaseConditionSolution x(t)
Under-damped ζ < 1 e−ζω₀t[A cos(ωd t) + B sin(ωd t)]
Critically damped ζ = 1 (A + Bt)·e−ω₀t
Over-damped ζ > 1 A·es₁t + B·es₂t

The constants A, B are determined from initial conditions x(0) = x₀, ẋ(0) = v₀. For the under-damped case:

A = x₀    B = (v₀ + ζω₀x₀) / ωd

For forced vibration (F ≠ 0) the full solution is:

x(t) = x_homogeneous(t) + x_particular(t)

The particular (steady-state) solution is found by assuming x_p = X cos(Ωt) + Y sin(Ωt) and solving for the amplitudes X and Y algebraically.

04 Numerical Solution — Runge-Kutta 4

The 2nd-order ODE is rewritten as a system of two 1st-order ODEs by introducing the state vector [x, v]:

ẋ = v
v̇ = [F cos(Ωt) − cv − kx] / m

This is integrated forward in time using the 4th-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) method with time-step Δt = 1/200 s:

Stage 1 — slope at start
k₁ = f(tₙ, yₙ)
Stage 2 — slope at midpoint
k₂ = f(tₙ+h/2, yₙ+h·k₁/2)
Stage 3 — corrected midpoint
k₃ = f(tₙ+h/2, yₙ+h·k₂/2)
Stage 4 — slope at end
k₄ = f(tₙ+h, yₙ+h·k₃)
yₙ₊₁ = yₙ + (h/6)(k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄)

RK4 achieves 4th-order accuracy — the local truncation error is O(h⁵). Compared to simple Euler (1st-order), it remains stable and accurate over hundreds of oscillation cycles. You can verify this directly in the simulation: the cyan (numerical) and orange (analytical) curves are nearly indistinguishable.

The simulation uses a fixed-timestep accumulator: each animation frame advances the physics by as many Δt steps as real time has elapsed, keeping physics independent of frame rate.

05 Phase Portrait — Geometric Insight

Instead of plotting x vs. time, a phase portrait plots displacement x against velocity ẋ. This 2D picture reveals the complete qualitative behaviour of the system:

  • ▸
    Under-damped — inward spiral converging to origin (equilibrium)
  • ▸
    Critically damped — direct path to origin, no loops
  • ▸
    Over-damped — slow monotone approach to origin
  • ▸
    Undamped (c = 0) — closed ellipse, energy conserved forever
  • ▸
    Forced — at steady state the trajectory becomes a closed loop (limit cycle)

The gradient colouring in the simulation (cyan → teal) encodes time: recent history appears brighter, so you can read the direction of motion directly from the colour.

Energy: E = ½mv² + ½kx²

Constant energy ↔ ellipse in phase space. Damping dissipates energy, shrinking the ellipse into a spiral toward (0, 0).

06 Forced Response & Resonance

When a harmonic force F·cos(Ωt) is applied, the steady-state amplitude of the particular solution is:

X = (F/k) / √[(1−r²)² + (2ζr)²]

where r = Ω/ω₀ is the frequency ratio. The factor in the denominator is the Dynamic Magnification Factor (DMF).

  • ▸
    r ≪ 1 (slow forcing) — DMF ≈ 1, response ≈ static deflection F/k
  • ▸
    r = 1 (resonance) — DMF = 1/(2ζ), amplitude can be enormous for small ζ
  • ▸
    r ≫ 1 (fast forcing) — DMF → 0, mass barely moves

Try it in the simulation:

  • 1
    Set F > 0, then sweep Ω toward ω₀ — watch the amplitude explode.
  • 2
    Increase c — the resonance peak flattens and shifts left.
  • 3
    Set c = 0 and Ω = ω₀ — the amplitude grows without bound (beat phenomenon).
