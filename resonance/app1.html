Resonance

The phenomenon where a system absorbs energy most efficiently — oscillating at its natural frequency with dramatically amplified amplitude.

01 — Definition

What is Resonance?

Resonance occurs when an external periodic force drives a system at or near its natural (eigen) frequency, causing the system's oscillation amplitude to grow — potentially to extreme values in the ideal lossless case.

Every physical system — a pendulum, a bridge, an atom, an electrical circuit — has one or more natural frequencies at which it oscillates freely after a disturbance. When a driving frequency matches one of these natural frequencies, energy transfer from driver to system becomes maximally efficient, leading to resonance.

In a damped system, the response remains finite but still reaches a sharp peak near the natural frequency. The sharpness of this peak is quantified by the quality factor Q.

Resonance Condition
ω_drive ≈ ω₀ = √(k/m)

where ω₀ is the natural angular frequency, k is the restoring force constant, and m is the effective mass (or analogous inertial quantity).

Amplitude at Resonance (Driven Harmonic Oscillator)
A(ω) = F₀/m · 1/√[(ω₀²−ω²)² + (γω)²]

F₀ = driving force amplitude, γ = damping coefficient. At ω = ω₀ the denominator is minimised and amplitude is maximised.

03 — Classification

Types of Resonance

Resonance is not confined to mechanical springs. The same mathematical structure appears across every branch of physics, engineering, and even chemistry and biology.

⚙️
Mechanical Resonance

A physical structure vibrates at its natural mode frequency. Classic examples: Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse (1940), tuning forks, and wine-glass singing. Relevant in civil, aerospace, and mechanical engineering.

Classical Mechanics
🔊
Acoustic Resonance

Standing sound waves build up in cavities (organ pipes, concert halls, vocal tracts). The cavity geometry selects allowed wavelengths: λ = 2L/n. Exploited in all musical instruments.

Acoustics
⚡
Electrical Resonance

In an LC or RLC circuit, resonance occurs at ω₀ = 1/√(LC). At this frequency impedance is purely resistive and current is maximal. Underpins radio tuning, filters, and wireless power transfer.

Electromagnetism
🌊
Optical Resonance

Light bouncing between mirrors in a Fabry–Pérot cavity builds standing electromagnetic waves. Atomic transitions absorb light at precise resonant frequencies — the basis of spectroscopy and lasers.

Optics / EM
🧲
Nuclear Magnetic Resonance

Nuclear spins in a magnetic field precess at the Larmor frequency. An RF pulse at that frequency flips spins; relaxation emits detectable signals. The foundation of MRI and NMR spectroscopy.

Quantum / Medical
⚛️
Particle Physics Resonances

Short-lived intermediate states in scattering — Δ(1232), ρ meson, Z boson — appear as peaks in the cross-section vs. energy. Described by the Breit–Wigner formula, analogous to the classical resonance curve.

High-Energy Physics
🪐
Orbital Resonance

Celestial bodies exert periodic gravitational kicks when their orbital periods are in small integer ratios (e.g. 1:2, 2:3). Jupiter's moons Io, Europa, and Ganymede are locked in a 1:2:4 Laplace resonance.

Astrophysics
🧪
Plasma Resonance

Electromagnetic waves can propagate in plasma only above the plasma frequency ωₚ. Below it, waves are reflected — explaining ionospheric radio reflection. Relevant in fusion reactors and astrophysical plasmas.

Plasma Physics

04 — Quality Factor

The Q Factor

The quality factor Q measures how sharp a resonance is — equivalently, how much energy is stored per cycle relative to energy lost.

Quality Factor
Q = ω₀ / Δω = ω₀ · m / γ = 2π · E_stored / E_lost_per_cycle

Δω is the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of the power resonance curve. High Q → narrow bandwidth, long ring-down time.

Interpreting Q

Low Q (< 1) — Overdamped system; no oscillations; energy dissipates quickly. Example: door dampers.

Medium Q (10–100) — Musical instruments, loudspeakers. Broad resonance, rich sustained tone.

High Q (> 1000) — Atomic clocks, optical cavities, superconducting microwave resonators. Extremely sharp frequency selectivity.

05 — Key Equations

The Mathematics of Resonance

Equation of Motion — Damped Driven Oscillator
mẍ + γẋ + kx = F₀ cos(ωt)

The universal model. Replace m→L, γ→R, k→1/C and x→q to get the RLC circuit equation.

Steady-State Phase Lag
φ = arctan(γω / (ω₀² − ω²))

At resonance (ω = ω₀): φ = 90°. The response lags the drive by exactly a quarter period.

Breit–Wigner (Particle Physics)
σ(E) ∝ Γ² / [(E − E₀)² + (Γ/2)²]

Cross-section near a resonance of energy E₀ and total decay width Γ = ℏ/τ (τ = lifetime). Identical in form to the classical Lorentzian.

Larmor / NMR Resonance Frequency
ω_L = γ_N · B₀

γ_N is the nuclear gyromagnetic ratio, B₀ the static magnetic field. MRI exploits the linear dependence on B₀ for spatial encoding.

Breit–Wigner area theorem
∫σ(E) dE = (2π²ℏ²/μ) · (2J+1)/[(2s₁+1)(2s₂+1)]

The integrated cross-section is conserved regardless of width — a narrow resonance is tall and thin; a broad one is short and wide. Total probability is conserved.

Three distinct physical mechanisms can trap a particle or wave in a quasi-bound state, producing resonance behaviour even without a conventional potential well.
I
Shape Resonance

A particle is temporarily trapped behind a centrifugal or potential-energy barrier. Although above the dissociation threshold, it must tunnel through the barrier to escape. The probability density peaks inside the barrier region.

Key feature: purely single-channel phenomenon.
Origin of width: tunnelling rate through the barrier.
Examples: α-decay (Gamow tunnelling), ℓ>0 scattering resonances in atomic collisions, predissociation of diatomic molecules.
II
Feshbach Resonance

The system has at least two coupled channels with different thresholds. A true bound state in a closed (energetically inaccessible) channel couples to the open continuum, creating a resonance — even though no barrier exists in the open channel alone.

Key feature: requires channel coupling V₁₂ ≠ 0.
Width: Γ ∝ |V₁₂|² (coupling strength).
Examples: Feshbach resonances in ultracold atoms (magnetically tuned by adjusting B, used to control scattering length), compound-nucleus resonances in neutron scattering.
III
Gamow / Quasi-bound States

Named after George Gamow (1928), these are solutions of the Schrödinger equation with purely outgoing boundary conditions. The energy eigenvalue becomes complex: E = E₀ − iΓ/2, giving an exponentially decaying norm.

Key feature: complex eigenvalue; not normalisable in L².
Pole location: Im(k) < 0 sheet of complex k-plane.
Examples: all radioactive α-decays, virtual (antibound) states near threshold, unstable heavy nuclei.
In quantum scattering theory, every resonance leaves a unique and equivalent mathematical trace. These three signatures are different projections of the same underlying S-matrix pole.
𝕊
Poles of the S-matrix

The S-matrix S(E) is an analytic function of energy. A resonance corresponds to a pole at complex energy E_pole = E₀ − iΓ/2 on the unphysical Riemann sheet (Im E < 0). The Breit–Wigner form is the Laurent expansion around this pole.

S(E) ~ e²ⁱδ = 1 + iΓ/(E₀−E−iΓ/2)

Poles on the real axis ↔ true bound states. Poles just below real axis ↔ narrow resonances. Deep poles ↔ broad resonances.

σ
Peak in Cross-Section

The ℓ-th partial wave contribution to the total cross-section shows a Lorentzian peak centred on E₀ with FWHM = Γ. This is the experimentally observable signature — a bump in the measured cross-section vs. beam energy plot.

σ_ℓ(E) = 4π(2ℓ+1)/k² · Γ²/4/[(E−E₀)²+Γ²/4]

The height at the peak is σ_max = 4π(2ℓ+1)/k₀², limited only by unitarity (the "unitarity limit").

δ
Rapid Phase Shift (π-jump)

The phase shift δ_ℓ(E) of the ℓ-th partial wave increases by π through every resonance. Far below resonance δ_ℓ → 0; far above δ_ℓ → π (or nπ for n prior resonances). The rate of increase is steepest at E₀.

δ_ℓ(E) = arctan[ Γ/2/(E₀−E) ] + nπ

Levinson's theorem: δ_ℓ(0) − δ_ℓ(∞) = n_b · π, where n_b counts bound states — each resonance contributes one π unit of phase.

The three signatures — S-matrix pole, cross-section peak, phase-shift π-jump — are equivalent: any one of them completely determines the other two. This is guaranteed by the analyticity and unitarity of the S-matrix.

08 — Applications

Engineering Resonance

Resonance can be both a powerful tool and a catastrophic hazard. Understanding it is central to countless technologies.

📡
Radio & Wireless

LC and crystal resonators select specific frequencies for transmission and reception. Quartz oscillators exploit piezoelectric resonance (Q ~ 10⁵).

🏥
Medical Imaging

MRI uses NMR to image soft tissue non-invasively. Ultrasound transducers operate at piezoelectric resonance for high sensitivity.

⚠️
Structural Hazards

Resonance must be avoided in bridges, aircraft, and tall buildings. Engineers detune structures or add dampers to shift natural frequencies away from expected excitation sources.

🔬
Spectroscopy

Atoms and molecules absorb photons only at resonant frequencies — their fingerprint. IR, UV-Vis, and NMR spectroscopy all exploit resonance absorption for chemical identification.
