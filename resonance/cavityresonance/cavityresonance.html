Maxwell's Equations — The Foundation of Electromagnetism

All electromagnetic phenomena in this simulator—field patterns, resonance, wave propagation—arise directly from four coupled partial differential equations formulated by James Clerk Maxwell in 1865.

Differential Form (in free space)

∇ · E = ρ/ε₀ (Gauss's Law — electric) ∇ · B = 0 (Gauss's Law — magnetic, no monopoles) ∇ × E = −∂B/∂t (Faraday's Law of induction) ∇ × H = J + ∂D/∂t (Ampère-Maxwell Law)

Where: E = electric field [V/m], H = magnetic field [A/m], B = μH = magnetic flux density [T], D = εE = electric displacement [C/m²], ρ = charge density [C/m³], J = current density [A/m²]

Inside a Perfect Metal Cavity (source-free)

ρ = 0, J = 0 → source-free Maxwell equations: ∇ × E = −μ ∂H/∂t ∇ × H = ε ∂E/∂t ∇ · E = 0 ∇ · H = 0
Faraday's Law says a changing magnetic field induces an electric field. Ampère's Law says a changing electric field induces a magnetic field. Together, they create self-sustaining oscillations — electromagnetic waves.

Material Constitutive Relations

Inside the cavity

D = εE = ε₀εᵣE
B = μH = μ₀μᵣH

For air/vacuum: εᵣ = μᵣ = 1

Speed of Light

Derived from Maxwell's equations:
phase velocity in medium

c = 1/√(με) = 3×10⁸ m/s

Integral Form

∮ E·dA = Q_enc/ε₀ (total flux through closed surface) ∮ B·dA = 0 (no magnetic monopoles) ∮ E·dl = −dΦ_B/dt (EMF = rate of flux change) ∮ H·dl = I_enc + dΦ_E/dt (circulation of H)

The integral form is used to derive boundary conditions at material interfaces and at conductor walls — the key to understanding cavity mode shapes.

The Electromagnetic Wave Equation

Taking the curl of Faraday's Law and substituting Ampère's Law eliminates one field and yields the wave equation — governing how fields propagate through space and time.

Derivation

1
Start with Faraday's Law: ∇ × E = −μ ∂H/∂t
2
Take curl of both sides: ∇ × (∇ × E) = −μ ∂/∂t (∇ × H)
3
Use vector identity: ∇ × (∇ × E) = ∇(∇·E) − ∇²E = −∇²E (since ∇·E = 0)
4
Substitute Ampère's Law ∇ × H = ε ∂E/∂t into step 2
∇²E = με ∂²E/∂t² ∇²H = με ∂²H/∂t² These are the vector wave equations for E and H fields.

Scalar Form (2D cavity)

∂²u/∂x² + ∂²u/∂y² = (1/c²) ∂²u/∂t² where c = 1/√(με) = phase velocity

Standing Wave Solution

In a bounded cavity with PEC walls, the spatial part must satisfy Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions. Separation of variables gives:

u(x,y,t) = X(x)·Y(y)·T(t) X(x) = A·sin(kₓx) + B·cos(kₓx) Y(y) = C·sin(k_y·y) + D·cos(k_y·y) T(t) = E·cos(ωt) + F·sin(ωt) Dispersion relation: kₓ² + k_y² = ω²με = k²
Wavenumbers

kₓ = mπ/a (x-direction)
k_y = nπ/b (y-direction)

m, n = integers (mode indices)

Cutoff condition

kc² = kₓ² + k_y²

Waves exist only if ω ≥ ωc = c·kc
(below cutoff: evanescent decay)

The dispersion relation kₓ² + k_y² = ω²/c² is the heart of cavity resonance. It directly gives the discrete resonant frequencies: only specific (m,n) combinations satisfy the boundary conditions.

Boundary Conditions at Cavity Walls

The cavity walls are modelled as Perfect Electric Conductors (PEC). Inside a PEC, E = 0 and H = 0. Applying Maxwell's equations at the interface determines how fields must behave at the walls.

Conditions at a PEC Surface

E_tangential = 0 (no tangential E at PEC) H_normal = 0 (no normal H at PEC) D_normal = ρ_s (surface charge density) H_tangential = J_s (surface current density)

The tangential E = 0 condition means the electric field must be zero everywhere along the wall surface. The normal H = 0 condition means the magnetic field must be parallel to the wall (no component penetrating it).

Consequence for Mode Shapes

TE modes

Eₙ = 0 at walls → E∥ can be non-zero at walls

Hz uses cosines → non-zero at x=0
Ey uses sines → zero at x=0, x=a ✓
Ex uses sines → zero at y=0, y=b ✓

TM modes

Ez = 0 at all walls → must use sines in both directions

Ez = sin(mπx/a)sin(nπy/b) → zero at all four walls ✓

Both m ≥ 1 and n ≥ 1 required.

Physical Interpretation

E
Tangential E = 0: Free charges in the metal redistribute instantly, creating surface charges that cancel any tangential field. This is why E-field lines meet walls perpendicularly.
H
Normal H = 0: The continuity of magnetic flux (∇·B = 0) combined with B = 0 inside the conductor forces the normal component to vanish. H-field lines run along walls.
J
Surface currents: Jₛ = n̂ × H flows on the conductor surface. These currents sustain the magnetic field and are responsible for ohmic losses in real (non-ideal) cavities.
Boundary conditions are what quantize the allowed modes. Only discrete (m,n) pairs satisfy all four wall conditions simultaneously — this is the origin of the mode spectrum shown in the resonance chart.

Transverse Electric (TE) Modes

In TE modes, the electric field has no component along the z-axis (propagation direction). The longitudinal magnetic field Hz is the source term from which all other field components are derived.

Ez = 0 everywhere Hz ≠ 0 (longitudinal H) TE₁₀ is dominant mode

Field Component Equations

Hz = H₀ cos(mπx/a) cos(nπy/b) cos(ωt) Ez = 0 Ex = +(ωμ/kc²)(nπ/b) cos(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b) sin(ωt) Ey = −(ωμ/kc²)(mπ/a) sin(mπx/a) cos(nπy/b) sin(ωt) Hx = +(jβ/kc²)(mπ/a) sin(mπx/a) cos(nπy/b) sin(ωt) Hy = +(jβ/kc²)(nπ/b) cos(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b) sin(ωt) where kc² = (mπ/a)² + (nπ/b)²

The Dominant TE₁₀ Mode

With m=1, n=0, this is the lowest-frequency TE mode (n=0 eliminates terms involving nπ/b). It has a single half-cosine field variation across the width a.

Hz = H₀ cos(πx/a) cos(ωt) [standing wave] Ey = −(ωμa/π) H₀ sin(πx/a) sin(ωt) [only E component] Ex = 0, Ez = 0 [no other E components] Resonant frequency: f₁₀ = c/(2a) Cutoff wavelength: λ_c = 2a

For TE modes, if n = 0, the mode is uniform in y — requiring only a single-dimension boundary condition in x. This is why TE₁₀ exists but TM₁₀ does not: TM requires sin functions in both x and y simultaneously.

Transverse Magnetic (TM) Modes

In TM modes, the magnetic field has no z-component. The longitudinal electric field Ez drives all other components. TM modes require both m ≥ 1 and n ≥ 1 — they cannot have a zero index in either direction.

Hz = 0 everywhere Ez ≠ 0 (longitudinal E) Min. indices: m≥1, n≥1

Field Component Equations

Ez = E₀ sin(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b) cos(ωt) Hz = 0 Ex = −(jβ/kc²)(mπ/a) cos(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b) sin(ωt) Ey = −(jβ/kc²)(nπ/b) sin(mπx/a) cos(nπy/b) sin(ωt) Hx = +(jωε/kc²)(nπ/b) sin(mπx/a) cos(nπy/b) sin(ωt) Hy = −(jωε/kc²)(mπ/a) cos(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b) sin(ωt) where kc² = (mπ/a)² + (nπ/b)²

Why TM₁₀ and TM₀₁ Don't Exist

For TM modes, Ez = E₀ sin(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b). If m=0, sin(0) = 0 everywhere → trivial (zero) solution. Same for n=0. Therefore the minimum TM mode is TM₁₁.

TM₁₁ — The Lowest TM Mode

Ez = E₀ sin(πx/a) sin(πy/b) cos(ωt) Hx = +(jωε/kc²)(π/b) sin(πx/a) cos(πy/b) sin(ωt) Hy = −(jωε/kc²)(π/a) cos(πx/a) sin(πy/b) sin(ωt) Resonant frequency: f₁₁ = (c/2)√[(1/a)² + (1/b)²] The H-field curls in closed loops around the Ez maximum.

Key Differences vs TE Modes

TM strengths

• On-axis Ez field accelerates particles (used in accelerator cavities)
• Simpler H-field topology (loops)
• Ez maxima in interior of cavity, not near walls

TM applications

• Particle accelerator RF cavities (TM₀₁₀)
• Microwave oscillators
• Cavity filters requiring axial coupling
• Electron cyclotron resonance sources

TM modes have their electric field maximum on the cavity axis. This makes them ideal for particle acceleration — a charged particle traveling along the axis experiences the maximum accelerating gradient from the Ez field.

Resonance Conditions & Mode Spectrum

Resonance occurs when the cavity dimensions allow standing waves to form — a self-consistent field pattern that satisfies boundary conditions on all walls simultaneously. Each (m,n) pair defines a unique resonant frequency.

Resonant Frequency Formula

f_mn = (c/2) √[ (m/a)² + (n/b)² ] where: c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s (speed of light in vacuum) m = number of half-wave variations in x (0,1,2,...) n = number of half-wave variations in y (0,1,2,...) a = cavity width [m] b = cavity height [m]

Cutoff Wavenumber

kc = π √[ (m/a)² + (n/b)² ] [rad/m] ωc = c · kc (cutoff angular frequency) λc = 2π/kc (cutoff wavelength) Below cutoff (f < f_mn): fields decay evanescently. At resonance (f = f_mn): standing wave sustained.

Mode Degeneracy

Two different modes can share the same resonant frequency — this is called mode degeneracy. For a square cavity (a = b), many TE and TM modes become degenerate:

Square cavity: a = b → f_mn = f_nm (degenerate pair) TE₁₂ ↔ TE₂₁ at same frequency TE₁₁ ↔ TM₁₁ (always degenerate when a = b)

Mode Density

As frequency increases, the number of modes per frequency interval (mode density) grows rapidly. At high frequencies, many modes coexist, causing complex multi-mode behavior in microwave systems.

3D Cavity Extension

For a 3D rectangular cavity (a × b × d): f_mnp = (c/2) √[ (m/a)² + (n/b)² + (p/d)² ] Adds a third index p for the z-direction half-waves. TE and TM degeneracy is partially broken by different boundary conditions in the third dimension.

Poynting Vector & Energy Flow

The Poynting vector describes the instantaneous direction and magnitude of electromagnetic power flow. It is the cross product of E and H fields and has units of Watts per square metre [W/m²].

Definition

S = E × H [W/m²] Time-averaged Poynting vector: ⟨S⟩ = ½ Re(E × H*) [W/m²] where H* is the complex conjugate of H.

Physical Meaning

Energy flux

|S| = power crossing unit area perpendicular to S. Total power through surface A:

P = ∮ S · dA [Watts]

In a resonating cavity

⟨S⟩ = 0 in interior (standing wave — equal forward/backward flow). S ≠ 0 at walls → power absorbed by finite conductivity.

Energy Density

Electric energy density: u_E = ½ε|E|² [J/m³] Magnetic energy density: u_H = ½μ|H|² [J/m³] Total energy density: u = u_E + u_H At resonance in a lossless cavity: ⟨u_E⟩ = ⟨u_H⟩ (energy equipartition) Total stored energy: W = ∫∫∫ u dV

Poynting Theorem (Energy Conservation)

∂u/∂t + ∇·S = −J·E Physical interpretation: ∂u/∂t = rate of change of stored EM energy ∇·S = net power flux out of volume J·E = power delivered to charges (Joule heating) Integrated over cavity volume: dW/dt = −∮ S·dA − P_loss

Visualization in This Simulator

Selecting "Poynting" view shows the cross product magnitude |E × H| using the Inferno colormap. In a standing wave cavity, this oscillates at twice the field frequency. The time-averaged pattern reveals where energy circulates between electric and magnetic form — typically creating rotating energy vortices near the cavity walls.

In the TE₁₀ mode, the Poynting vector shows energy sloshing back and forth between the wide walls. At quarter-period intervals: all energy is electric (E max, H = 0), then all magnetic (H max, E = 0) — a perfect electromagnetic harmonic oscillator analogous to a mass-spring system.

Quality Factor (Q)

The Q-factor quantifies how efficiently a resonator stores energy relative to how quickly it loses it. A high-Q cavity stores energy for many oscillation cycles before dissipating it — enabling sharp resonance peaks and long coherence times.

Fundamental Definition

Q = ω₀ · (Energy stored) / (Power dissipated) Q = ω₀ · W / P_loss Equivalently: Q = f₀ / Δf (resonant freq. / 3dB bandwidth) Q = ω₀ · τ (τ = 1/e energy decay time)

Finite-Difference Time-Domain (FDTD) Method

FDTD is a direct numerical solution of Maxwell's equations in the time domain. It discretises space and time on a staggered grid (Yee grid) and updates E and H fields alternately — a leapfrog scheme that conserves energy and satisfies Gauss's laws automatically.

The Yee Grid (1966)

Kane Yee's key insight: stagger E and H fields in both space and time. E-field components sit at cell edges; H-field components sit at cell face centres. E at integer time steps, H at half-integer steps. This ensures central differences are always second-order accurate.

1D update equations (Ez, Hy): Hy^(n+½)[i] = Hy^(n-½)[i] − (Δt/μ·Δx)(Ez^n[i+1] − Ez^n[i]) Ez^(n+1)[i] = Ez^n[i] − (Δt/ε·Δx)(Hy^(n+½)[i] − Hy^(n+½)[i-1]) Boundary conditions (PEC): Ez[0] = Ez[Nmax] = 0

Courant Stability Condition

For stable (non-diverging) simulation: Δt ≤ Δx / (c·√D) where D = number of dimensions 1D: Δt ≤ Δx/c → Courant number S = c·Δt/Δx ≤ 1 2D: Δt ≤ Δx/(c·√2) → S ≤ 1/√2 3D: Δt ≤ Δx/(c·√3) → S ≤ 1/√3 This simulator uses S = 0.95 (slightly below limit for safety)

Accuracy & Numerical Dispersion

FDTD introduces numerical dispersion: the simulated phase velocity depends weakly on wavelength. For accurate results, spatial resolution should be at least 10–20 cells per wavelength (Δx ≤ λ/10). The simulator uses 120 grid points across the cavity.

FDTD strengths

• Time-domain broadband results
• Natural handling of dispersive media
• Straightforward implementation
• Parallelises efficiently on GPU

FDTD limitations

• Staircase approximation for curved boundaries
• Memory: O(N³) in 3D
• Numerical dispersion at coarse grids
• Requires fine time step for accuracy

Comparing FDTD to Analytical Solution

The "FDTD vs Analytical" tab plots the numerical Ez field (green) against the exact analytical standing wave (dashed gold). At t=0 they match perfectly; as the simulation progresses, small phase errors accumulate — this is numerical dispersion. The error grows as ~ (π/λ)² (Δx)² per unit distance.

FDTD was originally developed for antenna and scattering problems (Yee 1966). Today it is the workhorse of computational electromagnetics, used for everything from designing iPhone antennas and photonic chips to simulating radar cross-sections of stealth aircraft.

Runge-Kutta RK4 Integration

The Runge-Kutta 4th-order method (RK4) is a family of explicit ODE solvers with 4th-order accuracy — error per step ∝ h⁵. It is used here to numerically integrate particle trajectories through the field for the particle-tracing visualization.

The ODE Problem

Given: dψ/dt = f(t, ψ) For a particle at position (x,y) in the field: dx/dt = vₓ(x,y,t) = Eₓ(x,y,t) / |E| dy/dt = v_y(x,y,t) = E_y(x,y,t) / |E| Goal: advance (x,y) from t to t + h accurately.

RK4 Algorithm

k₁ = h · f(t, ψ) k₂ = h · f(t + h/2, ψ + k₁/2) k₃ = h · f(t + h/2, ψ + k₂/2) k₄ = h · f(t + h, ψ + k₃) ψ_{n+1} = ψ_n + (k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄) / 6 Geometric interpretation: k₁ = slope at start k₂ = slope at midpoint (using k₁) k₃ = slope at midpoint (using k₂, more accurate) k₄ = slope at end Final: weighted average — most weight on midpoints

Why Not Euler's Method?

Euler (1st order)

ψ_{n+1} = ψ_n + h·f(t,ψ)

Error per step: O(h²)
Global error: O(h)

Simple but inaccurate — particles spiral to wrong positions for large h

RK4 (4th order)

4 function evaluations per step

Error per step: O(h⁵)
Global error: O(h⁴)

1000× more accurate than Euler for same step size h

Phase Portrait Connection

The "Phase Portrait" tab integrates a point in the centre of the cavity and plots Ez versus dEz/dt. For a perfect resonance with no damping, this traces a perfect ellipse — a hallmark of simple harmonic motion. Real cavities with finite Q spiral inward as energy is lost.

RK4 was developed by Carl Runge (1895) and Wilhelm Kutta (1901). It remains one of the most widely used numerical methods in science and engineering — from celestial mechanics and plasma simulation to the field-line tracing in this simulator.
