I. QUANTUM MECHANICS — FOUNDATIONS
WAVE-PARTICLE DUALITY · SCHRÖDINGER EQUATION · OPERATORS · UNCERTAINTY
The Schrödinger Equation

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation is the central law of non-relativistic quantum mechanics. It describes how the quantum state ψ(r,t) — the wavefunction — evolves in time under a Hamiltonian operator Ĥ.

iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ

Ĥ = −ℏ²/2m · ∇² + V(r,t)

Time-independent form:
Ĥψₙ = Eₙψₙ

The time-independent form is an eigenvalue equation: only specific energies Eₙ are allowed (energy quantization). The spatial part ψₙ(r) is the stationary state; the full solution oscillates as ψₙ(r)·e^(−iEₙt/ℏ).

Physical meaning of ψ: The wavefunction itself has no direct physical meaning. However, |ψ(r,t)|² gives the probability density of finding the particle at position r at time t. This Born rule interpretation connects the mathematical formalism to measurable outcomes.
Operators & Observables

In quantum mechanics, every physical observable (position, momentum, energy) is represented by a Hermitian operator. Measurement yields an eigenvalue; repeated measurements on identical states give a probability distribution.

Position: x̂ = x (multiply by x)
Momentum: p̂ = −iℏ ∂/∂x
Energy: Ê = iℏ ∂/∂t = Ĥ

Commutator: [x̂, p̂] = iℏ

The commutator [x̂, p̂] = iℏ ≠ 0 encodes the Heisenberg uncertainty principle: position and momentum cannot both be precisely known simultaneously.

Heisenberg Uncertainty:
Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ/2
ΔE · Δt ≥ ℏ/2
Particle in a Box — Exact Solution

The infinite square well (particle in a box) is the paradigm exactly-soluble quantum system. A particle of mass m is confined to 0 ≤ x ≤ L by infinite potential walls. The boundary conditions ψ(0) = ψ(L) = 0 quantize the allowed wavelengths.

Step 1 — Schrödinger equation inside the box (V = 0):
−ℏ²/2m · d²ψ/dx² = Eψ
d²ψ/dx² = −k²ψ, k² = 2mE/ℏ²
Step 2 — General solution:
ψ(x) = A·sin(kx) + B·cos(kx)
Step 3 — Boundary conditions ψ(0)=0 → B=0; ψ(L)=0 → kL = nπ:
kₙ = nπ/L, n = 1, 2, 3, ...
Step 4 — Normalize ∫|ψ|²dx = 1 → A = √(2/L):
ψₙ(x) = √(2/L) · sin(nπx/L)
Eₙ = n²π²ℏ²/(2mL²) = n²·E₁
Key physical results: (1) Energy is quantized — only Eₙ = n²E₁ are allowed. (2) Zero-point energy E₁ > 0 — even the ground state has kinetic energy (a consequence of the uncertainty principle). (3) The n-th eigenstate has n−1 nodes. (4) The energy spacing grows as n, becoming nearly continuous for large n (correspondence principle).
Superposition & Time Evolution

A general state is a superposition of energy eigenstates. Each component oscillates at its own frequency, leading to interference — the quantum system "beats" between configurations.

Ψ(x,t) = Σₙ cₙ ψₙ(x) e^(−iEₙt/ℏ)

|Ψ(x,t)|² = Σₙ|cₙ|²|ψₙ|² + interference terms

Beat frequency: ω_mn = (Em−En)/ℏ

The interference terms oscillate at frequencies corresponding to energy differences. This is directly observable in quantum systems — for example, the 1s+2p superposition in hydrogen gives an oscillating dipole moment at the transition frequency, which radiates a photon.

II. NMR & ESR — SPIN PHYSICS
NUCLEAR SPIN · ZEEMAN EFFECT · LARMOR PRECESSION · BLOCH EQUATIONS · RELAXATION
Nuclear Spin & Magnetic Moment

Atomic nuclei with odd mass number (¹H, ¹³C, ¹⁵N, ³¹P) possess intrinsic angular momentum called nuclear spin I. Like a tiny bar magnet, a spinning nucleus has a magnetic moment μ proportional to its spin.

μ = γ · ℏI

γ = gyromagnetic ratio (nucleus-specific)
γ(¹H) = 26.752 × 10⁶ rad/(s·T)
γ(¹³C) = 6.728 × 10⁶ rad/(s·T)

In the absence of a magnetic field, all nuclear spin orientations are degenerate (equal energy). Applying an external field B₀ breaks this symmetry via the Zeeman effect.

Zeeman energy levels:
E_m = −γ·ℏ·B₀·m
m = −I, −I+1, ..., +I

For ¹H (I = 1/2): two levels
ΔE = γℏB₀ = ℏω₀
Larmor precession: The spin angular momentum precesses around B₀ at the Larmor frequency ω₀ = γB₀, analogous to how a gyroscope precesses under gravity. This classical picture correctly predicts the resonance condition even though the underlying physics is quantum mechanical.
Bloch Equations — Macroscopic Spin Dynamics

Felix Bloch (1946) formulated phenomenological equations for the macroscopic magnetization vector M = (Mx, My, Mz) of an ensemble of nuclear spins. They combine precession with empirical relaxation terms.

dMx/dt = γ(MyBz − MzBy) − Mx/T₂
dMy/dt = γ(MzBx − MxBz) − My/T₂
dMz/dt = γ(MxBy − MyBx) − (Mz−M₀)/T₁

Compact form: dM/dt = γ(M × B) − R·(M−M₀)
T₁ (spin-lattice / longitudinal relaxation): Time for Mz to recover to thermal equilibrium M₀ along B₀. Governed by energy exchange between spins and the surrounding lattice (molecular motions). Typically 10 ms – 10 s in liquids.
T₂ (spin-spin / transverse relaxation): Time for Mxy to decay to zero. Caused by dephasing: individual spins precess at slightly different frequencies due to local field inhomogeneities and spin–spin interactions. Always T₂ ≤ T₁. Determines the NMR linewidth: FWHM = 1/(πT₂).
RF Pulse Sequence — The NMR Experiment

In an NMR experiment, the equilibrium magnetization M₀ (along z, parallel to B₀) is tipped into the transverse plane by a resonant radiofrequency (RF) pulse, then allowed to precess and relax.

  • System at thermal equilibrium: Mz = M₀, Mxy = 0. Boltzmann population difference: excess spin-↑ by factor exp(ΔE/kT) ≈ 1 + γℏB₀/kT.
  • Apply resonant B₁ RF pulse at ω₀ along x̂. In the rotating frame, M tips away from z by flip angle θ = γB₁τ_p. A π/2 pulse tips M into the xy-plane (maximum signal). A π pulse inverts M to −z.
  • After the pulse: Mxy precesses at ω₀ and decays with time constant T₂*. This oscillating transverse magnetization induces a voltage in the receiver coil — the Free Induction Decay (FID).
  • Fourier Transform of FID → NMR spectrum. Each chemically distinct nucleus resonates at a slightly different frequency (chemical shift δ in ppm), encoding molecular structure.
FID signal:
S(t) = M₀ · e^(−t/T₂*) · cos(ω₀t + φ)

Chemical shift: δ = (ν − ν_ref)/ν₀ × 10⁶ ppm

Spectrum: S(ω) = FT[S(t)]
ESR — Electron Spin Resonance

Electron Spin Resonance (ESR), also called EPR, probes unpaired electrons using the same resonance principle as NMR. The electron has spin S = 1/2 and a g-factor close to 2.

Resonance condition:
hν = g·μB·B₀

g ≈ 2.002319 (free electron)
μB = eℏ/2mₑ = 9.274×10⁻²⁴ J/T

At B₀ = 0.34 T: ν ≈ 9.5 GHz (X-band)
At B₀ = 3.4 T: ν ≈ 95 GHz (W-band)

The electron gyromagnetic ratio γₑ = 1.761×10¹¹ rad/(s·T) is ~658 times larger than γ(¹H), pushing ESR into the microwave GHz range vs. NMR's MHz radio frequencies. The g-factor deviation from 2.002319 encodes spin-orbit coupling and the local chemical environment.

ESR vs NMR contrast: ESR is far more sensitive (electron spin magnetic moment ≫ nuclear) but requires unpaired electrons — only radicals, biradicals, transition metal complexes, and defect centers are ESR-active. NMR applies universally to any nucleus with I ≠ 0.

Hydrogen Atom — Exact Solution

The hydrogen atom (one electron, one proton) is the only atom with an exact analytical solution to the Schrödinger equation. In spherical coordinates, the wavefunction separates as ψ(r,θ,φ) = R(r)·Y(θ,φ).

Eₙ = −13.6 eV / n² (n = 1, 2, 3, ...)

rₙ = n²·a₀ (most probable radius)
a₀ = 0.529 Å (Bohr radius)

ψₙₗₘ(r,θ,φ) = Rₙₗ(r) · Yₗᵐ(θ,φ)

Four quantum numbers label each state:

Degeneracy: The n-th shell has n² spatial states (and 2n² with spin). All ℓ values within a shell are degenerate in hydrogen — this accidental degeneracy is broken by relativistic corrections (fine structure) and electron-electron repulsion in multi-electron atoms.
Fine Structure & Spin-Orbit Coupling

Relativistic corrections and the interaction between the electron's spin and its orbital angular momentum split the n-degenerate levels into a fine structure. The total angular momentum quantum number J = ℓ + S ranges from |ℓ−S| to ℓ+S.

E_SO = (1/2) · α_FS² · Ry · [J(J+1) − ℓ(ℓ+1) − S(S+1)] / n³

α_FS = e²/(4πε₀ℏc) ≈ 1/137 (fine structure const.)
Ry = 13.6 eV (Rydberg energy)

The sodium D-line doublet (589.0 nm / 589.6 nm) is the most famous manifestation: the 3p level splits into ²P₁/₂ and ²P₃/₂ states separated by 0.6 nm due to spin-orbit coupling. Both transitions to the ²S₁/₂ ground state are electric-dipole allowed.

Spectroscopic notation: States are labeled ²S⁺¹L_J — e.g. ²P₃/₂ means S=1/2 (2S+1=2), L=1 (P), J=3/2. The ground state of hydrogen is ¹S₁/₂: S=1/2, L=0, J=1/2.
Selection Rules — Derivation from Symmetry

Selection rules are not arbitrary restrictions — they follow from the requirement that the transition matrix element is nonzero:

Transition rate: Γᵢ→f ∝ |⟨f|Ĥ_int|i⟩|²

For electric dipole (E1) transitions, the interaction Hamiltonian is Ĥ_E1 = −e·r·E (dipole moment times field). The matrix element ⟨nℓm|r|n'ℓ'm'⟩ is nonzero only when:

Parity selection rule (from r being an odd-parity operator):
Δℓ = ±1 (parity must change: (−1)^ℓ → (−1)^(ℓ±1))
Magnetic quantum number (from angular momentum algebra):
Δmₗ = 0, ±1
Spin (since E1 doesn't act on spin):
ΔS = 0, ΔmS = 0
Total angular momentum (triangle rule for J, photon spin=1):
ΔJ = 0, ±1 (but J=0 → J=0 forbidden)

Rabi Oscillations — Coherent Excitation

When a two-level system (ground |g⟩, excited |e⟩) is driven by a resonant coherent field, the population oscillates sinusoidally between the two states — a quantum mechanical analogue of a classical coupled oscillator.

State: |ψ(t)⟩ = c_g(t)|g⟩ + c_e(t)|e⟩

Rabi frequency: Ω_R = μ_ge·E₀/ℏ
(dipole matrix element × field amplitude)

Population of excited state:
Pₑ(t) = (Ω_R/Ω̃)² · sin²(Ω̃·t/2)
Ω̃ = √(Ω_R² + Δ²) (generalized Rabi freq.)
Δ = ω − ω₀ (detuning)

At exact resonance (Δ = 0): Pₑ oscillates between 0 and 1 at frequency Ω_R. Off-resonance (Δ ≠ 0): oscillations are faster but never reach Pₑ = 1 — incomplete inversion. This is fundamental to laser pulse shaping, quantum gates, and atomic interferometry.

π-pulse and π/2-pulse: A pulse of duration τ_π = π/Ω_R inverts the population (|g⟩ → |e⟩) — used in NMR inversion recovery and quantum NOT gates. A π/2-pulse creates an equal superposition (1/√2)(|g⟩ + |e⟩) — the starting point of Ramsey spectroscopy and atomic clocks.

IV. CLASSICAL RESONANCE PHYSICS

Equation of Motion — Derivation

A mass m on a spring (stiffness k) subject to viscous damping (coefficient b) and a periodic driving force F(t) = F₀cos(ωt) obeys Newton's second law:

Newton's second law:
mẍ = −kx − bẋ + F₀cos(ωt)
Divide by m, define ω₀²=k/m and γ=b/2m:
ẍ + 2γẋ + ω₀²x = (F₀/m)cos(ωt)
Steady-state (particular) solution — assume x=A·cos(ωt−φ):
A(ω) = (F₀/m) / √[(ω₀²−ω²)² + 4γ²ω²]

Phase lag: tan(φ) = 2γω/(ω₀²−ω²)
Resonance peak at ω_res = √(ω₀² − 2γ²):
A_max = F₀/(m·2γ·√(ω₀²−γ²)) ≈ F₀/(2mγω₀) (low damping)
Low-damping limit (γ ≪ ω₀): The peak amplitude scales as A_max ∝ Q/ω₀² where Q = ω₀/2γ. A wine glass with Q ≈ 1000 amplifies the resonant driving force by a factor of Q — a singer at 100 dB effectively delivers the equivalent of 130 dB acoustic energy to the glass rim.
Q-Factor — A Universal Measure of Resonance Quality

The quality factor Q quantifies how "sharp" a resonance is. High Q means narrow bandwidth, slow energy loss, and large peak amplitude.

Q = ω₀ / 2γ = ω₀ / Δω_FWHM

Δω = bandwidth (FWHM of A²(ω))

Energy stored per cycle:
Q = 2π · (Energy stored) / (Energy lost per cycle)

Decay time: τ = 1/γ = 2Q/ω₀

Three Dynamical Regimes

The transient (homogeneous) solution x_h(t) determines the system's approach to steady state, and depends critically on the ratio γ/ω₀:

Underdamped γ < ω₀
x_h = Ae^(−γt)cos(ω_d·t+φ)
ω_d = √(ω₀²−γ²)

Oscillates with decaying envelope. Springs back repeatedly past equilibrium.

Critically damped γ = ω₀
x_h = (A+Bt)e^(−γt)

Fastest return to equilibrium without oscillation. Ideal for control systems.

Overdamped (γ > ω₀): x_h = Ae^(−r₁t) + Be^(−r₂t) where r₁,₂ = γ ± √(γ²−ω₀²). Returns slowly without oscillation. Two distinct exponential time constants. Example: door closer mechanisms.
Runge-Kutta 4 — Numerical Integration

The driven damped oscillator ODE is converted to a first-order system and integrated numerically by the RK4 method. The system state is the vector y = [x, v]:

f([x,v], t) = [v, F₀cos(ωt)/m − ω₀²x − 2γv]

k₁ = f(yₙ, tₙ)
k₂ = f(yₙ + h·k₁/2, tₙ + h/2)
k₃ = f(yₙ + h·k₂/2, tₙ + h/2)
k₄ = f(yₙ + h·k₃, tₙ + h)

yₙ₊₁ = yₙ + (h/6)(k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄)

RK4 is a 4th-order method: the local truncation error per step is O(h⁵), and the global error is O(h⁴). With step size h = 0.01 s, the position error after 1000 cycles is typically <10⁻⁷ — effectively indistinguishable from the exact solution on a plot.

Why not Euler? Euler's method (yₙ₊₁ = yₙ + h·f(yₙ)) has global error O(h). For a harmonic oscillator with h = 0.1, Euler slowly injects energy into the system, causing amplitude growth — a numerical artifact. RK4 is symplectic-adjacent and conserves amplitude to very high accuracy even over many cycles.

Einstein A & B Coefficients

Albert Einstein (1917) showed that three fundamental processes govern the interaction between atoms and radiation:

  • Spontaneous emission (A₂₁): An excited atom spontaneously emits a photon with rate A₂₁ (s⁻¹). The excited-state lifetime τ = 1/A₂₁. For allowed transitions, A₂₁ ~ 10⁸ s⁻¹ (lifetime ~10 ns).
  • Stimulated absorption (B₁₂): A ground-state atom absorbs a photon from the radiation field with rate B₁₂·ρ(ω), where ρ(ω) is the radiation spectral energy density.
  • Stimulated emission (B₂₁): An excited atom is triggered by a passing photon to emit an identical copy — same frequency, direction, phase. This is the process exploited in lasers (Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation).
Relations (from thermal equilibrium):
B₁₂ = B₂₁ = B (for non-degenerate levels)
A₂₁ = ℏω³/(π²c³) · B₂₁

Gain condition (population inversion):
N₂/g₂ > N₁/g₁ (more in upper than lower)
Population inversion: In thermal equilibrium, N₂ < N₁ always (Boltzmann distribution). Laser action requires a non-equilibrium state where N₂ > N₁, achieved by "pumping" energy into the system via optical, electrical, or chemical means. This is why lasers require an external energy source — they are amplifiers, not energy sources.
Spectral Line Broadening — Three Mechanisms

Spectral lines are never infinitely sharp. Three distinct broadening mechanisms operate simultaneously:

1. Natural broadening (Lorentzian profile — homogeneous):
L(ω) = (Γ/2π) / [(ω−ω₀)² + (Γ/2)²]
Γ = 1/τ = A₂₁ (natural linewidth)
2. Doppler broadening (Gaussian profile — inhomogeneous):
G(ω) = (1/σ√2π) · exp[−(ω−ω₀)²/2σ²]
σ_D = (ω₀/c)√(2kBT/m) (thermal velocity distribution)
3. Pressure broadening (Lorentzian, collision-induced):
Γ_pressure = n·σ_coll·v̄ (collision rate)
4. Combined profile (Voigt = convolution):
V(ω) = (L * G)(ω) = ∫L(ω')G(ω−ω')dω'

At room temperature and atmospheric pressure, Doppler broadening typically dominates for light atoms (H, He), while pressure broadening dominates in dense plasmas. Natural broadening is the absolute minimum linewidth, corresponding to the Fourier limit of the excited-state lifetime.

Laser Cavity Resonance — Standing Wave Modes

A laser cavity consists of two mirrors separated by length L. Light bouncing between the mirrors forms standing waves — only specific wavelengths (cavity modes) are resonant.

Resonance condition (integer number of half-wavelengths):
L = q · λ_q / 2, q = 1, 2, 3, ...
Mode frequencies:
ν_q = q · c / 2nL (n = refractive index of medium)
Free Spectral Range (spacing between adjacent modes):
FSR = Δν = c / 2nL

Example: L = 30 cm → FSR = 500 MHz
Example: L = 1 μm (VCSEL) → FSR = 150 THz
Cavity finesse (analogous to Q-factor):
F = π√R / (1−R) (R = mirror reflectivity)
Linewidth δν = FSR / F
Mode-locking: If all cavity modes are forced to oscillate with fixed phase relationships, constructive interference concentrates all the energy into ultrashort pulses. A 1 nm bandwidth at 800 nm produces pulses of duration τ ≈ 1/Δν ~ 10 fs (femtoseconds) — among the shortest human-made events.
Beer-Lambert Law & Absorption Spectroscopy

When light passes through an absorbing medium of length L with N absorbing atoms per unit volume, the transmitted intensity follows the Beer-Lambert law:

I(ω, L) = I₀ · exp[−α(ω) · L]

α(ω) = N · σ(ω) (absorption coefficient)

σ(ω) = σ₀ · L(ω−ω₀) (cross-section × lineshape)

Absorbance: A = log₁₀(I₀/I) = α·L / ln(10)

The frequency-dependent absorption cross section σ(ω) is proportional to the lineshape function L(ω−ω₀). Measuring the transmitted spectrum I(ω)/I₀ directly reveals the lineshape and integrated cross-section — the basis of all absorption spectroscopy from UV-Vis to infrared.

Saturable absorption: At high intensities, the absorption saturates when stimulated emission matches absorption (N₂ ≈ N₁). The effective cross-section decreases as σ_eff = σ₀/(1 + I/I_sat) where I_sat = ℏω/(2σ₀τ). Saturable absorbers are used as passive Q-switches and modelockers in pulsed lasers.
Rydberg Formula & Hydrogen Spectrum

Johann Balmer empirically noticed in 1885 that visible hydrogen lines fit a simple formula. Johannes Rydberg generalized this to all series in 1888. Niels Bohr derived it from first principles in 1913.

1/λ = R_∞ · (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²)

R_∞ = mₑe⁴/(8ε₀²h³c) = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹

Balmer series (n₁=2): 656.3, 486.1, 434.0, 410.2 nm
Lyman series (n₁=1): 121.6, 102.6, 97.2, ... nm

The full quantum-mechanical derivation gives R_∞ in terms of fundamental constants — one of the earliest triumphs of wave mechanics. The Rydberg constant is now known to 11 significant figures and is used to determine other fundamental constants.
