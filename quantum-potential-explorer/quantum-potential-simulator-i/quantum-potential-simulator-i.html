Theory: motion in a central potential

This section covers the physics and numerical methods behind the simulator: the radial Schrödinger equation, the effective potential, classical turning points, bound states, the WKB approximation and quantum tunnelling. The Try it buttons load the matching preset.

1. The radial Schrödinger equation

For a particle of mass \(m\) in a central potential \(V(r)\), the wavefunction separates as \(\psi(\mathbf r)=\frac{u(r)}{r}\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)\). The reduced radial function \(u(r)\) satisfies a one-dimensional equation:

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{d^2u}{dr^2}+V_{\text{eff}}(r)\,u=E\,u,\qquad V_{\text{eff}}(r)=V(r)+\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2},\] \[u(0)=0,\qquad \int_0^\infty|u(r)|^2\,dr=1 .\]
The centrifugal term is the quantum version of the classical \(L^2/2mr^2\). It pushes particles with \(\ell>0\) away from the origin, so their wavefunctions start as \(u\propto r^{\ell+1}\). \(|u(r)|^2dr\) is the probability of finding the particle between \(r\) and \(r+dr\).

Only the combination \(\hbar^2/2m\) enters the equation, which is why the simulator asks for it instead of the mass. Useful values:

2. Classical turning points

A classical particle with energy \(E\) can only be where its kinetic energy is positive, \(E>V_{\text{eff}}(r)\). The boundaries \(V_{\text{eff}}(r_t)=E\) are the turning points, where the radial velocity vanishes.

  • Allowed region (\(E>V\)): the quantum wavefunction oscillates, with local wavenumber \(k(r)=\sqrt{(E-V)/(\hbar^2/2m)}\).
  • Forbidden region (\(E<V\)): \(u\) decays or grows exponentially with \(\kappa(r)=\sqrt{(V-E)/(\hbar^2/2m)}\). It is not zero there; the simulator reports the probability of finding the particle in the forbidden region.
  • Two turning points enclosing an allowed region give a bound (periodic) classical orbit. A forbidden region enclosed by two turning points is a barrier.

3. Bound states: how the simulator solves the equation

The interval \([0,R_{\text{box}}]\) is divided into \(M\) points with spacing \(h=R_{\text{box}}/(M+1)\), with \(u=0\) at both ends. The second derivative becomes a three-point difference, so the equation turns into a symmetric tridiagonal matrix eigenvalue problem:

\[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{u_{i+1}-2u_i+u_{i-1}}{h^2}+V_{\text{eff}}(r_i)\,u_i=E\,u_i .\]
Sturm sequence bisection: the number of eigenvalues below any trial energy \(x\) equals the number of negative pivots in the \(LDL^{\mathsf T}\) factorisation of \(H-x\). Bisection on \(x\) finds each level \(E_{n_r}\) reliably, without missing states. The eigenvectors then come from inverse iteration.
Richardson extrapolation: the discretisation error is \(O(h^2)\). Solving again with \(h/2\) and combining as \(E\approx(4E_{h/2}-E_h)/3\) cancels the leading error, leaving \(O(h^4)\). For hydrogen with 2000 points the result agrees with \(-13.606/n^2\) eV to about 6 digits.

A state counts as bound if \(E<V_{\text{eff}}(R_{\text{box}})\). A state marked ⚠ still has appreciable amplitude at the box wall; increase \(R_{\text{box}}\) until its energy stops changing. Weakly bound states, such as the deuteron or Rydberg states, need large boxes.

Node theorem: the \(n_r\)-th state (counting from 0) has exactly \(n_r\) radial nodes. In atomic notation \(n=n_r+\ell+1\); in nuclear notation the label is \((n_r+1)\,\ell_j\), e.g. 1s₁/₂, 1p₃/₂.

4. The WKB approximation and the Langer correction

When the potential varies slowly on the scale of the wavelength, the phase accumulated between the turning points \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) quantises the energy:

\[\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\sqrt{\frac{E-V(r)-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{(\ell+\frac12)^2}{r^2}}{\hbar^2/2m}}\;dr=\Big(n_r+\tfrac12\Big)\pi .\]
Langer replaced \(\ell(\ell+1)\to(\ell+\frac12)^2\) to correct the behaviour near \(r=0\). With this correction, WKB gives the exact spectrum for the Coulomb potential and the 3D harmonic oscillator. Compare the “WKB” and “Exact” columns. For hard walls (infinite well) the ½ should be changed, so WKB is less accurate there.

5. Quantum tunnelling and α decay

A particle can cross a forbidden barrier between turning points \(r_a\) and \(r_b\). In the WKB approximation the transmission probability is

\[T\approx e^{-G},\qquad G=2\int_{r_a}^{r_b}\kappa(r)\,dr=2\int_{r_a}^{r_b}\sqrt{\frac{V(r)-E}{\hbar^2/2m}}\;dr\quad\text{(Gamow factor)}.\]

Gamow's theory of α decay (1928): an α particle with energy \(Q\) moves inside the nucleus at speed \(v\), hitting the barrier \(\nu=v/2R\) times per second. Each time it escapes with probability \(T\), so

\[\lambda_{\text{decay}}=\nu\,e^{-G},\qquad t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{\nu\,e^{-G}} .\]
Because \(G\) depends on \(Q^{-1/2}\), a few MeV change in \(Q\) changes the half-life by more than 20 orders of magnitude (the Geiger–Nuttall law). For ²¹²Po (\(Q=8.954\) MeV), the simulator gives \(G\approx32\) and \(t_{1/2}\sim10^{-8}\)–\(10^{-7}\) s, compared with the measured 0.30 μs: good agreement for such a simple model.

6. Model physics highlights

Hydrogen-like atoms

\(V=-Zke/r\) gives \(E_n=-Z^2\,\mathrm{Ry}/n^2\) with \(\mathrm{Ry}=(ke)^2/4(\hbar^2/2m)=13.606\) eV. Levels with the same \(n=n_r+\ell+1\) but different ℓ are degenerate: an “accidental” degeneracy caused by the hidden SO(4) symmetry (the Runge–Lenz vector). Any screening (Debye, Yukawa, Hulthén) lifts it.

The nuclear shell model

A nucleon moves in the mean field of all the others, well described by the Woods–Saxon potential with \(R=1.25A^{1/3}\) fm and \(a\approx0.65\) fm. The spin–orbit term

\[V_{LS}(r)=V_{so}\,\lambda_\pi^2\,\frac{1}{r}\frac{df}{dr}\,\langle\vec\sigma\cdot\mathbf L\rangle,\qquad \langle\vec\sigma\cdot\mathbf L\rangle=\begin{cases}\ell&j=\ell+\frac12\\-(\ell+1)&j=\ell-\frac12\end{cases},\qquad \lambda_\pi^2\approx2.0\ \text{fm}^2,\]
with \(f=[1+e^{(r-R)/a}]^{-1}\), pushes the \(j=\ell+\frac12\) level down and the \(j=\ell-\frac12\) level up. Together with the oscillator-like ordering of levels, this produces the magic numbers 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126 (Mayer and Jensen, Nobel Prize 1963).

The deuteron

The only bound two-nucleon system has binding energy 2.224 MeV. With a square well of radius 2.1 fm, a depth of 33.7 MeV reproduces it. Because the state is so weakly bound, the wavefunction extends far outside the well (\(1/\kappa\approx4.3\) fm), and most of the probability lies in the classically forbidden region.

Molecular vibrations

For the Morse potential the 1D levels are exactly \(E_v=-D_e+\hbar\omega(v+\frac12)-\frac{[\hbar\omega(v+\frac12)]^2}{4D_e}\), with \(\hbar\omega=2\alpha\sqrt{D_e\,\hbar^2/2\mu}\). For H₂ this gives \(\hbar\omega=0.545\) eV (4396 cm⁻¹). The anharmonic term makes the levels crowd together towards dissociation.

Critical binding

A short-range attractive potential in 3D does not always bind. The Yukawa potential \(-V_0e^{-\alpha r}/r\) has a bound state only if \(V_0/(\alpha\,\hbar^2/2m)>1.680\). Lower \(V_0\) in the preset and watch the last bound state approach \(E=0\), its wavefunction spreading out before it disappears.

7. Exercises

1. Show that for the infinite spherical well with ℓ = 0, \(E_n=n^2\pi^2(\hbar^2/2m)/a^2\). Evaluate it for a nucleon with \(a=4\) fm and check with the solver.

Answer

With \(V=0\) inside, \(u=\sin(kr)\) satisfies \(u(0)=0\); \(u(a)=0\) requires \(ka=n\pi\), so \(E=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}k^2=n^2\pi^2\frac{\hbar^2/2m}{a^2}\). For \(a=4\) fm: \(E_1=\pi^2\cdot20.74/16=12.79\) MeV, \(E_2=51.2\) MeV.

2. For the 3D harmonic oscillator with ħω = 10 MeV, list the energies of the states with \(2n_r+\ell=2\). How many states (counting \(m\) and spin) does this shell hold?

Answer

\(E=\hbar\omega(2n_r+\ell+\frac32)=35\) MeV for (n_r, ℓ) = (1, 0) [2s] and (0, 2) [1d]. Degeneracy: \(2(1)+2(5)=12\) states. Cumulative shell closures 2, 8, 20, … are the oscillator magic numbers.

3. Check the virial theorem for hydrogen: what are \(\langle T\rangle\) and \(\langle V\rangle\) in the ground state?

Answer

For \(V\propto r^{-1}\), \(2\langle T\rangle=-\langle V\rangle\), so \(\langle T\rangle=-E=13.6\) eV and \(\langle V\rangle=2E=-27.2\) eV. The selected-state panel shows both.

4. By how much does the ²¹²Po half-life change if \(Q\) falls from 8.95 to 6.0 MeV, keeping everything else fixed?

Answer

Set E = 6.0 MeV in the α-decay preset. G rises from about 32 to about 50, so \(T\) falls by \(e^{-18}\approx10^{-8}\): the half-life grows by roughly 8 orders of magnitude. This is the Geiger–Nuttall law in action.

5. Why is the probability of finding the deuteron's nucleons outside the potential range so large?

Answer

The binding energy (2.22 MeV) is tiny compared with the well depth (about 34 MeV), so \(\kappa=\sqrt{B/(\hbar^2/2\mu)}=0.23\) fm⁻¹ is small and the exponential tail \(e^{-\kappa r}\) extends to about 4 fm, twice the well radius. The simulator gives roughly 60–65 % of the probability in the forbidden region.

User guide

Quick start

  1. Choose a preset, or switch potentials on and off in the list. Click ▸ to open a potential's parameters and the ● swatch to change its colour.
  2. Set the units, the particle (which fixes ℏ²/2m) and the orbital angular momentum ℓ.
  3. Move the energy E with the slider or by dragging the dashed line in the plot. Turning points, allowed and forbidden regions, and the tunnelling probability update immediately.
  4. The bound-state table lists all levels below the asymptote, with exact and WKB values for comparison. Click a row or a level in the plot to see its wavefunction and expectation values.
  5. Export the plot as PNG, or the data (r, every V term, Veff) as CSV.

Accuracy tips

  • Weakly bound or high-lying states need a large Rbox. A ⚠ in the table marks states that touch the wall.
  • Strongly singular or very narrow potentials, such as Coulomb with large Z or molecular wells, benefit from more grid points.
  • Potentials with sharp steps (square wells, infinite wall) are averaged over each grid cell, which keeps the error O(h²) so Richardson extrapolation still works.
