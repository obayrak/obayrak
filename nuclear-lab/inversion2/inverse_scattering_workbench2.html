1 · Workflow: from experiment to potential

Elastic scattering measures \(|f(\theta)|^2\). From it we want the complex optical potential \(U(r)\). The forward map \(U\mapsto d\sigma/d\Omega\) is smooth and well posed. Its inverse is not: the data fix the potential only near the nuclear surface, and only certain combinations of parameters are determined. All curves on this page are computed live in your browser with the solver described below.

Key takeaways: workflow
  • The loop Model → Forward → χ² → Update is the computational core. Every iteration costs \(N_p+1\) forward solutions for the Jacobian.
  • The output is the potential together with its covariance, sensitivity range and family. A single "best" parameter set is not the result.
  • Including more physics, such as σR, wide-angle data, several energies (dispersion relations) or bound and quasi-bound states, shrinks the valley and removes discrete families.

Key takeaways: EXFOR data
  • Check the frame before fitting. Tables given in the laboratory frame are converted here to c.m. with \(\theta_{\rm c.m.}=\theta_{\rm lab}+\arcsin(\gamma\sin\theta_{\rm lab})\), \(\gamma=m_p/m_t\), and \(d\Omega_{\rm lab}/d\Omega_{\rm c.m.}=|1+\gamma\cos\theta_{\rm c.m.}|/(1+\gamma^2+2\gamma\cos\theta_{\rm c.m.})^{3/2}\). This is unique only for \(m_p\lt m_t\).
  • Many tables lack uncertainties, or quote only statistical ones. An assumed Δσ is labelled and changes the absolute \(\chi^2\) scale, not the location of the minimum.
  • Absolute normalisation errors (often 5–10 %) are not in Δσ. They couple to the depth of \(\operatorname{Im}U\) and to \(\sigma_R\), so check them against the reference in the EXFOR entry.
  • For p, d, t and ³He the spin–orbit term matters. The spinless model here gives only a spin-averaged description and cannot use analysing powers.

3 · The direct problem

Radial Schrödinger equation

$$\left[\frac{d^2}{dr^2}+k^2-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}-\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\,U(r)\right]u_\ell(r)=0,\qquad k^2=\frac{2\mu E_{\rm c.m.}}{\hbar^2}$$

Optical potential

$$U(r)=V_C(r)-V f(r;R_V,a_V)-iW f(r;R_W,a_W)-4iW_D\, f_D(1-f_D)\;\big[+\,U_{so}\big]$$ $$f(r;R,a)=\Big[1+e^{(r-R)/a}\Big]^{-1},\qquad R=r_0A^{1/3}$$

Form-factor library (FRESCO conventions, as in the Gamow-state solver; selectable in M1). In general \(U_N=-(V+iW)\,f(r)+(V_s+iW_D)\,g(r;R_D,a_D)\): the real and imaginary volume terms share the form \(f\), with radii \(R_V,R_W\). The form \(g\) carries a real surface strength \(V_s\) and the absorptive \(W_D\). With \(E=e^{(r-R)/a}\) and \(\rho=(r-R)/a\) (for Yukawa and exponential, \(E=e^{-r/a}\)):

$$f:\ \ \frac{1}{1+E},\ \ \frac{1}{(1+E)^2},\ \ e^{-\rho^2},\ \ \frac{e^{-r/a}}{r/a},\ \ e^{-r/a},\ \ \theta(R-r)\qquad g:\ \ -\frac{4E}{(1+E)^2},\ \ -\frac{8E^2}{(1+E)^3},\ \ -2\rho\,e^{-\rho^2},\ \ -\frac{E(1+r/a)}{r^2},\ \ -E$$

In order: Woods–Saxon, WS², Gaussian, Yukawa, exponential, square well; then their surface (derivative) forms. A tabulated \(U(r)\), e.g. a folding potential, can replace the volume term with normalisations \(N_R,N_I\). For WS, \(g=-4f(1-f)=4a\,df/dr\), so \(W_Dg\) is the surface absorption written above.

Surface absorption: \(-4iW_D f_D(1-f_D)=+4i\,a_DW_D\,\tfrac{d}{dr}f(r;R_D,a_D)\). The sign makes \(\operatorname{Im}U\le 0\), i.e. absorptive. Written with \(-i\,4a_DW_D\,df/dr\), the term would emit flux because \(df/dr\lt 0\). The spin–orbit term \(U_{so}=\big(\tfrac{\hbar}{m_\pi c}\big)^2\tfrac{V_{so}}{r}\tfrac{d}{dr}f(r;R_{so},a_{so})\,2\boldsymbol{\ell}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{s}\) vanishes for the spin-0 projectiles used here (α, ¹²C, ¹⁶O) and is not included. The Coulomb potential is that of a uniform sphere of radius \(R_C=r_CA^{1/3}\); setting \(r_C=0\) gives a point charge.

$$V_C(r)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{Z_pZ_te^2}{2R_C}\Big(3-\dfrac{r^2}{R_C^2}\Big), & r\lt R_C\\[2mm] \dfrac{Z_pZ_te^2}{r}, & r\ge R_C\end{cases}\qquad \eta=\frac{Z_pZ_te^2\mu}{\hbar^2k}$$

Asymptotics and the S-matrix

$$u_\ell(r)\;\xrightarrow{\,r\ge R_N\,}\;\tfrac{i}{2}\big[H^-_\ell(\eta,kr)-S_\ell H^+_\ell(\eta,kr)\big],\quad H^\pm_\ell=G_\ell\pm iF_\ell$$ $$S_\ell=\eta_\ell\,e^{2i\delta_\ell},\qquad 0\le\eta_\ell\le1,\qquad \sigma_\ell=\arg\Gamma(\ell+1+i\eta)$$

Amplitude and cross sections

$$f(\theta)=f_C(\theta)+\frac{1}{2ik}\sum_{\ell}(2\ell+1)\,e^{2i\sigma_\ell}\,(S_\ell-1)\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)$$ $$f_C(\theta)=-\frac{\eta}{2k\sin^2(\theta/2)}\exp\!\big[-i\eta\ln\sin^2(\theta/2)+2i\sigma_0\big]$$ $$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta)|^2,\qquad \frac{d\sigma_{\rm Ruth}}{d\Omega}=\frac{\eta^2}{4k^2\sin^4(\theta/2)},\qquad \sigma_R=\frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_\ell(2\ell+1)\big(1-|S_\ell|^2\big)$$

\(\eta_\ell\) (reflection coefficient) and \(\eta\) (Sommerfeld parameter) are different quantities. \(T_\ell=1-|S_\ell|^2\) is the transmission coefficient.

Numerical method (what the browser actually does)

Numerov integration of \(u''=Q(r)u\), with \(Q=\ell(\ell+1)/r^2+\tfrac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}(U-E)\), on a uniform grid \(r_n=nh\):

$$\Big(1-\tfrac{h^2}{12}Q_{n+1}\Big)u_{n+1}=2\Big(1+\tfrac{5h^2}{12}Q_n\Big)u_n-\Big(1-\tfrac{h^2}{12}Q_{n-1}\Big)u_{n-1}$$

Each partial wave starts at the first grid point where \(h^2\ell(\ell+1)/(12r^2)\le 0.1\), with \(u\propto r^{\ell+1}(1+c_2r^2)\). This keeps the Numerov factor positive and avoids spurious nodes near the origin at high \(\ell\). For a point charge the start is at \(r\approx1\) fm from the exact Coulomb power series. For Yukawa forms (\(\propto1/r\) or \(1/r^2\) at the origin), \(q=\tfrac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}(U-E)\approx C/r^2+A/r+B\) is fitted near \(r=0\), and the start is at \(r\ge0.3\) fm with \(u\propto r^s(1+c_1r+c_2r^2)\), where \(s=\tfrac12+\sqrt{(\ell+\tfrac12)^2+C}\), \(c_1=A/2s\) and \(c_2=(Ac_1+B)/(4s+2)\). The step is set by \(k_{\rm loc}h\le0.05\) (local truncation error \(\propto h^6\), global error \(\propto h^4\)).

Matching. With two grid points \(r_a\lt r_b=r_{\max}\) outside the nuclear range,

$$S_\ell=\frac{u_\ell(r_b)H^-_\ell(r_a)-u_\ell(r_a)H^-_\ell(r_b)}{u_\ell(r_b)H^+_\ell(r_a)-u_\ell(r_a)H^+_\ell(r_b)}$$

\(r_{\max}=\max(R+15a,\;r_{\rm turn}+8/k,\;20\ \text{fm})\) satisfies the requirement \(r_{\max}\gt R+10a\). \(\ell_{\max}\) is increased until \(|S_\ell-1|\lt10^{-8}\) for three consecutive \(\ell\).

Coulomb functions by Steed's method (Barnett's COULFG algorithm). CF1 gives \(f_L=F'_L/F_L\) at a large \(L\), where \(\rho\) lies inside the turning point; \(F\) is then recurred downward to \(\ell=0\). CF2 gives \(p+iq=H^{+\prime}_0/H^+_0\):

$$f_L=S_{L+1}-\cfrac{R_{L+1}^2}{S_{L+1}+S_{L+2}-\cfrac{R_{L+2}^2}{S_{L+2}+S_{L+3}-\cdots}},\quad S_\ell=\frac{\ell}{\rho}+\frac{\eta}{\ell},\ R_\ell=\sqrt{1+\frac{\eta^2}{\ell^2}}$$ $$p+iq=i\Big(1-\frac{\eta}{\rho}\Big)+\frac{i}{\rho}\,\cfrac{a_1}{b_1+\cfrac{a_2}{b_2+\cdots}},\ \ a_n=(i\eta+n-1)(i\eta+n),\ b_n=2(\rho-\eta+in)$$

The Wronskian \(F'G-FG'=1\) then fixes \(F_0=\pm[q(1+\gamma^2)]^{-1/2}\) and \(G_0=\gamma F_0\), with \(\gamma=(f_0-p)/q\). \(G_\ell\) follows by upward recursion. Coulomb phases come from Stirling's series for \(\ln\Gamma(N+1+i\eta)\) followed by recursion. Kinematics are non-relativistic, with nuclear masses from AME2020 and \(\hbar c=197.3269804\) MeV fm, \(e^2=1.43996448\) MeV fm.

Key takeaways: direct problem
  • The forward map \(U(r)\to\{S_\ell\}\to d\sigma/d\Omega\) is well posed. A partial-wave sum up to \(\ell_{\max}\approx kR+\) a few, matched to exact Coulomb functions, is converged to \(10^{-8}\).
  • \(\sigma/\sigma_{\rm Ruth}\) separates nuclear effects from the Coulomb background: Fresnel or Fraunhofer oscillations in the diffractive regime, and an exponential fall-off past the nuclear rainbow in the refractive regime.
  • The absorptive part removes flux from low partial waves. As a result, \(\sigma_R\) and the angular distribution are governed mainly by the transition region \(\ell\approx\ell_{1/2}\).
  • Move \(V\) and \(r_V\) together, keeping \(V\,e^{R_V/a_V}\) fixed. For a strongly absorbed, Coulomb-dominated system the forward-angle cross section hardly changes. This is the Igo ambiguity, previewed here and quantified in §7.

4 · The inverse problem

Definition

Given measurements \(\{\theta_i,\sigma_i^{\rm exp}\pm\Delta\sigma_i\}_{i=1}^N\), and when available analysing powers \(A_y(\theta)\) (spin-½ projectiles) or \(\sigma_R\), find \(U(r)\) such that

$$\mathcal{A}[U](\theta_i)\equiv\Big|f_C(\theta_i)+\tfrac{1}{2ik}\textstyle\sum_\ell(2\ell+1)e^{2i\sigma_\ell}(S_\ell[U]-1)P_\ell(\cos\theta_i)\Big|^2\simeq\sigma_i^{\rm exp}$$

Why it is ill-posed (Hadamard)

  • Non-uniqueness of the amplitude. \(|f|^2\) fixes \(f\) only up to a phase. With a finite number of partial waves, discrete (Crichton-type) ambiguities remain even in the \(\{S_\ell\}\).
  • Non-uniqueness of the potential. At fixed energy, \(S_\ell\) is known only at integer \(\ell\). Many potentials, including phase-equivalent and transparent ones, give the same set. Fixed-energy inversion therefore needs extra conditions (Newton–Sabatier).
  • Instability. Linearising, \(\delta\sigma_i=\int K_i(r)\,\delta U(r)\,dr\). Strong absorption makes \(K_i(r)\) negligible for \(r\lesssim R_{1/2}-2a\). The interior lies in the near-null space, so small data errors give large interior changes.
  • Limited angular range. Forward angles probe only the far tail of \(U\). Refractive (rainbow) data at large angles are needed to reach deeper radii.

Linear response (used by the iterative-perturbative method)

For a small change \(\delta U\), with \(u_\ell\to\tfrac{i}{2}(H^-_\ell-S_\ell H^+_\ell)\):

$$\delta S_\ell=-\frac{2i}{k}\,\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty u_\ell(r)^2\,\delta U(r)\,dr$$

This follows from the Wronskian identity \(\big[u\,\delta u'-u'\delta u\big]_0^\infty=\int\delta Q\,u^2dr\) and \(W(H^-,H^+)=2ik\). The kernel \(u_\ell^2\) is small inside the strong-absorption radius, which is the quantitative statement of the sensitivity problem.

Regularised least squares

$$\chi^2(\mathbf{p})=\sum_i\left[\frac{\sigma^{\rm th}_i(\mathbf p)-\sigma^{\rm exp}_i}{\Delta\sigma_i}\right]^2+\lambda_{\rm reg}\,\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{p}\|^2$$

A Tikhonov term (\(\lambda_{\rm reg}\gt0\)) stabilises model-independent fits. The fits on this page use \(\lambda_{\rm reg}=0\) and show the resulting covariance directly.

Methods

(a) Phenomenological χ² fitting

$$\chi^2/N=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\left[\frac{\sigma_{\rm th}(\theta_i)-\sigma_{\rm exp}(\theta_i)}{\Delta\sigma_{\rm exp}(\theta_i)}\right]^2$$ $$\big(\mathbf J^{\sf T}\mathbf J+\lambda\,\mathrm{diag}\,\mathbf J^{\sf T}\mathbf J\big)\,\delta\mathbf p=-\mathbf J^{\sf T}\mathbf r,\quad J_{ij}=\frac{\partial r_i}{\partial p_j},\quad \mathbf C=(\mathbf J^{\sf T}\mathbf J)^{-1}$$

Levenberg–Marquardt interpolates between Gauss–Newton (small \(\lambda\)) and steepest descent (large \(\lambda\)). \(\lambda\) is decreased after a successful step and increased after a failed one. Parameter errors are \(\sqrt{C_{jj}}\) (\(\Delta\chi^2=1\)), scaled by \(\sqrt{\chi^2/\nu}\) when the fit is imperfect.

Pros

  • Few physically interpretable parameters
  • Fast, robust; global systematics
  • Connects to folding models via \(J_V/A\)

Cons

  • Shape bias from the Woods–Saxon form
  • Local minima and discrete families
  • Small parameter errors hide strong correlations

(b) Model-independent forms

$$U_{\rm FB}(r)=U_{\rm WS}(r)+\sum_{n=1}^{N_b}b_n\,j_0\!\Big(\frac{n\pi r}{R_c}\Big),\quad r\le R_c$$ $$U_{\rm SOG}(r)=\sum_i A_i\Big\{e^{-[(r-R_i)/\gamma]^2}+e^{-[(r+R_i)/\gamma]^2}\Big\}$$

The Fourier–Bessel (FB) form is used for α scattering (Friedman & Batty) and the sum of Gaussians (SOG) for charge densities (Sick). The error band follows from the covariance, \(\delta U(r)^2=\mathbf g^{\sf T}\mathbf C\,\mathbf g\) with \(g_j=\partial U(r)/\partial p_j\). The FB form is implemented in M3.

Pros

  • No shape prejudice
  • Honest error band: shows where the data constrain \(U\)

Cons

  • Needs precise wide-angle (refractive) data
  • Many correlated parameters; requires regularisation
  • Band diverges in the interior

(c) Fixed-energy inversion: \(S_\ell\to U(r)\)

Two steps: first a phase-shift (S-matrix) analysis of \(\sigma(\theta)\), then inversion. Newton–Sabatier (units with \(q=\tfrac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}U\), \(\hat u_\ell(x)=x\,j_\ell(x)\)):

$$\psi_\ell(r)=\hat u_\ell(kr)-\int_0^r K(r,t)\,\hat u_\ell(kt)\,\frac{dt}{t^2},\qquad K(r,t)=\sum_\ell c_\ell\,\psi_\ell(r)\,\hat u_\ell(kt)$$ $$q(r)=-\frac{2}{r}\,\frac{d}{dr}\Big[\frac{K(r,r)}{r}\Big]$$

The \(c_\ell\) follow from \(\delta_\ell\) through the asymptotics of \(\psi_\ell\). Lipperheide–Fiedeldey uses the Langer variable \(\lambda=\ell+\tfrac12\) and a rational S-matrix relative to a reference (e.g. Coulomb):

$$S(\lambda)=S_{\rm ref}(\lambda)\prod_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\lambda^2-\beta_n^2}{\lambda^2-\alpha_n^2}$$

With this form the kernel is degenerate and \(U(r)\) follows algebraically from Riccati–Coulomb functions evaluated at \(\lambda=\alpha_n,\beta_n\). This works for complex potentials with Coulomb.

Pros

  • No assumed shape
  • Analytic (rational S) and fast

Cons

  • Needs \(S_\ell\) first; phase-shift analysis is itself ambiguous
  • Unique only under extra conditions
  • Sensitive to the high-\(\ell\) tail of the S-matrix

(d) Fixed-ℓ inversion (all energies)

Gel'fand–Levitan (spectral data) and Marchenko (S(k) and bound states), for \(\ell=0\), in units \(q=\tfrac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}U\):

$$K(r,t)+F(r,t)+\int_0^r K(r,s)F(s,t)\,ds=0,\qquad q(r)=2\frac{d}{dr}K(r,r)$$ $$A(r,t)+\Omega(r+t)+\int_r^\infty A(r,s)\,\Omega(s+t)\,ds=0,\qquad q(r)=-2\frac{d}{dr}A(r,r)$$ $$\Omega(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big[1-S(k)\big]e^{ikx}dk+\sum_n M_n^2e^{-\kappa_nx}$$

Pros

  • Rigorous and unique for local, real, energy-independent \(q\)
  • Standard for NN potentials

Cons

  • Needs \(S(k)\) at all energies; optical potentials are energy-dependent and complex
  • Rarely applicable directly to nucleus–nucleus data

(e) Semiclassical (WKB) and iterative-perturbative (IP) inversion

The WKB phase \(\delta(\lambda)\) and deflection function \(\Theta(\lambda)=2\,d\delta/d\lambda\) (with \(b=\lambda/k\)) invert for real \(U\) by Firsov's formula, with \(n(r)=[1-U(r)/E]^{1/2}\) and \(\rho=r\,n(r)\):

$$\ln n(\rho)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_\rho^\infty\frac{\Theta(b)\,db}{\sqrt{b^2-\rho^2}}\qquad(\Theta\gt0\ \text{attractive})$$

IP method (Cooper & Mackintosh): \(U^{(n+1)}=U^{(n)}+\sum_i a_i v_i(r)\), with the \(a_i\) from a linear least-squares solution of \(S_\ell^{\rm target}-S_\ell^{(n)}=\sum_i a_i\,\partial S_\ell/\partial a_i\), using the response formula above. Iterate to convergence. The method can be fitted directly to \(\sigma(\theta)\).

Pros

  • WKB: transparent link to rainbows and orbiting
  • IP: complex, Coulomb, spin; fast, stable with SVD

Cons

  • WKB fails near barriers and at low \(\ell\); needs monotonic \(\rho(r)\)
  • IP depends on basis and starting potential
Key takeaways: inverse problem
  • Inversion is ill-posed in Hadamard's sense: phase loss in \(|f|^2\), integer-\(\ell\) sampling, absorption that hides the interior, and finite angular range all contribute.
  • Phenomenological fitting is the most common method. Model-independent (FB/SOG) and inversion methods (Newton–Sabatier, Lipperheide–Fiedeldey, IP) remove shape bias but need more and better data, plus regularisation.
  • Whatever the method, quote what the data actually determine: \(U\) near \(R_{1/2}\), \(J_V/A\) and \(\langle r^2\rangle^{1/2}\), together with an error band.

6 · Inversion: data → Sℓ → U(r)

This page carries out fixed-energy inversion in two steps. First, M8 extracts the S-matrix from \(\sigma(\theta)\) without assuming any potential. Then M9 recovers \(U(r)\) from \(\{S_\ell\}\) by the iterative-perturbative (IP) method. M10 uses the same linear response to map exactly which radii the cross section sees.

Step 1: model-independent S-matrix

McIntyre (smooth-cutoff) form, 6 parameters:

$$\eta_\ell=\varepsilon+\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1+e^{(L_\eta-\ell)/\Delta_\eta}},\qquad \operatorname{Re}\delta_\ell=\frac{\delta_0}{1+e^{(\ell-L_\delta)/\Delta_\delta}}$$

Spline form: natural cubic splines through knots \(\ell_k=0,\Delta\ell,2\Delta\ell,\dots\), with \(\eta_\ell=[1+e^{-s(\ell)}]^{-1}\) (so that \(0\lt\eta_\ell\lt1\)) and \(\operatorname{Re}\delta_\ell=d(\ell)\). The last knot is fixed at \(S=1\). A smoothness penalty \(\lambda_s\sum_k[(y_{k-1}-2y_k+y_{k+1})^2]\) is added to \(\chi^2\).

$$S_\ell=\eta_\ell e^{2i\delta_\ell}\ \Rightarrow\ f(\theta)\ \Rightarrow\ \chi^2=\sum_i\Big[\frac{\sigma(\theta_i;\{S_\ell\})-\sigma_i}{\Delta\sigma_i}\Big]^2$$

Step 2: iterative-perturbative inversion

$$U^{(n+1)}(r)=U^{(n)}(r)+\sum_{i=1}^{N_b}(a_i+ib_i)\,g_i(r),\qquad g_i(r)=e^{-[(r-c_i)/w]^2}$$ $$A_{\ell i}=-\frac{2i}{k}\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty u_\ell^{(n)}(r)^2g_i(r)\,dr,\qquad u_\ell\to\tfrac{i}{2}(H^-_\ell-S_\ell H^+_\ell)$$ $$\min_{a,b}\ \sum_\ell w_\ell^2\Big|S^{\rm t}_\ell-S^{(n)}_\ell-\sum_iA_{\ell i}(a_i+ib_i)\Big|^2+\lambda\sum_i(a_i^2+b_i^2)$$

Three targets are available. (i) The S-matrix of a known potential, with optional noise: a test of the method. (ii) The S-matrix extracted in M8 (two-step route). Here the misfit uses the data metric \(\mathbf W=\mathbf J_S^{\sf T}\mathbf J_S+\tau^{-2}\mathbf 1\), with \((\mathbf J_S)_{i\ell}=\Delta\sigma_i^{-1}\partial\sigma_i/\partial S_\ell\) at the extracted solution. (iii) The cross-section data directly. Here Gauss–Newton is applied to \(\chi^2\) with \(\partial\sigma_i/\partial a_j=\sum_\ell(\partial\sigma_i/\partial S_\ell)A_{\ell j}\), recomputed at every iteration. Steps that increase the misfit are halved.

$$\frac{\partial\sigma(\theta)}{\partial U(r)}=2\operatorname{Re}\Big[f^*(\theta)\,\frac{1}{2ik}\sum_\ell(2\ell+1)e^{2i\sigma_\ell}K_\ell(r)P_\ell(\cos\theta)\Big],\quad K_\ell(r)=-\frac{2i}{k}\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}u_\ell(r)^2$$ $$\frac{\partial\sigma(\theta)}{\partial\ln U(r)}=U(r)\,\frac{\partial\sigma(\theta)}{\partial U(r)},\qquad F(r)=\Big\{\sum_i\Big[\frac{1}{\Delta\sigma_i}\frac{\partial\sigma(\theta_i)}{\partial\ln U(r)}\Big]^2\Big\}^{1/2}$$

The response is taken to a relative change \(\delta U=U(r)\,\delta\ln U\), the continuous version of the notch test. The absolute kernel \(\partial\sigma/\partial U\) grows at large \(r\) only because \(U(r)\to0\) there. Left: \(\partial\ln\sigma(\theta)/\partial\ln U(r)\) per fm. The kernels are the exact first-order (Born-like, distorted-wave) response of the full optical-model solution, not a semiclassical estimate.

Key takeaways: inversion
  • An S-matrix fitted to \(\sigma(\theta)\) without a potential reproduces the data at least as well as a Woods–Saxon fit. It is well determined only in the transition region \(\ell\approx\ell_{1/2}\); the bands widen for \(\ell\ll\ell_{1/2}\), where \(|S_\ell|\approx0\).
  • IP inversion of an exact S-matrix (α + ⁴⁰Ca, 104 MeV) converges to \(|S^{\rm t}-S|\sim10^{-4}\). It recovers \(U(r)\) to about 0.03 MeV outside \(R_{1/2}-1\) fm and \(J_V/4A\) exactly, but the interior only to a few MeV. With noise 0.01 on \(S_\ell\) the surface error grows to several MeV; a larger \(\lambda\) trades misfit for stability. From a distant start the method can stall in another family: it is a local method.
  • The two-step route is fragile for refractive data. The back-angle amplitude is a cancellation of partial waves by about three orders of magnitude, so for the spline S-matrix of M8 (\(\chi^2/N\approx1.6\)) random errors of \(10^{-3}\) in all \(S_\ell\) already raise \(\chi^2/N\) to about 13, and errors of \(10^{-2}\) to about \(10^5\). A potential that matches the extracted \(S_\ell\) to \(10^{-2}\) can therefore still fit the data badly. Fitting \(\sigma(\theta)\) directly with the same IP machinery avoids this, but needs \(\lambda\) to prevent over-fitting and can end in emissive (\(\operatorname{Im}U\gt0\)) regions, which the page flags.
  • The exact relative kernels \(\partial\sigma/\partial\ln U(r)\) define a sensitivity shell. For α + ⁴⁰Ca at 104 MeV it peaks near 5 fm, inside \(R_{1/2}\approx6.6\) fm, because refractive trajectories penetrate; the finite notch of page 8 peaks near 6 fm.

7 · Ambiguities

Continuous (Igo) ambiguity

If the data probe only the tail, where \(f\simeq e^{-(r-R)/a}\), then

$$V f(r;R,a)\simeq V\,e^{R/a}\,e^{-r/a}\quad\Rightarrow\quad V\,e^{R_V/a_V}\approx\text{const}$$

Along this curve in the \((V,r_V)\) plane, \(\chi^2\) is nearly flat: a long narrow valley. Its length measures how far inside the surface the data reach.

Discrete ambiguities and the robust quantities

Potentials of different depth can give the same \(S_\ell\) in the transition region if their low-\(\ell\) internal phases differ by multiples of \(\pi\). The extra half-wavelength inside is invisible under strong absorption. The resulting families have discretely spaced volume integrals and are separated only by refractive (nuclear rainbow) data, or by physics input such as bound or quasi-bound states (Pauli-forbidden state count). Within a family the robust quantities are

$$\frac{J_V}{A_pA_t}=\frac{4\pi}{A_pA_t}\int_0^\infty V(r)\,r^2dr,\qquad \langle r^2\rangle_V^{1/2}=\left[\frac{\int V r^4dr}{\int V r^2dr}\right]^{1/2},\qquad V(R_s)$$

Key takeaways: ambiguities
  • The χ² valley follows \(V e^{R_V/a_V}\approx\) const. Its length shrinks when large-angle (refractive) data are included.
  • Equivalent potentials cross near one radius \(R_s\) (close to \(R_{1/2}\)), where \(V(R_s)\) is determined to a few percent while \(V(0)\) can vary by a factor of two or more.
  • Discrete families differ in \(J_V/A\) by large steps. With only forward-angle data several depths fit; refractive (wide-angle) data, or physics constraints such as the folding-model \(J_V\), dispersion relations and bound states, select among them.

8 · Sensitivity: where do the data look?

Strong-absorption radius

$$|S_{\ell_{1/2}}|=\tfrac12,\qquad kR_{1/2}=\eta+\sqrt{\eta^2+(\ell_{1/2}+\tfrac12)^2}$$

\(R_{1/2}\) is the distance of closest approach on the Coulomb orbit with \(\lambda=\ell_{1/2}+\tfrac12\). Elastic data are mostly sensitive within about \(\pm 2a\) of \(R_{1/2}\), and deeper only if the absorption is weak enough for refractive (rainbow) trajectories to penetrate.

Notch test (Cramer & DeVries)

$$U(r)\to U(r)\Big[1-d\,e^{-[(r-r_n)/w]^2}\Big],\qquad \Delta\chi^2(r_n)=\chi^2_{\rm notch}(r_n)-\chi^2_0$$

A localised notch is moved through the potential. Where \(\Delta\chi^2\) stays near zero the data do not see \(U\). This profile is a direct, model-dependent map of the radial sensitivity.

  • The notch response peaks near \(R_{1/2}\), close to the crossing radius \(R_s\) of the equivalent potentials in M6.
  • For a strongly absorbed, Coulomb-dominated system such as α + ²⁰⁸Pb near the barrier, the sensitive region is a narrow shell far outside the nuclear interior. At 104 MeV on ⁴⁰Ca the window extends further in.

Key takeaways: validation
  • With \(W=W_D=0\) the S-matrix is unitary to machine precision, \(|S_\ell|=1\). With \(V=W=W_D=0\) and a point charge, \(S_\ell=1\) and \(\sigma/\sigma_{\rm Ruth}=1\) to the \(O(h^4)\) Numerov accuracy.
