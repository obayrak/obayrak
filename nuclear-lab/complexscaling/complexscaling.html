1. The radial problem

Two particles with mass numbers \(A_p, A_t\) interact through a central potential, optionally with a spin-orbit term. After separating the centre-of-mass motion and the angular part, each partial wave \(\ell\) obeys the radial equation for \(u_\ell(r) = r\,R_\ell(r)\):

\[ \left[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{d^2}{dr^2} + \frac{\hbar^2\,\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^2} + V_{\ell j}(r) \right] u(r) = E\,u(r), \qquad u(0) = 0. \]

The reduced mass is \(\mu = \dfrac{A_pA_t}{A_p+A_t}\,m_u\). The code works with a single kinetic constant

\[ \kappa \equiv \frac{\hbar^2}{\mu} = \frac{(\hbar c)^2}{\mu c^2}\ \ [\mathrm{MeV\,fm^2}], \qquad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{d^2}{dr^2} = -\frac{\kappa}{2}\frac{d^2}{dr^2}. \]

Energies are then in MeV and lengths in fm, with no further conversion. In dimensionless mode \(\kappa = 1\) (\(\hbar = \mu = 1\)). For n + ⁴He, \(\mu = 745.2\) MeV and \(\kappa = 52.25\) MeV fm².

With channel spin \(s\), the orbital and spin angular momenta couple to \(\vec j = \vec\ell + \vec s\), with \(|\ell - s| \le j \le \ell + s\). The spin-orbit operator is diagonal in this basis:

\[ \langle \sigma\cdot\ell \rangle = 2\langle \ell\cdot s\rangle = j(j+1) - \ell(\ell+1) - s(s+1). \]
s = ½j = ℓ + ½j = ℓ − ½
⟨σ·ℓ⟩+ℓ−(ℓ + 1)

Each \((\ell, j)\) pair is solved as an independent channel. The State box selects the orbital \(\ell\), and with spin both \(j = \ell \pm s\) channels are solved. The node number \(n\) labels the state as in the Gamow-states page: for a bound state it is the number of nodes of \(u\) on \(0 < r < \infty\); for a resonance it is the number of nodes of \(\mathrm{Re}\,u\) (with the c-normalised phase of section 9) between the origin and the inner turning point \(R_I\) at \(E_r\), or the barrier top if \(E_r\) lies above it. For narrow states this equals the node count of the bound state the pole is connected to. For very broad states it is only a guide, and two states can share the same \(n\). Deep cluster potentials contain Pauli-forbidden states, so \(n\) should then follow the Wildermuth rule \(2n + \ell = G\). Without a barrier (the inverted oscillator) there is no inner turning point, and \(n\) is the order of the poles by width, which reproduces the exact labels of \(E_n = V_0 - i\hbar\omega(2n + \ell + \tfrac32)\).

2. Resonances as poles of the S-matrix

Outside the range of the nuclear force, the solution is a combination of incoming and outgoing waves:

\[ u_\ell(r) \;\to\; H^{-}_\ell(kr) - S_\ell(k)\,H^{+}_\ell(kr), \qquad H^{\pm}_\ell \sim e^{\pm i(kr - \ell\pi/2)} . \]

A resonance (Gamow state) is a pole of \(S_\ell(k)\) in the lower half of the complex \(k\)-plane, where only the outgoing wave survives. Writing the pole as \(k = |k|\,e^{-i\varphi}\) gives

\[ E = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2\mu} = \frac{\kappa |k|^2}{2}\,e^{-2i\varphi} \equiv E_r - \tfrac{i}{2}\Gamma, \qquad |\arg E| = 2\varphi, \qquad \tau = \frac{\hbar}{\Gamma}. \]

Here \(E_r\) is the resonance energy, \(\Gamma\) the width, and \(\tau\) the mean lifetime. The difficulty is that the outgoing wave grows exponentially:

\[ e^{ikr} = e^{\,i|k|r\cos\varphi}\; e^{\,+|k|r\sin\varphi} . \]

A Gamow state is therefore not normalisable and cannot be found by diagonalising an ordinary Hermitian Hamiltonian in a box.

3. Complex scaling

The dilation \(U(\theta)\colon r \to r\,e^{i\theta}\) is applied to every coordinate. The Gamow function then becomes

\[ e^{ik\,r e^{i\theta}} = e^{\,i|k|r\cos(\theta-\varphi)}\; e^{\,-|k|r\sin(\theta-\varphi)} , \]

which decays like a bound state whenever \(\theta > \varphi\), that is when

\[ 2\theta > |\arg E| . \]

The rotated Hamiltonian is

\[ H(\theta) = e^{-2i\theta}\left[-\frac{\kappa}{2}\frac{d^2}{dr^2} + \frac{\kappa\,\ell(\ell+1)}{2r^2}\right] + V_{\ell j}\!\left(r e^{i\theta}\right). \]

It is no longer Hermitian, but it is complex symmetric: \(H^{\mathsf T} = H\). The Aguilar-Balslev-Combes theorem describes its spectrum for dilation-analytic potentials:

  • Bound states stay on the negative real axis and do not depend on θ.
  • Resonances with \(|\arg E| < 2\theta\) appear as isolated eigenvalues \(E_r - i\Gamma/2\), also independent of θ.
  • The continuum rotates onto the ray \(\arg E = -2\theta\) starting at the threshold \(E = 0\). Its eigenvalues move when θ changes.
The whole method rests on this contrast: physical poles are θ-invariant, while the discretised continuum moves. The code tests every eigenvalue for exactly that property.

4. Which potentials can be rotated

\(V(z)\) must be analytic in the sector \(0 \le \arg z \le \theta\) and must not blow up along the ray \(z = re^{i\theta}\). This limits θ for each potential type:

  • Woods-Saxon, \(f(z) = 1/(1 + e^{(z-R)/a})\), has poles at \(z = R + i\pi a(2n+1)\). The ray must pass below the first one, which requires \(\tan\theta < \pi a / R\). The sidebar shows this limit. The surface term \(-4V_s e^x/(1+e^x)^2\) and the spin-orbit term \(V_{so}\lambda_\pi^2\,\frac{1}{z}\frac{df_{so}}{dz}\langle\sigma\cdot\ell\rangle\) have the same poles.
  • Gaussian: \(e^{-\alpha z^2} = e^{-\alpha r^2\cos 2\theta}\,e^{-i\alpha r^2\sin 2\theta}\) stays bounded only for \(\theta < 45^\circ\).
  • Coulomb: the uniformly charged sphere is piecewise, \(\frac{Z_pZ_te^2}{2R_C}\left(3 - \frac{z^2}{R_C^2}\right)\) inside and \(\frac{Z_pZ_te^2}{z}\) outside. At complex \(z\) the two pieces no longer meet at \(r = R_C\), so the rotated potential is discontinuous. The code instead uses the field of a Gaussian charge cloud, which is an entire function and has the same rms radius when \(b = R_C\sqrt{2/5}\): \[ V_C(z) = Z_pZ_te^2\,\frac{\operatorname{erf}(z/b)}{z} . \]
  • Inverted oscillator: \(V_0 - Cz^2\). With \(C = \tfrac12\mu\omega^2\) the poles are known exactly, \(E_n = V_0 - i\hbar\omega\,(2n + \ell + \tfrac32)\), which makes it a strict test of the numerics.

Combining terms. The sidebar builds the potential from independent terms, in the FRESCO conventions of the Gamow-states page:

\[ V(r) = V_C(r) + \frac{\hbar^2 l(l+1)}{2\mu r^2} - V_0 f(r) + V_s\,g(r) + V_{so}\lambda_\pi^2\,h(r)\,\langle\sigma\cdot\ell\rangle + i\bigl[-W_v f_w(r) + W_D\,g_D(r)\bigr]. \]

Volume shapes \(f\) are Woods–Saxon \(1/(1+E)\) with \(E = e^{(r-R)/a}\), its square, a Gaussian \(e^{-((r-R)/a)^2}\), Yukawa \(e^{-r/a}/(r/a)\) and exponential \(e^{-r/a}\). Surface shapes \(g\) are, in the same order, \(-4E/(1+E)^2\), \(-8E^2/(1+E)^3\), \(-2\rho e^{-\rho^2}\) with \(\rho = (r-R)/a\), \(-E(1+r/a)/r^2\) and \(-e^{-r/a}\). Spin–orbit shapes \(h\) are \(-E/((1+E)^2ar) = r^{-1}df/dr\), \(-E^2/(2ar(1+E)^3)\), \(-\rho e^{-\rho^2}/(2ar)\), \(-E(1+r/a)/(4ar^3)\) and \(-E/(4ar)\). Every shape is analytic in the right half-plane, apart from the Woods–Saxon poles at \(R + i\pi a(2n+1)\) and the Yukawa pole at \(r = 0\), so all of them can be complex scaled. The θ limit for uniform scaling is the smallest \(\arctan(\pi a/R)\) over the Woods–Saxon-type terms that are switched on. With absorption the Hamiltonian is non-Hermitian even at θ = 0; it stays complex symmetric, so every method and the c-product still apply, and every state acquires \(\mathrm{Im}\,E < 0\).

Tabulated potentials are interpolated on the real axis (natural cubic spline or linear) and set to zero beyond the last point. A table cannot be continued to complex \(r\), so the uniform methods (finite differences, sinc-DVR) cannot use it. The exterior-scaling methods only need the potential on the real axis inside \(R_m\), and \(R_m\) is placed at or beyond the last table point, so they can. The interior and exterior solutions are matched further in, at the nuclear surface (where the table has fallen to half its maximum). The exterior solution is carried from \(R_m\) inward on the real axis by Numerov, after one RK4 step from the ray. A matching point deep in the forbidden region would make the Wronskian a near step function of \(E\) and a bound-state wave function unstable. The normalisation and \(\langle r^2\rangle\) include this real-axis segment. In \(V_{\rm eff}\) mode the centrifugal term of the table’s \(l\) is removed point by point, and the Coulomb potential is taken from the table inside its range and as \(Z_pZ_te^2/r\) beyond it.

Point Coulomb and Yukawa terms behave like \(1/r\) at the origin. The Numerov start of section 13 includes the first-order term \(\Sigma = \lim rK V\), and the GAMOW power series uses \(T_2 = W_2h\) with \(W_2 = K\lim_{r\to0} rV\).

5. Discretisation

The radial axis is cut at \(r_{\max}\) and sampled on a uniform grid, with the boundary conditions \(u_0 = u_{N+1} = 0\):

\[ r_n = n h, \qquad n = 1,\dots,N, \qquad h = \frac{r_{\max}}{N+1}. \]

The second derivative uses the three-point formula, whose leading error is

\[ u''(r_n) = \frac{u_{n+1} - 2u_n + u_{n-1}}{h^2} - \frac{h^2}{12}\,u^{(4)}(r_n) + O(h^4). \]

This turns \(H(\theta)\) into an \(N \times N\) complex-symmetric tridiagonal matrix:

\[ H_{nn} = \kappa\,e^{-2i\theta}\left[\frac{1}{h^2} + \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2r_n^2}\right] + V_{\ell j}\!\left(r_n e^{i\theta}\right), \qquad H_{n,n\pm1} = -\frac{\kappa}{2}\,\frac{e^{-2i\theta}}{h^2}. \]

Its eigenvalues have an error expansion in even powers of the step,

\[ E(h) = E + c_1h^2 + c_2h^4 + c_3h^6 + \dots \]

The coefficients \(c_k\) are complex because the kinetic term carries \(e^{-2i\theta}\). On a coarse grid a bound state therefore picks up a spurious imaginary part that changes with θ. This was the main accuracy problem of the original code, and section 8 removes it.

The box must be large enough for the rotated wave function to have decayed. From section 3 the envelope at the box edge is

\[ |u(r_{\max})| \sim \exp\!\left[-|k|\,r_{\max}\,\sin\!\left(\theta - \tfrac12|\arg E|\right)\right]. \]

Narrow resonances below a Coulomb barrier need a large \(r_{\max}\): their wave function has to tunnel out past the outer turning point \(r \approx Z_pZ_te^2/E\), which is 21 fm for p + ¹²C at 0.42 MeV.

Section 12 describes the alternative sinc-DVR discretisation, which can be selected under Method in the sidebar.

6. All eigenvalues at once

The complete spectrum of the tridiagonal matrix is computed with the implicit QL algorithm. Each sweep chooses a shift from the trailing 2 × 2 block and chases the bulge with plane rotations. For a complex-symmetric matrix the rotations must satisfy

\[ c^2 + s^2 = 1 \qquad (\text{not } |c|^2 + |s|^2 = 1), \]

so they are complex orthogonal rather than unitary. This keeps the matrix symmetric, uses no complex conjugates, and costs \(O(N^2)\) per spectrum. The spectrum is computed twice, at θ and at θ + Δθ. These are the filled and open grey points in the complex energy plane.

7. Picking out the physical states

For each eigenvalue \(E\) in the first spectrum, the code finds its nearest neighbour \(E'\) in the second spectrum and keeps \(E\) as a candidate if

\[ \frac{\min_{E'} |E - E'|}{\max(|E|,\,E_{\text{floor}})} < \varepsilon \qquad (\varepsilon = \text{“θ-filter tolerance”}). \]

A continuum eigenvalue lies on the rotating ray and moves by roughly \(2\,\Delta\theta\,|E|\). For Δθ = 4° its relative shift is about 0.14, far above the default ε = 0.01, so continuum points are rejected. The floor \(E_{\text{floor}} = 10^{-3}\) prevents a division by zero for points near the threshold.

8. Refinement

Inverse iteration. Each candidate eigenvalue is used as a shift σ, and the code solves

\[ (H - \sigma)\,w = v, \qquad v \leftarrow w/\|w\| \]

with the Thomas algorithm in \(O(N)\) operations. The eigenvalue estimate is the bilinear Rayleigh quotient

\[ \rho(v) = \frac{v^{\mathsf T} H\, v}{v^{\mathsf T} v} \qquad (\text{transpose, no conjugate}). \]

For a complex-symmetric matrix this quotient is stationary at eigenvectors, so its error is quadratic in the eigenvector error. Once ρ has settled, it replaces σ as the shift (Rayleigh-quotient iteration, cubic convergence). A guard rejects the result if it jumps away from the starting value, which would mean it had converged to a different eigenvalue.

Richardson extrapolation. The same box is solved with \(N\), \(2N+1\) and \(4N+3\) points, which gives step sizes of exactly \(h\), \(h/2\) and \(h/4\). Eliminating the \(h^2\) and \(h^4\) terms of the error expansion gives

\[ R_1 = \frac{4E(h/2) - E(h)}{3}, \qquad R_2 = \frac{4E(h/4) - E(h/2)}{3}, \qquad E = \frac{16R_2 - R_1}{15} + O(h^6). \]

The quantity \(|E - R_2|\) is reported as the accuracy. The refined value is then computed again at θ + Δθ, and the relative change is reported as the θ-shift.

Classification. A state is bound if \(\operatorname{Re}E < 0\) and \(|\operatorname{Im}E|\) is within the numerical error. Otherwise it is a resonance with

\[ E_r = \operatorname{Re}E, \qquad \Gamma = -2\,\operatorname{Im}E . \]

9. Normalised wave functions

For a complex-symmetric Hamiltonian the left eigenvector is the transpose of the right one, not its complex conjugate. The natural inner product is therefore the bilinear c-product

\[ (f, g) = \int f(r)\,g(r)\,dr \qquad (\text{no complex conjugate}). \]

A Gamow state grows exponentially on the real axis, so \(\int_0^\infty u^2dr\) diverges. Its norm is defined by analytic continuation (Zel’dovich regularisation), and the integral is evaluated on a contour where the integrand decays. This page uses the method of the Gamow-states page for every solver: the energy \(E\) comes from the selected method, and the wave function and its norm are then built in seven steps.

Step 1: interior. Numerov outward on the real axis, \(r_j = jh\), from \(u_0 = 0\), \(u_1 = h^{\ell+1}\bigl[1 + \tfrac{\Sigma h}{2(\ell+1)}\bigr]\) up to the matching radius \(R_m\), at the complex energy \(E\). Here \(\Sigma = \lim_{r\to0} rK V(r)\) is non-zero only for \(1/r\) terms such as the spin-orbit potential.

Step 2: exterior on the rotated ray. With \(r(s) = R_m + s\,e^{i\theta}\) the radial equation becomes \(d^2u/ds^2 = e^{2i\theta} f\bigl(r(s)\bigr)\,u\), and the outgoing (or decaying) solution is the one that decays along the ray. Numerov runs inward from \(s = S\), with \(u(S) = 0\), \(u(S - h_e) = 1\). The contour length S is the smallest value with \(\int_0^S \mathrm{Re}\sqrt{e^{2i\theta}f}\,ds \ge 20\).

Step 3: match. The exterior solution is scaled so that \(u_e(R_m) = u_i(R_m)\).

Step 4: c-norm along the contour (Simpson’s rule on both parts):

\[ \mathcal N = \int_0^{R_m} u^2(r)\,dr \;+\; e^{i\theta}\int_0^{S} u^2\bigl(R_m + s\,e^{i\theta}\bigr)\,ds, \qquad u \to u/\sqrt{\mathcal N}. \]

Step 5: sign. The c-norm fixes the phase up to a sign (a factor \(e^{i\alpha}\) would turn the norm into \(e^{2i\alpha}\)). The sign is chosen so that \(\mathrm{Re}\,u(h) > 0\).

Step 6: why it is exact. By Cauchy’s theorem \(\mathcal N\) does not depend on θ, and it equals the Zel’dovich regularised norm

\[ \mathcal N = \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^+}\int_0^\infty e^{-\varepsilon r^2}\,u^2(r)\,dr . \]

For a bound state it is the ordinary \(\int_0^\infty |u|^2dr\), because \(u\) is real.

Step 7: expectation values use the same contour, for example

\[ \langle r^2\rangle = \int_0^{R_m} u^2r^2\,dr + e^{i\theta}\int_0^S u^2(z)\,z^2\,ds, \qquad z = R_m + s\,e^{i\theta}. \]

They are complex for resonances. The real part is usually read as the value and the imaginary part as its uncertainty from the decay, which is meaningful when \(|\mathrm{Im}| \ll |\mathrm{Re}|\).

What is plotted. The physical \(u(r)\) on the real axis: the interior solution up to \(R_m\) (marked in the plot), then continued outward for a resonance (its tail grows, so outward Numerov is stable) or inward from far away for a bound state, starting where \(\int\kappa\,dr \ge 18\) beyond the turning point.

Why not the box eigenvector? Normalising the uniformly rotated eigenvector of the finite-difference or sinc matrix (\(h\sum_n v_n^2 = 1\)) gives the right norm only up to the grid error of the eigenvector, which is complex and θ-dependent. For the Gaussian benchmark at N = 600 it gave \(\langle r^2\rangle = 1.193986 - 6.6\times10^{-5}i\) for the bound state, whose exact value is real (1.1940640). For the narrow ℓ = 0 resonance it gave \(\mathrm{Im}\langle r^2\rangle = 1.85\times10^{-4}\) at θ = 20° and \(4.7\times10^{-5}\) at 28°. The contour norm gives \(6.647\times10^{-4}\) at 15°, 20° and 30° alike. The worked example shows this comparison for the selected state.

10. Reading the diagnostics

  • Accuracy \(|E - R_2|\): the residual grid error. A large value means \(N\) should be increased.
  • θ-shift: should be at the 10⁻⁶ level or below. A large θ-shift means the eigenvalue belongs to the continuum, the resonance is barely exposed (2θ close to |arg E|), or θ is near the potential's analyticity limit.
  • Grid sweep: the raw three-point values (open points) should converge onto the Richardson line. The gap shows how large the uncorrected \(O(h^2)\) error is.
  • Box sweep: drift with \(r_{\max}\) means the rotated wave function has not decayed inside the box. Increase \(r_{\max}\) or θ.

12. Alternative method: sinc-DVR

The sinc discrete variable representation (D. T. Colbert and W. H. Miller, J. Chem. Phys. 96, 1982 (1992)) replaces the three-point formula by a basis of shifted sinc functions on a uniform grid:

\[ \phi_n(r) = \frac{1}{\sqrt h}\,\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{r - r_n}{h}\right), \qquad \operatorname{sinc}x = \frac{\sin \pi x}{\pi x}. \]

Each basis function equals \(1/\sqrt h\) at its own grid point and vanishes at all others. The expansion coefficients are therefore function values, \(c_n = \sqrt h\,u(r_n)\), and the potential is diagonal, \(V_{nn'} = \delta_{nn'}V(r_ne^{i\theta})\). On an infinite grid the kinetic matrix is known exactly:

\[ \left(-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\right)_{nn'} = \frac{1}{h^2}\,T^\infty_{n-n'}, \qquad T^\infty_0 = \frac{\pi^2}{3}, \qquad T^\infty_d = \frac{2(-1)^d}{d^2}\ \ (d \ne 0). \]

The half-line. The radial function lives on \(r > 0\). It is extended to negative \(r\) by symmetry, and the mirror-image points are folded back onto the grid. The right symmetry depends on \(\ell\), because \(u \propto r^{\ell+1}\) near the origin: \(u\) is odd in \(r\) for even \(\ell\) and even in \(r\) for odd \(\ell\). The matching extension keeps the extended function smooth. The wrong one creates a kink, which makes the convergence algebraic instead of exponential. On the half-integer grid \(r_n = (n - \tfrac12)h\), \(h = r_{\max}/N\), the image of \(r_{n'}\) is \(-r_{n'} = r_{1-n'}\), which gives

\[ T_{nn'} = \frac{\kappa}{2h^2}\left[T^\infty_{n-n'} + \sigma_\ell\,T^\infty_{n+n'-1}\right], \qquad \sigma_\ell = \begin{cases} -1, & \ell \text{ even (odd extension)},\\ +1, & \ell \text{ odd (even extension)}, \end{cases} \] \[ H(\theta)_{nn'} = e^{-2i\theta}\left[T_{nn'} + \delta_{nn'}\,\frac{\kappa\,\ell(\ell+1)}{2r_n^2}\right] + \delta_{nn'}\,V_{\ell j}\!\left(r_ne^{i\theta}\right). \]

For the Gaussian test potential with \(\ell = 1\), the usual integer grid with an odd image for all \(\ell\) still has an error of \(10^{-3}\) at \(N = 200\). The parity-adapted grid reaches \(5\times10^{-7}\) at \(N = 120\) and machine precision at \(N = 200\).

Convergence. If the integrand is analytic in a strip of half-width \(d\) around the integration line, the sinc error falls like \(e^{-\pi d/h}\). Two things limit \(d\) here:

  • Singularities of the rotated potential. The Woods-Saxon poles at \(R + i\pi a(2n+1)\) lie at an angle \(\arctan(\pi a/R) - \theta\) from the ray. For n + \(^4\)He at θ = 38° this is only about 9°, which corresponds to \(d \approx 0.4\) fm.
  • Behaviour at the origin. The symmetric extension is perfectly smooth only if \(V\) is even in \(r\). The Gaussian, the oscillator and the erf-Coulomb potential are even; the Woods-Saxon form is not, since \(f'(0) \ne 0\).

In practice the Gaussian benchmark converges to \(10^{-14}\) at \(N \approx 200\), while the Woods-Saxon presets need \(N \approx 450\)–\(600\) for errors near \(10^{-6}\).

Eigenvalues. The matrix is dense and complex symmetric. All eigenvalues are found by Householder reduction to upper Hessenberg form, using reflections \(P = I - 2vv^\dagger\), followed by single-shift complex QR iteration. The shift is the eigenvalue of the trailing 2 × 2 block closer to the corner element, and a block is deflated when \(|H_{k,k-1}| < \varepsilon\,(|H_{kk}| + |H_{k-1,k-1}|)\). These transformations are unitary rather than complex orthogonal: symmetry is not needed for the eigenvalues, and unitary steps are numerically stable. The cost is about \(10N^3\) operations per spectrum.

Refinement and error estimate. Candidates are selected exactly as in section 7. Each one is refined by inverse iteration with an LU factorisation of \(H - \sigma\) (partial pivoting), and the eigenvalue is again the bilinear Rayleigh quotient. Richardson extrapolation does not apply, because the error has no power series in \(h\). Instead the accuracy is estimated as \(|E(N) - E(N')|\) with \(N' = 0.85\,N\) on the same box. With exponential convergence this is an upper bound, since the error at \(N'\) dominates.

Wave function. Because \(c_n = \sqrt h\,u(r_n)\), the normalisation of section 9 carries over unchanged with \(h' = h\) and the half-integer points: \(h\sum_n \tilde u(r_n)^2 = 1\).

13. Complex scaling + Numerov (exterior scaling, shooting)

Exterior complex scaling (B. Simon, Phys. Lett. A 71, 211 (1979)) leaves \(r\) real up to a radius \(R_m\) and rotates only beyond it, \(r = R_m + s\,e^{i\theta}\). \(R_m\) is placed beyond every Woods-Saxon radius, so the swept wedge \(\mathrm{Re}\,r \ge R_m\), \(0 \le \arg(r - R_m) \le \theta\) contains none of the poles at \(R + i\pi a(2n+1)\). Unlike uniform scaling, the arctan\((\pi a/R)\) limit on θ therefore does not apply. The interior solution is the physical one on the real axis.

Interior. Numerov outward to \(R_m\) at complex \(E\):

\[ \Bigl(1 - \tfrac{h^2}{12}f_{j+1}\Bigr)u_{j+1} = 2\Bigl(1 + \tfrac{5h^2}{12}f_j\Bigr)u_j - \Bigl(1 - \tfrac{h^2}{12}f_{j-1}\Bigr)u_{j-1}, \qquad f = \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2} + K(V - E), \quad K = \frac{2}{\kappa}. \]

In the first step the finite limit \((fu)(0)\) replaces the singular term: 2 for ℓ = 1, Σ for ℓ = 0.

Exterior. Numerov inward along the ray, as in section 9, steps 2–3.

Derivatives at \(R_m\) use a Numerov-consistent one-sided formula, accurate to \(O(h^6)\); the ray derivative is divided by \(e^{i\theta}\):

\[ u'_N = \frac{u_N - u_{N-1}}{h} + h\sum_{k=0}^{4} c_k f_{N-k}u_{N-k}, \qquad c = \Bigl(\tfrac{367}{1440}, \tfrac{3}{8}, -\tfrac{47}{240}, \tfrac{29}{360}, -\tfrac{7}{480}\Bigr). \]

Pole condition. A scaled Wronskian is used, which has no poles where \(u\) vanishes. It is solved by complex secant, with the steps limited to \(2 + |E|/2\):

\[ W(E) = \frac{u_i'(R_m)\,u_e(R_m) - u_e'(R_m)\,u_i(R_m)}{\lVert(u_i, u_i')\rVert\,\lVert(u_e, u_e')\rVert} = 0 . \]

Start energies without a guess. For a fixed contour length and fixed start values the unscaled Wronskian is an analytic function of \(E\), and the positive scaling factor does not change its phase. The number of poles inside a cell \(\mathcal C\) of the complex energy plane is therefore given by the argument principle,

\[ n(\mathcal C) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\partial\mathcal C} d\arg W(E). \]

The search window is covered by 90 × 24 cells. The phase increment along each edge is accumulated, and the edge is bisected whenever a step exceeds 0.8 rad. A cell with \(n = 1\) is then bisected into quarters five times, keeping the quarter that still winds, which locates the zero to 1/32 of a cell. Cells lying on the continuum ray \(\arg E = -2\theta\) are dropped, because their zeros belong to the discretised contour.

Coarse edge sampling can miss the very fast phase rotation next to a narrow pole close to the real axis. Before this was added, the ¹³N 1/2⁺ resonance (Γ = 38 keV) was found only by chance. The start energies are therefore the union of two independent sets:

  • The θ-stable eigenvalues of a small spectral matrix (section 14, N = 32), which needs no guess and resolves narrow poles.
  • The refined scan cells away from the continuum ray.

Each start energy is then converged with the Numerov secant on the fine contour, whose length is capped at \(\max(4S_{\rm scan}, 600)\) fm.

Validation and accuracy. The finite contour with \(u(S) = 0\) also has “box” solutions along \(\arg E \approx -2\theta\). Every converged root is solved again at θ + Δθ. A pole moves by less than \(\max(10^{-6}(1+|E|), 10\,\delta)\), where δ is its error estimate; box solutions move far more and are rejected. Each pole is solved with steps h and h/2 and extrapolated, \(E = (16E(h/2) - E(h))/15\), and \(|E - E(h/2)|\) is reported as the accuracy.

14. Spectral complex scaling (Chebyshev collocation)

The same exterior-scaled problem is discretised once and diagonalised, so every pole in the window appears as an eigenvalue without a start energy or continuation path. The contour is split into a real domain \([0, R_m]\) and two ray domains, \(s \in [0, s_1]\) and \([s_1, S]\) with \(s_1 = \min(12, S/3)\). On each domain \(u\) is represented on Chebyshev–Gauss–Lobatto points with the differentiation matrix \(D\) (L. N. Trefethen, Spectral Methods in MATLAB, SIAM 2000):

\[ x_j = \cos\frac{\pi j}{N}, \qquad \Bigl(-\frac{1}{K}\,e^{-2i\vartheta}D^2 + V + \frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{Kr^2}\Bigr)u_j = E\,u_j, \]

with \(\vartheta = 0\) on the real domain and \(\vartheta = \theta\) on the ray. The real domain uses \(\min(\max(N, 2.5k_{\max}R_m + 16), 120)\) points, and the far ray domain more points when it is long.

Boundary and interface conditions. \(u(0) = 0\), \(u(R_m + Se^{i\theta}) = 0\), and at each interface \(u\) is shared and \(u'\) is continuous, with \(du/dr = e^{-i\theta}du/ds\) on the ray. The interface rows contain no \(E\). Writing \(u_q\) for the interface values and \(u_p\) for the collocation values, they are eliminated exactly by a Schur complement:

\[ C_{qq}u_q + C_{qp}u_p = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; H = A_{pp} - A_{pq}\,C_{qq}^{-1}C_{qp}, \qquad H u_p = E\,u_p . \]

Eigenvalues and selection. All eigenvalues of \(H\) come from the Householder–Hessenberg + shifted complex QR routine of section 12. The matrix is rebuilt at θ + Δθ, and an eigenvalue is kept if it moves by less than \(10^{-5}(1+|E|)\). Each kept eigenvalue then starts the Numerov secant of section 13, which polishes it and gives the reported value with its Richardson error estimate. The worked example shows the raw spectral eigenvalue next to the polished one.

15. GAMOW 1982 (Vertse, Pál and Balogh)

A re-implementation of the program GAMOW (T. Vertse, K. F. Pál and Z. Balogh, Comput. Phys. Commun. 27, 309 (1982)), ported from the Gamow-states page. It iterates Newton corrections from a start energy and controls the node number.

Interior start. Near the origin \(u = \sum_i C_i r^{\ell+1+i}\). With \(a_i = C_ih^i\), \(T_1 = (W_1 - k^2)h^2\) and \(T_2 = W_2h\):

\[ a_{i+2} = \frac{T_2a_{i+1} + T_1a_i}{(i+2)(2\ell+i+3)}, \qquad a_0 = 1,\ \ a_1 = \frac{T_2}{2\ell+2}, \qquad u(m) = (mh)^{\ell+1}\sum_{i=0}^{4}a_im^i . \]

This gives the first few points; Fox–Goodwin (Numerov) integration then continues outward to \(R_m + 3h\).

Exterior. The nuclear potential is cut to zero beyond \(R_M\), the last radius where it exceeds \(10^{-7}\) of its maximum. On the steepest-descent line \(r = R_M + t\,e^{i\varphi}\), with \(\varphi = \pi/2 - \arg k\), the solution starts from the asymptotic Coulomb series

\[ O_\ell(z) \simeq e^{i(z - \eta\ln 2z)}\Bigl[1 + \sum_{n\ge1}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(i\eta + \ell + j)(i\eta - \ell + j - 1)}{j\,(2iz)}\Bigr], \qquad z = kr . \]

The series is evaluated at the far end of the line and integrated inward to \(R_M\). One RK4 step then supplies \(u(R_M - h)\), and Numerov continues inward on the real axis to \(R_m - 3h\).

Matching and correction. \(R_m\) is an even multiple of \(h\) just inside the inner turning point \(R_I\). Seven-point derivatives give the log-derivative mismatch \(L = L_i - L_e\). With \(u\) Zel’dovich-normalised along the real axis and the line, \(\partial L/\partial E = -K/u^2(R_m)\), so the Newton correction is

\[ \Delta E = \frac{L\,u^2(R_m)}{K} . \]

If the node count differs from the requested \(n\), a fixed step \(\pm D_1/K\) is taken instead.

Here. The start energies come from section 13 (spectral eigenvalues and refined scan cells). The node number \(n\) of the first iteration then becomes the requested node number. Each pole is recomputed with \(h/2\) (Richardson) and with \(1.2R_M\). The reported accuracy is the larger of the two changes.

Known weakness. Between \(R_M\) and \(R_m\) the outgoing solution is integrated inward on the real axis. For a broad resonance this amplifies the incoming component by about \(e^{2|\mathrm{Im}\,k|(R_M - R_m)}\). For the Gaussian ℓ = 1 state at 2.0175 − 0.4863i, \(R_M = 20\) gives an error of \(1\times10^{-3}\), and moving \(R_M\) changes the result visibly. The error column reports this. GAMOW also needs an asymptotic region where \(V_N \to 0\), so it cannot treat the inverted oscillator.
