The theory curve is a corrected Bethe–Bloch calculation (relativistic Bethe logarithm with effective-charge, Barkas polarization, generic Sternheimer–Peierls density-effect, Barkas–Berger shell-correction and exact Bloch-correction terms — toggle each above) built directly from the target's parsed elemental composition (Bragg additivity for Z/A and mean excitation energy I) and, when a reference density is on file, the target density. The Barkas term is implemented in the stopping number as \(+z_{\rm eff}L_1(\beta)\), using the compact first-order approximation \(L_1(\beta)\approx\pi\alpha/(2\beta)\); consequently its stopping-power contribution scales approximately as \(z_{\rm eff}^{\,3}/\beta^3\). An optional ZBL universal nuclear-stopping term (off by default) can be added on top of the electronic curve above — computed per element and combined by Bragg (mass-fraction) additivity, since nuclear stopping cannot be reduced to a single averaged target Z the way the electronic terms are. The shell correction uses the Barkas–Berger fit where it is valid (βγ ≳ 0.36); below that the fit itself diverges (it is built from negative powers of η²), so by default it is instead continued as a boundary-matched power law, C(η²) = C(0.13)·(η²/0.13)ⁿ — a smooth, adjustable placeholder rather than a literature result — with exponent n and the extrapolation itself both switchable in the sidebar. Reference · Corrected Bethe–Bloch

Stopping-power theory and physical corrections

Stopping power describes the rate at which a charged projectile loses kinetic energy while traversing matter. The Bethe–Bloch framework describes the dominant electronic (collision) stopping of sufficiently fast charged particles. At lower energies, shell, effective-charge, Barkas and Bloch effects become increasingly important, while nuclear stopping must be treated separately.

01 Linear & mass stopping power

The linear stopping power is the energy lost per unit path length. Dividing by the absorber density gives the mass stopping power, which is particularly useful when comparing different materials.

$$ \boxed{ S=-\frac{dE}{dx}, \qquad \frac{S}{\rho} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dE}{dx} } $$

Here, \(S\) is the linear stopping power and \(S/\rho\) is the mass stopping power obtained by normalizing to the absorber density. Dividing out \(\rho\) removes the trivial density dependence, which is why mass stopping power is the quantity most often tabulated and compared across materials.

Term Meaning Physical interpretation Why it matters
\(S\) Linear stopping power Energy lost by the projectile per unit path length traveled in the absorber. Directly sets the projectile's local energy-deposition rate as a function of position.
\(E\) Projectile kinetic energy The energy being removed, collision by collision, as the particle slows down in the medium. The quantity whose loss with distance defines the stopping power in the first place.
\(x\) Path length Distance traveled by the projectile through the material. The independent variable of the energy-loss derivative \(dE/dx\).
\(\rho\) Absorber mass density Mass per unit volume of the target material. Normalizing by \(\rho\) yields a mass stopping power that is far less material-specific, so tabulated data compare directly across absorbers.
Analysis Condition / result Physical explanation Practical implication
Extreme Large S A steep energy-loss rate means the projectile deposits most of its remaining energy over a very short distance. Short residual range. Rapid energy loss corresponds to a short remaining path length in the material.
Pinpoint Bragg region Stopping power rises sharply as the projectile decelerates near the end of its range, producing the characteristic Bragg peak. This region is the most important feature for range calculations and localized dose deposition.
Suggestion Always identify the mechanism A large low-energy stopping value can originate from either electronic or nuclear stopping. Do not interpret it as purely electronic stopping without separately checking the nuclear contribution.

02 Energy per nucleon

For ions with different masses, total kinetic energy is not a direct measure of projectile velocity. For non-relativistic ions the velocity is primarily determined by energy per nucleon.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{E}{A}\;[\mathrm{MeV/u}], \qquad v\propto\sqrt{\frac{E}{A}} } $$

Here, \(E/A\) is the projectile kinetic energy per nucleon, and the proportionality shows that, non-relativistically, velocity depends only on \(E/A\) and not on the total mass of the ion. This is why \(E/A\) — rather than total kinetic energy \(E\) — is used as the common energy coordinate throughout the explorer.

Term Meaning Physical interpretation Why it matters
\(A\) Projectile mass number The number of nucleons making up the projectile ion. Divides out the trivial mass scaling of total kinetic energy so that ions of different mass can be compared at equal velocity.
\(E/A\) Energy per nucleon Kinetic energy normalized to the number of nucleons, conventionally expressed in MeV/u. The common energy coordinate used throughout the explorer for plotting and comparing different projectiles.
\(v\) Projectile velocity Non-relativistically, \(v\propto\sqrt{E/A}\): velocity is set by energy per nucleon alone, independent of \(A\). Stopping power depends fundamentally on velocity, so \(E/A\) is a more physically meaningful axis than total energy.
Analysis Condition / result Physical explanation Practical implication
Extreme Low E/A The projectile velocity becomes small, and corrections to the asymptotic Bethe theory become increasingly important. Shell, effective-charge, Barkas and Bloch corrections (sections 07–10) should all be considered in this regime.
Pinpoint Same velocity ≈ same E/A
\[ v_1=v_2 \;\Rightarrow\; (E/A)_1\approx(E/A)_2 \]
Comparing stopping curves using \(E/A\) largely removes the trivial mass dependence of the projectile velocity. Curves for different ions overlap approximately at equal \(E/A\) when the underlying physics is velocity-driven.
Suggestion Use E/A for cross-ion comparisons Total kinetic energy conflates mass and velocity effects, making it a poor axis for comparing different projectiles. Always distinguish total kinetic energy from energy per nucleon when comparing protons, alpha particles and heavy ions.

03 Corrected Bethe–Bloch equation

For a heavy charged projectile, the practical Bethe–Bloch expression can be written in terms of the projectile effective charge and the principal physical corrections: density effect, shell correction, Barkas correction and Bloch correction.

$$ \boxed{ -\frac{1}{\rho} \left\langle\frac{dE}{dx}\right\rangle = K z_{\rm eff}^{\,2} \frac{Z}{A} \frac{1}{\beta^2} \left[ \frac{1}{2} \ln\!\left( \frac{2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2W_{\max}} {I^2} \right) - \beta^2 - \frac{\delta(\beta\gamma)}{2} - \frac{C(\beta)}{Z} + z_{\rm eff}L_1(\beta) + z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}L_2(\beta) \right] } $$
$$ \boxed{ K=4\pi N_A r_e^2m_ec^2 \simeq 0.307075\; \mathrm{MeV\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}} } $$

Here, \(K\) is the constant defined above, and the bracketed stopping-number expression collects the Bethe logarithm together with the density, shell, Barkas and Bloch corrections detailed in sections 06–09.

Term Meaning Physical interpretation Why it matters
\(z_{\rm eff}\) Effective projectile charge The charge the projectile presents to the medium, which can be less than its bare nuclear charge (see section 10). Enters the leading term squared, so it dominates the overall magnitude of the stopping power.
\(Z\) Absorber atomic number Number of electrons per target atom available to interact with the projectile. Sets the electron density of the target together with \(A\) and \(\rho\).
\(A\) Absorber molar mass Molar mass of the target material. The ratio \(Z/A\) sets the electron density per unit mass of the absorber.
\(\beta=v/c\) Reduced projectile speed Projectile velocity as a fraction of the speed of light. Governs the overall \(1/\beta^2\) scaling and the argument of the logarithmic stopping number.
\(\gamma=(1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}\) Lorentz factor Relativistic factor associated with the projectile velocity. Controls the relativistic rise of \(W_{\max}\) and the density-effect correction at high energy.
\(I\) Mean excitation energy Effective average ionization/excitation energy of the target atom. A material-specific parameter that sets the argument of the Bethe logarithm; small errors in \(I\) shift the whole curve.
\(\delta(\beta\gamma)\) Density-effect correction Reduction from dielectric screening of the projectile field by the medium (section 06). Suppresses the relativistic rise of stopping power at high \(\beta\gamma\).
\(C(\beta)/Z\) Shell correction Accounts for bound-electron structure at low velocity (section 07). Lowers the stopping number when the projectile is not fast compared with atomic electron motion.
\(L_1(\beta)\) Barkas term Odd-in-charge polarization correction (section 08). Distinguishes the stopping of positive and negative projectiles at the same speed.
\(L_2(\beta)\) Bloch term Higher-order Coulomb correction beyond the first Born approximation (section 09). Becomes significant for slow, highly charged projectiles.
Interpretation: this equation describes primarily the electronic/collision stopping of the projectile. Experimental or tabulated total stopping data can additionally contain nuclear stopping, particularly at low projectile energy.

04 Maximum energy transfer Wmax

Wmax is the largest kinetic energy that a projectile of mass M can transfer to a free electron in a single collision.

$$ \boxed{ W_{\max} = \frac{ 2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2 }{ 1+2\gamma m_e/M+(m_e/M)^2 } } $$

Here, \(m_e\) is the electron mass and \(c\) the speed of light, so the numerator \(2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2\) sets the overall scale of the energy transfer, while the denominator accounts for the finite projectile mass \(M\).

For a heavy projectile, \(M \gg m_e\), this becomes

$$ \boxed{ W_{\max} \approx 2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2 } $$

In this limit the mass-ratio terms in the denominator vanish and \(W_{\max}\) depends only on the projectile velocity through \(\beta^2\gamma^2\).

Term Meaning Physical interpretation Why it matters
\(W_{\max}\) Maximum energy transfer The largest kinetic energy a projectile can impart to a free electron in a single head-on collision. Sets the upper limit of the energy-transfer spectrum and appears inside the Bethe logarithm.
\(M\) Projectile mass Rest mass of the incoming charged particle. Determines how closely the projectile approaches the heavy-particle limit \(M\gg m_e\).
\(m_e\) Electron mass Rest mass of the target electron receiving the energy transfer. The natural mass scale of \(W_{\max}\); its ratio to \(M\) controls the size of the denominator correction.
\(\beta,\ \gamma\) Reduced speed & Lorentz factor Projectile velocity relative to \(c\) and the associated relativistic factor. \(W_{\max}\) grows with \(\beta^2\gamma^2\), directly driving the relativistic rise of the stopping-number logarithm.
Analysis Condition / result Physical explanation Practical implication
Extreme Heavy projectile
\[ M \gg m_e \]
The finite projectile-mass denominator approaches unity and the heavy-particle approximation becomes excellent. The simplified \(W_{\max}\approx 2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2\) expression is adequate for protons and heavier ions.
Pinpoint Relativistic growth \(W_{\max}\) increases with \(\beta^2\gamma^2\) and therefore contributes to the logarithmic rise of the stopping number. This growth is a key driver of the relativistic rise seen in measured stopping-power curves at high energy.
Suggestion Retain the exact expression The heavy-particle approximation can break down for very light or very fast projectiles. Use the full expression in the calculator and expose the heavy-projectile approximation only as a diagnostic option.

05 Bethe stopping number

Defining a stopping number makes the physical contributions transparent. The total electronic stopping can then be separated into its prefactor and the dimensionless stopping number.

$$ \boxed{ L(\beta) = \frac12 \ln\!\left( \frac{ 2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2W_{\max} }{I^2} \right) - \beta^2 - \frac{\delta}{2} - \frac{C}{Z} + z_{\rm eff}L_1 + z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}L_2 } $$
$$ \boxed{ \frac{S_{\rm col}}{\rho} = K \frac{Z}{A} \frac{z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}}{\beta^2} L(\beta) } $$

Here, the mass stopping power \(S_{\rm col}/\rho\) factorizes into the prefactor \(K(Z/A)z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}/\beta^2\) and the dimensionless stopping number \(L(\beta)\), which carries all of the velocity- and correction-dependent physics.

Term Meaning Physical interpretation Why it matters
\(L(\beta)\) Bethe stopping number Dimensionless quantity collecting the Bethe logarithm and all physical corrections. Isolating \(L(\beta)\) lets each correction be evaluated and plotted independently of the overall \(K z_{\rm eff}^2Z/A\beta^2\) prefactor.
\(\delta\) Density-effect correction Enters as \(-\delta/2\); reduces \(L(\beta)\) at high \(\beta\gamma\) (section 06). Suppresses the relativistic rise of the stopping number.
\(C/Z\) Shell correction Enters as \(-C/Z\); reduces \(L(\beta)\) at low velocity (section 07). Accounts for bound-electron structure that the free-electron Bethe derivation neglects.
\(z_{\rm eff}L_1\) Barkas term Odd-in-charge correction added to \(L(\beta)\) (section 08). Splits the stopping number for positive versus negative projectiles of the same speed.
\(z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}L_2\) Bloch term Higher-order Coulomb correction added to \(L(\beta)\) (section 09). Becomes significant for slow, highly charged projectiles.
\(S_{\rm col}/\rho\) Collision (electronic) mass stopping power The full electronic stopping power reconstructed from the prefactor and \(L(\beta)\). Excludes nuclear stopping (section 11), which must be added separately if present.
Why this form is useful: the explorer can evaluate the individual terms inside \(L(\beta)\) and identify which correction is responsible for a deviation from the basic Bethe prediction.
Analysis Condition / result Physical explanation Practical implication
Extreme L(β) small The logarithmic stopping number can become strongly modified at low velocity by shell, effective-charge and higher-order effects. A small or negative \(L(\beta)\) signals that low-velocity corrections dominate over the bare Bethe logarithm.
Pinpoint Sign matters Density and shell corrections enter with negative signs, while the Barkas and Bloch terms modify the result through higher-order charge dependence. Tracking signs correctly is essential when diagnosing whether a correction raises or lowers the predicted stopping power.
Suggestion Use a term-by-term plot A single summed curve for \(L(\beta)\) hides which correction is responsible for a given feature. Plot each contribution to \(L(\beta)\) against \(E/A\) to locate the energy range where each physical correction becomes relevant.

06 Density-Effect Correction

At high projectile energies, the electric field of a fast charged particle extends over increasingly large distances. The surrounding medium responds through dielectric polarization, which screens this long-range field and reduces the contribution of distant collisions to the total energy loss.

The density effect therefore acts as a medium-polarization correction to the relativistic stopping-power increase. Its importance is governed primarily by the relativistic parameter

$$ \beta\gamma=\frac{v/c}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}} $$

As the projectile becomes more relativistic, the polarization response of the medium becomes increasingly important. The correction is incorporated by reducing the effective logarithmic contribution to the stopping power:

$$ L_{\mathrm{eff}} = L_{\mathrm{collision}} - \frac{1}{2}\,\delta $$

Here, \(L_{\mathrm{collision}}\) represents the logarithmic collision term obtained without dielectric screening, while \(\delta\) is the material-dependent density-effect correction. Thus, the density effect does not represent an additional interaction mechanism; instead, it modifies the long-range electromagnetic interaction already included in the collision term.

$$ \delta = \delta\!\left(\beta\gamma,\, \rho,\, \text{electronic response of the medium}\right) $$
Term Meaning Physical interpretation Why it matters
\(\beta\gamma\) Relativistic projectile parameter Combines velocity and Lorentz factor into the single variable governing the range of the projectile's electromagnetic field. The density effect is essentially a function of \(\beta\gamma\) alone, which is why it is used as its natural argument.
\(\delta\) Density-effect correction Reduction of the logarithmic stopping term caused by dielectric polarization of the medium. Grows with \(\beta\gamma\) and suppresses the relativistic rise of the stopping power at high energy.
\(\rho\) Material density Mass density of the absorbing medium. A denser medium polarizes more strongly, so \(\delta\) depends on \(\rho\) as well as on the electronic response of the material.
\(L_{\rm collision}\) Uncorrected logarithmic collision term The Bethe logarithmic stopping contribution evaluated without dielectric screening. The baseline from which the density effect subtracts \(\delta/2\).
\(L_{\rm eff}\) Dielectric-corrected logarithmic term The collision logarithm after the density-effect correction is applied. This is the quantity that actually enters the measured stopping power at high projectile energy.
Analysis Condition / result Physical explanation Practical implication
Extreme Low βγ — minimal screening For relatively small \(\beta\gamma\), the density-effect correction is generally small. Energy loss is therefore dominated by ordinary Coulomb collisions with atomic electrons. \(\delta\) can typically be neglected at non-relativistic projectile energies.
Pinpoint Relativistic rise is suppressed As the projectile becomes relativistic, dielectric screening of distant electromagnetic interactions grows, reducing their contribution to stopping power. Without this effect, stopping power would keep rising logarithmically at high energy. Dielectric screening is what produces the characteristic high-energy flattening of measured stopping-power curves.
Suggestion Use medium-specific data The magnitude of \(\delta\) depends on the electronic properties of the specific material, not just on \(\beta\gamma\). Obtain the density-effect correction from material-specific (e.g. Sternheimer) parameters or evaluated stopping-power data rather than a universal approximation.

07 Shell correction

The asymptotic Bethe derivation treats target electrons as approximately free during the collision. This approximation becomes less accurate when the projectile moves slowly enough that the binding and orbital motion of atomic electrons cannot be neglected.

$$ \boxed{ \Delta L_{\rm shell} = -\frac{C(\beta)}{Z} } $$

Here, \(\Delta L_{\rm shell}\) is the shell-dependent contribution to the stopping number. The minus sign indicates that electron binding generally reduces the stopping predicted by the ideal free-electron approximation.

$$ \boxed{ C(\beta) = C_K(\beta) + C_L(\beta) + C_M(\beta) +\cdots } $$

The total shell correction is constructed from contributions associated with the occupied atomic shells.

Formula used in this CBB implementation
$$ \boxed{\eta^2=(\beta\gamma)^2} $$
$$ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} C(I,\beta\gamma)={}& 10^{-6}I^2\left[ \frac{0.422377}{\eta^2}+ \frac{0.0304043}{\eta^4}- \frac{0.00038106}{\eta^6} \right]\\[4pt] &+10^{-9}I^3\left[ \frac{3.858019}{\eta^2}- \frac{0.1667989}{\eta^4}+ \frac{0.00157955}{\eta^6} \right] \end{aligned} } $$

Here \(I\) is the material mean excitation energy in eV and \(\eta=\beta\gamma\). The code evaluates this expression with the Barkas–Berger fit to Walske's shell correction. For compounds, the resulting \(C\) is normalized by the stoichiometric mean atomic number \(\bar Z\).

$$ \boxed{ \Delta L_{\rm shell}=-\frac{C(I,\beta\gamma)}{\bar Z} } $$
$$ \boxed{ \bar Z=\frac{\sum_i n_i Z_i}{\sum_i n_i} } $$

The fit is used directly when \((\beta\gamma)^2\ge 0.13\). Below this limit the polynomial itself is not merely inaccurate but diverges — every term above is built from negative powers of \(\eta^2\), so as \(\eta^2\to0\) the expression blows up rather than tending to zero. There is no literature closed form that continues this particular fit past its own boundary: ICRU/PDG instead switch to Bichsel's numerically tabulated Walske shell corrections in this regime, and abandon the corrected-Bethe framework altogether below \(\beta\approx0.05\) in favor of empirical Andersen–Ziegler stopping powers.

Rather than reproducing that full machinery, this implementation instead continues the fit below \(\eta^2=0.13\) with a boundary-matched power law:

$$ \boxed{ C(\eta^2)=C(0.13)\left(\frac{\eta^2}{0.13}\right)^{\!n}, \qquad \eta^2<0.13 } $$

\(C(0.13)\) is just the fit evaluated at the boundary, so the curve is continuous there by construction; the exponent \(n\) forces \(C\to0\) as \(v\to0\), which is physically necessary even though this specific power-law shape is not. This is a smooth placeholder, not a literature result — treat it as a convenient default rather than a validated low-energy model. \(n=0\) simply freezes \(C\) at its boundary value. Both the extrapolation itself and the exponent \(n\) (default 1) are adjustable in the Comparison tab sidebar, under the shell-correction toggle.

Term Meaning Physical interpretation Why it matters
\(\beta\) Reduced projectile speed \[ \beta=\frac{v}{c} \] compares the projectile velocity \(v\) with the speed of light \(c\). Shell corrections generally become more important as \(\beta\) decreases.
\(Z\) Atomic number of the absorber The correction is normalized by the number of electrons in an atom of the target material. It expresses the shell effect as a correction per target atom within the stopping-number formulation.
\(C(\beta)\) Total shell correction function Describes how the bound-electron structure modifies the free-electron Bethe approximation at a given projectile speed. A larger \(C(\beta)/Z\) means a stronger departure from the asymptotic high-velocity result.
\(C_K(\beta)\) K-shell contribution Correction associated primarily with tightly bound inner-shell electrons. Inner-shell binding can strongly affect the collision response when the projectile is not fast compared with electron orbital motion.
\(C_L(\beta)\) L-shell contribution Contribution from the next group of bound electron shells. Adds to the total atomic-structure correction and has its own characteristic binding and velocity dependence.
\(C_M(\beta)\) M-shell contribution Contribution from more weakly bound outer electrons. Outer-shell electrons also depart from the ideal free-electron response, particularly as projectile velocity decreases.
\(-C(\beta)/Z\) Net shell correction The complete shell contribution added to the stopping-number expression. The negative sign normally lowers the stopping number relative to the asymptotic Bethe result.

Extreme Low projectile velocity
\[ v \sim v_{\rm orbital} \]

08 Barkas correction

The leading Bethe–Bloch stopping power is proportional to \(z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}\) and is therefore even in projectile charge. The first higher-order polarization correction is the Barkas term. In the Comparison model it enters the stopping number as \(+z_{\rm eff}L_1(\beta)\). Since the complete Bethe–Bloch prefactor already contains \(z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}\), the resulting stopping-power contribution is proportional to \(z_{\rm eff}^{\,3}\). The present implementation uses the compact first-order approximation \(L_1(\beta)\approx\pi\alpha/(2\beta)\).

$$ \boxed{ L_{\rm corr}(\beta) =L_0(\beta)-\frac{\delta}{2}-\frac{C}{Z} +z_{\rm eff}L_1(\beta)+z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}L_2 } $$

The explicit Barkas function used by the Comparison calculation is

$$ \boxed{ L_1(\beta)\approx\frac{\pi\alpha}{2\beta} } $$

Therefore the explicit Barkas contribution to the mass stopping power is

$$ \boxed{ \left(\frac{S}{\rho}\right)_{\rm Barkas} =K\frac{Z}{A}\frac{z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}}{\beta^2} \left[z_{\rm eff}L_1(\beta)\right] } $$
$$ \boxed{ \left(\frac{S}{\rho}\right)_{\rm Barkas} \approx K\frac{Z}{A} \frac{\pi\alpha\,z_{\rm eff}^{\,3}}{2\beta^3} } $$
$$ \boxed{ S_{\rm Barkas}\propto\frac{z_{\rm eff}^{\,3}}{\beta^3} } $$
$$ \boxed{ S(+z)\neq S(-z) } $$

Physically, a positive projectile attracts the electron cloud whereas a negative projectile repels it. The induced polarization therefore depends on the sign of the projectile charge, which is why the Barkas contribution to the stopping number is odd in \(z_{\rm eff}\).

Term Meaning Physical interpretation Why it matters
\(z_{\rm eff}L_1(\beta)\) Barkas contribution to the stopping number The Comparison model adds this term directly to the corrected Bethe stopping number. Multiplication by the external \(z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}/\beta^2\) prefactor produces the characteristic odd-in-charge \(z_{\rm eff}^{\,3}\) stopping term. It introduces charge-sign asymmetry: positive and negative projectiles of the same speed do not, in general, have identical stopping power.
\(L_1(\beta)\approx\pi\alpha/(2\beta)\) First-order Barkas polarization function The explicit compact approximation used by the Comparison model. Here \(\alpha\) is the fine-structure constant and \(\beta=v/c\). Within this approximation \(L_1\propto1/\beta\), so the Barkas contribution increases relative to the leading term as the projectile velocity decreases.
\(\displaystyle(S/\rho)_{\rm Barkas}\approx K(Z/A)\pi\alpha z_{\rm eff}^{3}/(2\beta^3)\) Explicit Barkas stopping contribution Combining the \(z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}/\beta^2\) Bethe prefactor with \(+z_{\rm eff}L_1(\beta)\), and using \(L_1(\beta)\approx\pi\alpha/(2\beta)\), gives an explicit \(z_{\rm eff}^{\,3}/\beta^3\) contribution. This is the explicit first-order Barkas approximation used in the interactive Comparison calculation; it is a compact model correction rather than a complete all-velocity polarization theory.
Analysis Condition / result Physical explanation Practical implication
Extreme Small β / large |z| Higher-order Coulomb effects become more important as the Coulomb interaction between projectile and target electrons becomes stronger. The Barkas correction can no longer be neglected once \(z_{\rm eff}\alpha/\beta\) is not small.
Pinpoint Charge-sign dependence
\[ S(+z)\neq S(-z) \]
Barkas physics can be identified by comparing particles of opposite charge at similar velocity. A measured asymmetry between positive and negative projectiles at equal speed is the clearest experimental signature of the Barkas correction.
Suggestion Keep the sign of z Replacing the projectile charge with \(|z|\) inside the Barkas term destroys the very asymmetry the correction is meant to capture. The implementation must not replace the projectile charge by \(|z|\) inside the Barkas contribution.

09 Bloch correction

The Bethe treatment is based on the first-order Born approximation. The Bloch correction accounts for higher-order quantum-mechanical effects in the Coulomb interaction between the projectile and the target electrons.

$$ \boxed{ +z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}L_2(\beta) } $$

Consequently, the corresponding contribution to stopping behaves approximately as

$$ \boxed{ S_{\rm Bloch}\propto z_{\rm eff}^{\,4} } $$

For a non-relativistic point projectile, introduce the Coulomb parameter

$$ \boxed{ \eta = \frac{z_{\rm eff}\alpha}{\beta} = \frac{z_{\rm eff}e^2}{\hbar v} } $$

A standard Coulomb-series representation is

$$ \boxed{ L_{\rm Bloch}(\eta) = - \left[ \operatorname{Re}\psi(1+i\eta) + \gamma_E \right] } $$
$$ \boxed{ L_{\rm Bloch}(\eta) = - \eta^2 \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n(n^2+\eta^2)} } $$

Here, \(\psi\) is the digamma function and \(\gamma_E\) the Euler–Mascheroni constant; the equivalent infinite-sum form below makes the same function easier to evaluate numerically.

Term Meaning Physical interpretation Why it matters
\(z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}L_2(\beta)\) Bloch contribution to \(L(\beta)\) Higher-order quantum correction to the Coulomb interaction beyond the first Born approximation used by Bethe. Produces a stopping contribution scaling as \(z_{\rm eff}^{\,4}\), important for slow, highly charged projectiles.
\(\eta\) Coulomb interaction (Sommerfeld) parameter \(\eta=z_{\rm eff}\alpha/\beta=z_{\rm eff}e^2/\hbar v\) measures the strength of the Coulomb interaction relative to the projectile's quantum-mechanical wavelength. The natural expansion parameter of the Bloch correction: small \(\eta\) means the Born approximation is adequate.
\(\psi\) Digamma function Logarithmic derivative of the gamma function, evaluated at the complex argument \(1+i\eta\). Provides the closed-form expression for \(L_{\rm Bloch}(\eta)\).
\(\gamma_E\) Euler–Mascheroni constant Fixed mathematical constant (\(\approx0.5772\)) that normalizes the digamma expression. Ensures \(L_{\rm Bloch}(\eta)\to 0\) as \(\eta\to 0\), recovering the unperturbed Bethe result.
n Summation index Runs over positive integers in the series representation of \(L_{\rm Bloch}(\eta)\). Gives a numerically convergent way to evaluate the same function represented above by the digamma form.
Analysis Condition / result Physical explanation Practical implication
Extreme η ≪ 1 The correction becomes perturbatively small and the first-order Bethe treatment is increasingly adequate. The Bloch correction can be neglected for light, fast, and weakly charged projectiles.
Pinpoint η ∝ z/β Bloch effects grow for large projectile charge and decreasing velocity. Slow, highly charged heavy ions are exactly where this correction is most needed.
Suggestion Use a relativistic model at high energy The non-relativistic Coulomb expression for \(\eta\) does not capture relativistic modifications to the Bloch correction. For precision work involving relativistic projectiles, use the appropriate Lindhard–Sørensen generalization rather than a purely non-relativistic Coulomb expression.

10 Effective charge correction for ions

A partially stripped heavy ion does not generally behave as a bare nucleus with charge Z₁. At lower velocity, electron capture and charge exchange reduce the mean projectile charge.

$$ \boxed{ z_{\rm eff} = z_{\rm eff} \left(v,Z_1,\mathrm{target}\right) } $$

The dominant velocity and charge dependence of electronic stopping is

$$ \boxed{ S_{\rm col} \propto \frac{z_{\rm eff}^{\,2}}{v^2} } $$

One possible empirical representation is

$$ \boxed{ z_{\rm eff} = Z_1 \left[ 1- \exp\!\left( -a\frac{v} {v_0Z_1^{\,b}} \right) \right] } $$
Term Meaning Physical interpretation Why it matters
\(z_{\rm eff}\) Effective projectile charge The mean charge state a partially stripped ion presents to the medium, generally \(v\)- and target-dependent. Replaces the bare nuclear charge \(Z_1\) in the stopping-power formula whenever the projectile is not fully ionized.
\(Z_1\) Projectile nuclear charge The full atomic number of the projectile ion. The upper bound that \(z_{\rm eff}\) approaches at high velocity, once the ion is fully stripped.
\(v\) Projectile velocity Determines how effectively the projectile retains captured electrons against stripping collisions. The primary variable controlling the transition from partially screened to fully stripped charge states.
\(v_0=\alpha c\) Bohr velocity Characteristic atomic velocity scale (\(\alpha\) is the fine-structure constant). Sets the natural velocity scale against which \(v/Z_1^{\,b}\) is compared in the empirical model.
\(a,\,b\) Empirical model parameters Fitted constants controlling the shape of the velocity-dependent stripping curve. Different parameterizations exist; the specific values used should always be checked against the source model.
Important: this is an empirical example, not a universal effective-charge law. Different stopping-power models use different parameterizations and may depend explicitly on projectile species, target material and charge-state equilibrium.
Analysis Condition / result Physical explanation Practical implication
Extreme High velocity
\[ z_{\rm eff}\rightarrow Z_1 \]
The projectile becomes increasingly stripped and behaves more like a bare ion. At sufficiently high energy the bare-charge Bethe–Bloch expression becomes an adequate approximation on its own.
Pinpoint Low velocity
\[ z_{\rm eff} < Z_1 \]
Electron capture and charge exchange reduce the effective charge and therefore suppress the \(z^2/\beta^2\) stopping enhancement. Using the bare charge \(Z_1\) instead of \(z_{\rm eff}\) at low velocity will overestimate the stopping power for heavy ions.
Suggestion Make zeff selectable Different effective-charge models can give noticeably different low-velocity stopping predictions for the same ion. The explorer should distinguish bare-charge calculations from effective-charge calculations and clearly display the chosen model.

11 Correct separation of stopping mechanisms

The corrected Bethe–Bloch expression should not be interpreted as a universal expression for total stopping at all energies. The total energy loss can be separated into electronic and nuclear components.

$$ \boxed{ S_{\rm total} = S_{\rm electronic} + S_{\rm nuclear} } $$
$$ \boxed{ -\frac{dE}{dx} = \left(-\frac{dE}{dx}\right)_{\!e} + \left(-\frac{dE}{dx}\right)_{\!n} } $$

Here, \(S_{\rm total}\) is the full measured or tabulated stopping power, decomposed into an electronic part \(S_{\rm electronic}\) and a nuclear part \(S_{\rm nuclear}\); the second boxed line is the equivalent statement written as \((dE/dx)\) contributions.

Electronic stopping

Ionization, excitation, polarization and electronic energy transfer. The corrected Bethe–Bloch model primarily describes this component.

+

Nuclear stopping

Elastic collisions between the projectile and target nuclei. It becomes increasingly relevant as projectile energy decreases.

Term Meaning Physical interpretation Why it matters
\(S_{\rm total}\) Total stopping power The full energy-loss rate a projectile actually experiences in the material. What experiments generally measure, unless a technique specifically isolates one mechanism.
\(S_{\rm electronic}\) Electronic stopping Energy loss to ionization, excitation and polarization of target electrons. The component described by the corrected Bethe–Bloch expression (sections 03–10).
\(S_{\rm nuclear}\) Nuclear stopping Energy loss through elastic Coulomb collisions between the projectile and target nuclei. Not described by the Bethe–Bloch model; must be added separately, especially at low energy.
Explorer diagnostic: if experimental data deviate strongly from corrected Bethe–Bloch at low E/A, do not immediately introduce an additional electronic correction. First determine whether the plotted data represent electronic stopping alone or total stopping containing a significant nuclear component.
Analysis Condition / result Physical explanation Practical implication
Extreme High energy
\[ S_{e}\gg S_{n} \]
Electronic stopping generally dominates and the corrected Bethe–Bloch framework becomes increasingly appropriate on its own. Nuclear stopping can typically be neglected at high projectile energy.
Pinpoint Low energy
\[ S_{n}\ \text{increases} \]
Nuclear stopping can become comparable to, or larger than, the electronic contribution as projectile energy decreases. An apparent low-energy "excess" over corrected Bethe–Bloch may simply be unaccounted nuclear stopping rather than missing electronic physics.
Suggestion Plot both components A single total-stopping curve conflates two physically distinct mechanisms. When nuclear stopping data or a suitable model are available, display \(S_e\), \(S_n\), and \(S_{\rm total}\) separately.

13 Used statistics (Comparison tab)

The Comparison tab's statistics table quantifies agreement between measured stopping power \(S_{\rm exp}\) and the corrected Bethe–Bloch prediction \(S_{\rm th}\) for the points currently selected. All quantities below are computed from the per-point percentage deviation

$$ \delta_i = \left(\frac{S_{{\rm exp},i}}{S_{{\rm th},i}}-1\right)\times 100 $$

over the \(N\) points where the theory curve evaluates successfully. Since no free parameters are fitted to the data, the degrees of freedom used below is simply \(N\).

Statistic Formula What it flags
Points compared \(N\) Sample size behind every statistic below
Mean deviation \(\bar{\delta}=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_i \delta_i\) Overall systematic offset (sign matters)
RMS deviation \(\delta_{\rm RMS}=\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_i \delta_i^2}\) Typical spread, penalizes outliers more than MAD
Mean abs. deviation \(\mathrm{MAD}=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_i |\delta_i|\) Typical spread, robust to a few large outliers
Max |deviation| \(\delta_{\max}=\max_i |\delta_i|\) Worst single-point disagreement
χ² \(\chi^2=\displaystyle\sum_i \dfrac{(S_{{\rm exp},i}-S_{{\rm th},i})^2}{\sigma_i^2}\), \(\sigma_i=|S_{{\rm exp},i}|\cdot \mathrm{pe}_i/100\) Goodness of fit weighted by reported % error; only formed from points that carry a reported error
Reduced χ² (χ²/N) \(\chi^2_\nu=\chi^2/N\) Near 1 indicates consistency with reported errors alone
Pearson r (log–log) Correlation of \(x=\log_{10}S_{\rm exp}\) and \(y=\log_{10}S_{\rm th}\) Shape agreement across the log-log plot, independent of a constant multiplicative offset
R² (log–log) \(R^2=r^2\) Fraction of log-log variance shared between data and theory
Note: the Pearson r and R² are computed in log-space because stopping power spans several orders of magnitude, so a log-log correlation better reflects agreement as seen on the plot than a linear-space correlation would.
Analysis Condition / result Statistical explanation Practical implication
Extreme R² ≈ 1 with large mean deviation Because \(S\) spans several orders of magnitude, the log–log correlation is dominated by that broad dynamic range rather than by point-by-point accuracy. A near-perfect R² can coexist with a systematic 10–20% offset — R² alone is not evidence of good agreement.
Pinpoint Mean deviation ≈ 0% Positive and negative point-by-point deviations \(\delta_i\) can cancel in the average even when individual points disagree substantially. Always read the mean deviation alongside RMS/MAD — a small mean with a large RMS signals scatter that a single number hides.
Suggestion Read the statistics as a set, not in isolation No single statistic here separates a systematic offset from random scatter or from a genuine energy-dependent trend. Use mean deviation for bias, RMS/MAD for spread, and reduced χ² against reported errors — then cross-check with 12 — CBB diagnostic map to localize which correction the residual points to.

14 Unit reference

The explorer stores the mass stopping power in a common internal unit so that heterogeneous datasets can be compared directly.

$$ \boxed{ \frac{S}{\rho} \;[\mathrm{MeV\,cm^2/mg}] } $$

Here, \(S/\rho\) is the mass stopping power, stored internally in units of MeV·cm²/mg, the reference unit all other representations are converted from.

$$ 1\; \frac{\mathrm{MeV}}{\mathrm{mg/cm^2}} = 1\; \mathrm{MeV\,cm^2/mg} = 10\; \mathrm{MeV\,cm^2/g} $$

This identity relates the mg/cm² and g/cm² areal-density conventions; multiplying by 10 converts between them since 1 g = 1000 mg while 1 cm² stays fixed.

$$ S_{\rm lin}[\mathrm{eV/\AA}] = 10\,\rho[\mathrm{g/cm^3}] \left(\frac{S}{\rho}\right) [\mathrm{MeV\,cm^2/mg}] $$

Here, \(S_{\rm lin}\) is the linear stopping power in eV per angstrom, obtained by multiplying the stored mass stopping power by the material density \(\rho\); the factor of 10 accounts for the unit conversion from MeV/mg to eV/Å.

$$ \varepsilon [10^{-15}\mathrm{eV\,cm^2/atom}] = \left(\frac{S}{\rho}\right) \frac{\bar M}{0.602047} \times10^{-15} $$

Here, \(\varepsilon\) is the stopping cross-section per target atom, obtained by multiplying the mass stopping power by the mean atomic mass \(\bar M\) and Avogadro's number (expressed through the constant 0.602047).

Term Meaning Physical interpretation Why it matters
\(S/\rho\) Mass stopping power (MeV·cm²/mg) The explorer's internal reference unit for stopping power. Every other unit shown in the explorer is derived from this stored value.
\(S_{\rm lin}\) Linear stopping power (eV/Å) Energy loss per unit physical length rather than per unit areal mass. Useful for thin-film and depth-profiling applications where length, not mass thickness, is the natural variable.
\(\varepsilon\) Stopping cross-section (eV·cm²/atom) Energy loss expressed per target atom rather than per unit mass or length. The natural unit for comparing stopping on an atom-by-atom basis, independent of the material's bulk density.
\(\rho\) Material density Mass per unit volume of the target material. Required to convert between mass-normalized and length-normalized stopping power.
\(\bar M\) Mean atomic mass of the target Average atomic mass used to convert from a per-gram to a per-atom basis. Required to convert between mass-normalized and atom-normalized stopping power.

16 How to interpret deviations from CBB

A deviation between experimental stopping power and the corrected Bethe–Bloch prediction should be interpreted hierarchically rather than immediately treated as evidence for a new correction.

$$ \boxed{ \mathrm{Residual} = S_{\rm data} - S_{\rm CBB} } $$

Here, \(S_{\rm data}\) is the measured or tabulated stopping power and \(S_{\rm CBB}\) is the corrected Bethe–Bloch prediction; the residual isolates whatever physics the model has not yet accounted for.

Observed residual First physical question Next action
Constant offset Normalization / unit problem? Check density, A, Z and unit conversion.
High-E suppression Density effect correctly applied? Check Sternheimer parameters.
Low-E suppression Shell correction / effective charge? Inspect C/Z and zeff.
Charge dependence Barkas/Bloch physics? Inspect η and charge scaling.
Low-E excess Nuclear stopping present? Compare with Se + Sn.
Ion-specific deviation Incorrect charge state? Test alternative zeff models.
Residual after all checks Missing physical mechanism? Only then test an additional model term.
Key principle: a successful corrected Bethe–Bloch model should explain the residual structure without introducing unnecessary free parameters. A residual that remains after units, kinematics, material parameters, shell, density, Barkas, Bloch, effective-charge and nuclear-stopping effects have been independently examined is much stronger evidence for genuinely missing physics.
Why the theory fits the data at all:
  • Well-tested leading order. The log⟨T⟩/I − β² term captures the dominant electronic-collision energy loss from first-principles QM (Born approximation), verified over roughly 1–500 MeV/nucleon for nearly a century.
  • Some inputs are empirically calibrated, not purely predictive. The mean excitation energy I, the Sternheimer density-effect parameters, and the shell correction are all semi-empirical fits (ICRU 37/49, Barkas/Bichsel) tuned to reproduce measured stopping powers — so part of the agreement reflects deliberate calibration, not a blind a priori prediction.
  • Where it stops fitting well. Below ~0.1–1 MeV/u, charge-state effects, electron capture/loss, and nuclear stopping dominate and the Born approximation breaks down. At very high energy, radiative losses (bremsstrahlung, pair production — mainly for electrons) sit outside the pure collisional formula. Points with large reported % error also widen the scatter independent of theory quality.
  • Use the Comparison tab's χ²/reduced-χ² and deviation plot to see exactly where, and by how much, a specific projectile/target combination departs from this expected behavior.
