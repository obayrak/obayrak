1. Theory (reference)

Analytical Boundary Conditions for the α + Be Radial Schrödinger Equation

A unified symbolic treatment of bound, quasi-bound (Gamow–Siegert), and scattering states

Notation

Symbol Meaning
$\mu$ reduced mass, $\mu = m_\alpha m_{\rm Be}/(m_\alpha+m_{\rm Be})$
$l$ orbital angular momentum quantum number
$E$ total energy in the c.m. frame; $E=\hbar^2k^2/2\mu$
$k$ wavenumber (real for scattering, $k=i\kappa$ for bound states, $k=k_r-ik_i$ for resonances)
$\eta(k)$ Sommerfeld (Coulomb) parameter, $\eta(k)=\dfrac{Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}e^2\mu}{\hbar^2 k}$
$R,a$ Woods–Saxon radius and diffuseness
$V_0$ real Woods–Saxon well depth
$V_l=\hbar^2l(l+1)/2\mu R^2$ constant interior centrifugal approximation
$F_l(\eta,kr),\,G_l(\eta,kr)$ regular / irregular Coulomb wavefunctions
$H_l^{(\pm)}(\eta,kr)=G_l\pm iF_l$ outgoing ($+$) / incoming ($-$) Coulomb Hankel functions
$\sigma_l={\rm arg}\,\Gamma(l+1+i\eta)$ Coulomb phase shift
$S_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$ (nuclear-modified) S-matrix element
$W_{-\eta,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)$ Whittaker function of the second kind

The interaction is taken, as specified, to be a finite-range Woods–Saxon nuclear potential plus a constant interior centrifugal shift inside $r<R$, and the exact Coulomb + exact centrifugal potential outside:

$$ V(r)= \begin{cases} \displaystyle -\frac{V_0}{1+e^{(r-R)/a}} + V_l\,, & r<R \\[2ex] \displaystyle \frac{Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}e^2}{r}+\frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2\mu r^2}\,, & r\ge R. \end{cases} $$

The constant-centrifugal approximation in the interior is what keeps the interior equation exactly solvable in closed form (it is the same device that makes the Eckart/Woods–Saxon class of potentials integrable via a Riemann/hypergeometric reduction); the exterior equation, in contrast, keeps the full $1/r^2$ centrifugal term and the exact Coulomb potential, since there the Coulomb wave equation is itself exactly solvable without such an approximation.

All results below are exact, closed-form, symbolic solutions of the corresponding differential equations; no discretization, shooting, or variational approximation is used anywhere.

Part I — Regularity at the Origin

The radial equation for $u_l(r)=rR_l(r)$ is

$$ u_l''(r) + \left[k^2-\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}-\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}V(r)\right]u_l(r)=0. \tag{I.1} $$

Indicial analysis. Near $r=0$ the Woods–Saxon term is finite, $V_N(r)\to -V_0/(1+e^{-R/a})\equiv V_N(0)$, so the only singular term in the bracket of (I.1) as $r\to0$ is the centrifugal term $l(l+1)/r^2$. Substituting the Frobenius ansatz $u_l(r)=r^{s}(1+O(r))$ into

$$ u_l'' - \frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}u_l + O(1)\,u_l = 0 $$

and keeping only the leading $r^{s-2}$ terms gives the indicial equation

$$ s(s-1)-l(l+1)=0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; s=l+1 \quad\text{or}\quad s=-l. \tag{I.2} $$

Thus the two independent local solutions behave as

$$ u_l^{\rm reg}(r)\sim r^{l+1},\qquad u_l^{\rm irr}(r)\sim r^{-l}\quad (r\to0). \tag{I.3} $$

Selection of the regular branch. The irregular branch $u_l^{\rm irr}\sim r^{-l}$ makes the full three-dimensional wavefunction $R_l(r)=u_l(r)/r\sim r^{-l-1}$ diverge at the origin for every $l\ge0$; the associated probability density $|R_l|^2 r^2\,dr\sim r^{-2l}\,dr$ is non-normalizable at $r=0$ for $l\ge 0$ (divergent for $l\ge 1/2$, and for $l=0$ it produces a $\delta$-function source term $-\nabla^2(1/r)=4\pi\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r)$ inconsistent with a source-free Schrödinger equation at the origin). Hence the irregular branch is discarded on physical grounds (finite kinetic energy / no point source at the origin) for every $l$, including $l=0$.

Consequently the physically acceptable interior solution is uniquely, up to normalization,

$$ \boxed{u_l(r)\;\propto\; r^{l+1}\big(1+c_1 r + c_2 r^2+\cdots\big),\qquad r\to0,} $$

and in particular

$$ \boxed{u_l(0)=0\quad\text{for every } l\ge0.} \tag{I.4} $$

This is the origin boundary condition quoted as (2) in the reference formalism (Vertse–Pál–Balogh) and holds identically for bound, quasi-bound, and scattering solutions, since it is a statement about the local behavior of the differential equation at $r=0$ and does not depend on the sign or complexity of $E$.

Part II — Matching at the Nuclear Radius $r=R$

Continuity of $u$ and $u'$

The potential $V(r)$ defined above has a finite jump discontinuity at $r=R$ (the Woods–Saxon form factor and its exterior continuation are both finite there; only the diffuseness of the physical Woods–Saxon form makes the true potential continuous, but even in the idealized sharp-cutoff limit used for matching, the discontinuity in $V$ is finite). Integrate (I.1) across a small interval $(R-\varepsilon,R+\varepsilon)$:

$$ u_l'(R+\varepsilon)-u_l'(R-\varepsilon) = \int_{R-\varepsilon}^{R+\varepsilon}\left[\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}+\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}V(r)-k^2\right]u_l(r)\,dr. \tag{II.1} $$

Because the integrand is bounded (no $\delta$-functions or $1/r$ singularities appear at $r=R$ for finite $V_0,R,a$), the right-hand side vanishes as $\varepsilon\to0$:

$$ \boxed{u_{\rm in}(R)=u_{\rm out}(R)} \tag{II.2} $$ $$ \boxed{u_{\rm in}'(R)=u_{\rm out}'(R).} \tag{II.3} $$

Why both conditions are needed. Equation (I.1) is a second-order ODE; a well-posed matching of two second-order solutions across an interior point requires exactly two conditions (value and slope) to fix the two free constants of the exterior solution relative to the (already normalization-fixed) interior solution. If $V(r)$ contained a $\delta$-function, (II.1) would instead give a finite jump in $u'$ with continuous $u$; if $V(r)$ contained a $1/r^2$-type singularity exactly at $R$, neither condition would survive in this simple form. For our finite, bounded Woods–Saxon discontinuity, both (II.2) and (II.3) hold exactly.

Logarithmic derivative and the transcendental eigenvalue equation

Divide (II.3) by (II.2):

$$ \boxed{\left.\frac{u'}{u}\right|_{\rm in}(R;E) \;=\; \left.\frac{u'}{u}\right|_{\rm out}(R;E).} \tag{II.4} $$

The logarithmic derivative is invariant under rescaling of either solution, so (II.4) is the matching condition: it depends only on $E$ (through $k^2=2\mu E/\hbar^2$) and on the fixed geometric/potential parameters $V_0,R,a,Z_\alpha,Z_{\rm Be},l$. Because $u_{\rm in}$ is pinned by the regularity condition (I.4) and $u_{\rm out}$ is pinned by whichever asymptotic condition at $r\to\infty$ is imposed (Part III/IV/V), equation (II.4) is one complex (or real) equation in the single unknown $E$ (or $k$):

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\rm in}(E) - \mathcal{L}_{\rm out}(E) = 0, \tag{II.5} $$

which is the transcendental eigenvalue equation common to all three problems. The three physical cases differ only in which exterior solution enters $\mathcal L_{\rm out}$.

The exact interior solution $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)$

With the constant-centrifugal approximation, the interior equation is

$$ u_l''(r)+\left[k^2-\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}V_l-\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\Big(-\frac{V_0}{1+e^{(r-R)/a}}\Big)\right]u_l(r)=0,\qquad r<R, \tag{II.6} $$

i.e. an effective pure Woods–Saxon problem with the substitution $k^2\to \tilde k^2\equiv k^2-\dfrac{2\mu V_l}{\hbar^2}$. This is exactly the Eckart-class potential problem: the substitution $x=-e^{-(r-R)/a}$ (equivalently $z=\big(1+e^{(r-R)/a}\big)^{-1}$) reduces (II.6) to a Riemann differential equation with regular singular points at $0,1,\infty$, whose solution regular at the origin is the Gauss hypergeometric function

$$ u_{\rm in}(r) \;\propto\; e^{i\tilde k r}\,F\!\left(\gamma_+,\gamma_+',\,1-2ia\tilde k,\,-e^{(R-r)/a}\right)\Big|_{\text{regular combination fixed by (I.4)}}, \tag{II.7} $$

$$ \gamma_\pm = \mp ia\tilde k \pm ia\sqrt{\tilde k^2+U_0},\qquad U_0=\frac{2\mu V_0}{\hbar^2}, \tag{II.8} $$

exactly as derived for the Woods–Saxon (Saxon–Woods) member of the Eckart class. $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)=u_{\rm in}'(R)/u_{\rm in}(R)$ then follows in closed form from (II.7)–(II.8) and standard hypergeometric-function derivative identities; it is an entire, single-valued analytic function of $k$ (equivalently of $E$) because $F(a,b,c,z)$ is analytic in its parameters away from the poles of $\Gamma(c)$, which do not occur here for physical $a>0$. This analyticity is what allows $k$ to be continued off the real axis in Parts III and IV below.

Part III — Bound-State Boundary Conditions ($E<0$)

Exterior solution

For $E<0$ write $k=i\kappa$, $\kappa=\sqrt{-2\mu E}/\hbar>0$. The Coulomb parameter analytically continues to

$$ \eta(i\kappa) \equiv \eta_\kappa = \frac{Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}e^2\mu}{\hbar^2\kappa} \quad(\text{real, since } Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}>0). \tag{III.1} $$

The exterior equation for $r\ge R$,

$$ u_l''(r)+\left[-\kappa^2-\frac{2\eta_\kappa\kappa}{r}-\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}\right]u_l(r)=0, \tag{III.2} $$

is the Coulomb wave equation analytically continued to negative energy. Its two independent solutions are the Whittaker functions $M_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)$ and $W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)$. As $r\to\infty$,

$$ M_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)\;\sim\; e^{+\kappa r}(2\kappa r)^{-\eta_\kappa}\big[1+O(1/r)\big] \quad\text{(exponentially growing)}, \tag{III.3} $$ $$ W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)\;\sim\; e^{-\kappa r}(2\kappa r)^{\eta_\kappa}\big[1+O(1/r)\big] \quad\text{(exponentially decaying)}. \tag{III.4} $$

Selection of the decaying branch. Square-integrability, $\displaystyle\int_R^\infty|u_l(r)|^2dr<\infty$, forbids the exponentially growing branch (III.3) with any nonzero coefficient. Hence

$$ \boxed{u_{\rm out}(r) = N\,W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r),\qquad r\ge R,} \tag{III.5} $$

which satisfies $u(r)\to0$ as $r\to\infty$ automatically, for any $\kappa>0$ — the Coulomb tail vanishes at infinity, so unlike a bound Coulomb (attractive) problem there is no additional discreteness imposed by the exterior equation alone; discreteness comes entirely from the interior matching below.

Transcendental energy equation

Insert (III.5) into (II.4), evaluating both sides at $k=i\kappa$. The interior side follows directly from (II.7)-(II.8): writing $u_{\rm in}(r)\propto e^{i\tilde k r}F(z(r))$ with $F(z)\equiv F(\gamma_+,\gamma_+';1-2ia\tilde k;z)$ and $z(r)=-e^{(R-r)/a}$, so that $z(R)=-1$ and $z'(r)=e^{(R-r)/a}/a$, the hypergeometric derivative identity $$ \frac{d}{dz}F(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)=\frac{\alpha\beta}{\gamma}\,F(\alpha+1,\beta+1;\gamma+1;z) $$ gives $u_{\rm in}'(r)=i\tilde k\,u_{\rm in}(r)+e^{i\tilde k r}F'(z(r))\,z'(r)$, and hence the **exact interior logarithmic derivative** $$ \mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)=\left.\frac{u_{\rm in}'(r)}{u_{\rm in}(r)}\right|_{r=R} = i\tilde k \;+\; \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{\gamma_+\gamma_+'}{1-2ia\tilde k}\cdot \frac{F(\gamma_++1,\gamma_+'+1;\,2-2ia\tilde k;\,-1)}{F(\gamma_+,\gamma_+';\,1-2ia\tilde k;\,-1)}, \qquad \tilde k=\tilde k(i\kappa), $$ with $\gamma_+,\gamma_+'$ given by (II.8) at $\tilde k=\tilde k(i\kappa)$. On the exterior side, since $u_{\rm out}(r)=N\,W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)$, its logarithmic derivative at $r=R$ is, by the chain rule ($d(2\kappa r)/dr=2\kappa$) and with the overall normalization $N$ cancelling top and bottom, $$ \mathcal L_{\rm out}(-\hbar^2\kappa^2/2\mu)=\left.\frac{u_{\rm out}'(r)}{u_{\rm out}(r)}\right|_{r=R}=\frac{N\cdot 2\kappa\,W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}'(2\kappa R)}{N\,W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa R)}=2\kappa\,\frac{W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}'(2\kappa R)}{W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa R)}. $$ Setting $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(-\hbar^2\kappa^2/2\mu)=\mathcal L_{\rm out}(-\hbar^2\kappa^2/2\mu)$ per (II.4) gives the boxed transcendental equation::

$$ \boxed{ \mathcal L_{\rm in}(-\hbar^2\kappa^2/2\mu) \;=\; 2\kappa\,\frac{W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}'(2\kappa R)}{W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa R)}.} \tag{III.6} $$

This is one real transcendental equation in the single real unknown $\kappa>0$ (equivalently in $E<0$); its discrete roots $\kappa_{n_r}$, $n_r=0,1,2,\dots$ (ordered by the number of interior nodes, per the standard oscillation theorem for Sturm–Liouville problems on a finite interval with a fixed, energy-independent boundary condition of $u\to0$ at both ends of the relevant interval) give the bound-state energies

$$ E_{n_r} = -\frac{\hbar^2\kappa_{n_r}^2}{2\mu}. $$

Existence of at least one root requires the interior well to be deep enough that $\mathcal L_{\rm in}$ can match the (always negative, for $\kappa>0$) logarithmic derivative of the decaying Whittaker function at $R$; uniqueness of each $E_{n_r}$ within a bracketed interval follows from the strict monotonicity in $\kappa$ of the right-hand side of (III.6) (a standard property of $W_{-\eta,\,l+1/2}$) combined with the interlacing (Sturm) property of $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(\kappa)$'s poles and zeros as $\kappa$ increases through the well.

Part IV — Quasi-Bound (Gamow–Siegert Resonance) Boundary Conditions

Complex energy ansatz

Let

$$ E = E_R - i\frac{\Gamma}{2},\qquad E_R>0,\ \Gamma>0, \tag{IV.1} $$

with corresponding complex wavenumber

$$ k=k_r-ik_i,\qquad k_r,k_i>0,\qquad E=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}(k_r-ik_i)^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}(k_r^2-k_i^2) - i\frac{\hbar^2 k_rk_i}{\mu}, \tag{IV.2} $$

so that $E_R=\hbar^2(k_r^2-k_i^2)/2\mu$ and $\Gamma = 2\hbar^2k_rk_i/\mu$. This $k$ lies in the fourth quadrant of the complex $k$-plane, exactly as in the neutral (Woods–Saxon-only) case treated earlier; the Coulomb parameter $\eta(k)=Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}e^2\mu/(\hbar^2k)$ is now complex as well, and all Coulomb functions below are understood as their standard analytic continuations to complex argument and complex $\eta$ (well defined via the confluent-hypergeometric representation of $F_l,G_l$; no numerical evaluation is implied, only the existence of the analytic continuation).

Siegert (purely outgoing) boundary condition

The physical requirement defining a resonant (Gamow) state is that the state decays only by particle emission — there is no wave coming in from infinity. In terms of the Coulomb Hankel functions $H_l^{(\pm)}(\eta,kr)=G_l\pm iF_l$, with asymptotic forms

$$ H_l^{(\pm)}(\eta,kr)\underset{r\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[\pm i\Big(kr-\eta\ln 2kr-\frac{l\pi}{2}+\sigma_l\Big)\right], \tag{IV.3} $$

the general exterior solution is $u_{\rm out}=A_{\rm in}H_l^{(-)}(\eta,kr)+A_{\rm out}H_l^{(+)}(\eta,kr)$. The Siegert boundary condition sets the incoming amplitude to zero:

$$ \boxed{A_{\rm in}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_{\rm out}(r) = N\,O_l(\eta,kr)\equiv N\,H_l^{(+)}(\eta,kr),\qquad r\ge R.} \tag{IV.4} $$

Substituting $k=k_r-ik_i$ into (IV.3),

$$ O_l(\eta,kr)\sim \exp\left[i(k_r-ik_i)r+\cdots\right] = e^{+k_i r}\,e^{\,i(k_rr+\cdots)}, \tag{IV.5} $$

reproduces the characteristic Gamow tail — oscillation with exponentially growing amplitude, $e^{+k_ir}e^{ik_rr}$ — which is the hallmark of the outgoing Siegert solution and the reason Gamow states are not square-integrable in the ordinary (Hilbert-space) sense; they require the Zel'dovich regularization,

$$ N^2=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}\int_0^\infty e^{-\varepsilon r^2}u^2(r)\,dr, \tag{IV.6} $$

evaluated in practice (as in the reference GAMOW code) by analytic continuation of the norm integral along a contour into the complex $r$-plane where the integrand's exponential growth is converted to exponential decay — again a fully analytic, non-numerical operation in principle, since the relevant tail integral has a closed form in terms of the same Coulomb/Whittaker special functions.

Complex transcendental equation

Matching (II.4) with (IV.4), evaluating both sides at the complex wavenumber $k=k_r-ik_i$. The interior side follows directly from (II.7)-(II.8): writing $u_{\rm in}(r)\propto e^{i\tilde k r}F(z(r))$ with $F(z)\equiv F(\gamma_+,\gamma_+';1-2ia\tilde k;z)$ and $z(r)=-e^{(R-r)/a}$, so that $z(R)=-1$ and $z'(r)=e^{(R-r)/a}/a$, the hypergeometric derivative identity $$ \frac{d}{dz}F(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)=\frac{\alpha\beta}{\gamma}\,F(\alpha+1,\beta+1;\gamma+1;z) $$ gives $u_{\rm in}'(r)=i\tilde k\,u_{\rm in}(r)+e^{i\tilde k r}F'(z(r))\,z'(r)$, and hence the **exact interior logarithmic derivative**, now evaluated at complex $k$ (equivalently complex $E=\hbar^2k^2/2\mu$), $$ \mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)=\left.\frac{u_{\rm in}'(r)}{u_{\rm in}(r)}\right|_{r=R} = i\tilde k \;+\; \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{\gamma_+\gamma_+'}{1-2ia\tilde k}\cdot \frac{F(\gamma_++1,\gamma_+'+1;\,2-2ia\tilde k;\,-1)}{F(\gamma_+,\gamma_+';\,1-2ia\tilde k;\,-1)}, \qquad \tilde k=\tilde k(k), $$ with $\gamma_+,\gamma_+'$ given by (II.8) at $\tilde k=\tilde k(k)$; this expression is unchanged in form from the bound-state case, since (as noted after (II.8)) $\mathcal L_{\rm in}$ is one and the same entire analytic function of $k$, merely evaluated here off the imaginary axis. On the exterior side, since $u_{\rm out}(r)=N\,O_l(\eta,kr)$ (the Siegert condition (IV.4)), its logarithmic derivative at $r=R$ is, by the chain rule ($d(kr)/dr=k$) and with the overall normalization $N$ cancelling top and bottom, $$ \mathcal L_{\rm out}(E)=\left.\frac{u_{\rm out}'(r)}{u_{\rm out}(r)}\right|_{r=R}=\frac{N\,k\,O_l'(\eta,kR)}{N\,O_l(\eta,kR)}=k\,\frac{O_l'(\eta,kR)}{O_l(\eta,kR)}. $$ Writing this out in terms of the underlying regular/irregular Coulomb functions, $O_l(\eta,kr)=G_l(\eta,kr)+iF_l(\eta,kr)$ (Part VI notation), so that $O_l'=G_l'+iF_l'$ (derivatives with respect to the argument $kr$) and the exterior logarithmic derivative is, fully explicitly, $$ \mathcal L_{\rm out}(E)=k\,\frac{G_l'(\eta,kR)+iF_l'(\eta,kR)}{G_l(\eta,kR)+iF_l(\eta,kR)}. $$ Setting $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)=\mathcal L_{\rm out}(E)$ per (II.4) gives the boxed complex transcendental equation:

$$ \boxed{ \mathcal L_{\rm in}(E) \;=\; k\,\frac{O_l'(\eta,kR)}{O_l(\eta,kR)},\qquad E=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2\mu}.} \tag{IV.7} $$

This is one complex equation in one complex unknown $k$ (equivalently $E$); its roots in the fourth quadrant, $k_n = k_{r,n}-ik_{i,n}$, give the discrete set of resonance parameters

$$ E_{R,n}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}(k_{r,n}^2-k_{i,n}^2),\qquad \Gamma_n=\frac{2\hbar^2}{\mu}k_{r,n}k_{i,n},\qquad \tau_n=\frac{\hbar}{\Gamma_n}. \tag{IV.8} $$

Physical interpretation. A complex eigenvalue of a nominally Hermitian-looking boundary-value problem is possible here precisely because the Siegert boundary condition (IV.4) is not self-adjoint (it fixes different linear combinations of $u,u'$ than the $L^2$ condition $u\to0$ used for bound states); the problem is a non-Hermitian eigenvalue problem on the space of purely outgoing functions. $E_R$ is the position of the resonance (its energy relative to the $\alpha$+Be dissociation threshold), and $\Gamma$ its width; by the time–energy uncertainty relation the state has a finite lifetime $\tau=\hbar/\Gamma$, decaying as $|\Psi(t)|^2\propto e^{-\Gamma t/\hbar}$ for an observer at fixed finite $r$ before the exponentially growing tail is reached — the growing exterior tail is not a physical particle density at $t=0$ but the imprint, at a fixed radius, of flux that was emitted at earlier and earlier times as $r$ increases, consistent with a source that has been decaying since $t=-\infty$ in the idealized stationary-state treatment.

Note also, per the standard classification recalled in the reference GAMOW paper: poles of $S_l(k)$ on the positive imaginary axis ($k=i\kappa$, $\kappa>0$) are the bound states of Part III; poles in the lower half $k$-plane with $k_r>k_i>0$ are physical resonances (as derived here); poles with $k_i>k_r$ are mathematically admissible roots of (IV.7) but correspond to unphysical (excessively broad, non-resonant) solutions and are excluded from the physical spectrum.

Part V — Scattering Boundary Conditions ($E>0$)

Exterior solution

For real $k>0$, $\eta(k)=Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}e^2\mu/(\hbar^2 k)$ is real and positive (repulsive Coulomb). The two independent, oscillatory (non-decaying) solutions of the exact exterior equation are $F_l(\eta,kr)$ and $G_l(\eta,kr)$, or equivalently the incoming/outgoing combinations $H_l^{(\pm)}=G_l\pm iF_l$ with asymptotics (IV.3) now understood for real $k$ (purely oscillatory, no exponential growth or decay). The general physical scattering solution is written as an incoming wave of fixed unit amplitude plus a scattered outgoing wave:

$$ \boxed{u_{\rm out}(r) = H_l^{(-)}(\eta,kr) - S_l\,H_l^{(+)}(\eta,kr),\qquad r\ge R,} \tag{V.1} $$

with $S_l$ the (nuclear-modified) partial-wave S-matrix element to be determined; $\sigma_l$ is already absorbed into the definition of $H_l^{(\pm)}$, so $S_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$ with $\delta_l$ the purely nuclear phase shift (the total phase relative to a Coulomb-only asymptotic wave is $\sigma_l+\delta_l$).

Matching equation and closed-form S-matrix

Insert (V.1) into (II.4). With $L\equiv \mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)$ the (real, since the interior potential is real and $E>0$ in the classically-relevant sense — see remark below) interior logarithmic derivative,

$$ L=\frac{H_l^{(-)\prime}(\eta,kR)-S_lH_l^{(+)\prime}(\eta,kR)}{H_l^{(-)}(\eta,kR)-S_lH_l^{(+)}(\eta,kR)}, \tag{V.2} $$

solve linearly for $S_l$:

$$ \boxed{ S_l(k) = \frac{H_l^{(-)\prime}(\eta,kR)-L\,H_l^{(-)}(\eta,kR)}{H_l^{(+)\prime}(\eta,kR)-L\,H_l^{(+)}(\eta,kR)}.} \tag{V.3} $$

where $L\equiv\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)$, given explicitly for real $k>0$ (scattering regime) by the same closed-form expression derived from (II.7)-(II.8) in Parts III-IV, $$ \mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)=\left.\frac{u_{\rm in}'(r)}{u_{\rm in}(r)}\right|_{r=R} = i\tilde k \;+\; \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{\gamma_+\gamma_+'}{1-2ia\tilde k}\cdot \frac{F(\gamma_++1,\gamma_+'+1;\,2-2ia\tilde k;\,-1)}{F(\gamma_+,\gamma_+';\,1-2ia\tilde k;\,-1)}, \qquad \tilde k=\tilde k(k), $$ with $\gamma_+,\gamma_+'$ from (II.8) evaluated at this real $k$; this is the same entire function of $k$ used for the bound-state pole (III.6) and the Gamow pole (IV.7), now sampled on the real axis, which is precisely the statement of Part VI item 1.

Equation (V.3) is the exact, closed-form analytic S-matrix element for arbitrary real $k>0$; equivalently the phase shift follows from

$$ \delta_l(k) = \frac{1}{2i}\ln S_l(k). \tag{V.4} $$

An equivalent real-variable form is obtained by writing $u_{\rm out}=\cos\delta_l\,F_l+\sin\delta_l\,G_l$ (a real basis valid for real $k$) and matching logarithmic derivatives at $r=R$:

$$ \boxed{\tan\delta_l(k) = \frac{F_l'(\eta,kR) - L\,F_l(\eta,kR)}{G_l'(\eta,kR) - L\,G_l(\eta,kR)}.} \tag{V.5} $$

(Equation (V.5) follows from (V.2) by substituting $H_l^{(\pm)}=G_l\pm iF_l$ into (V.2) and separating real and imaginary parts; it is algebraically equivalent to (V.3) via $S_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$.)

Unitarity remark. Because the interior potential (real $V_0,R,a$) and the exact exterior potential are both real for $E>0$, $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)$ and $L$ in (V.2)–(V.5) are real, which guarantees $|S_l(k)|=1$ for all real $k$ — the analytic statement of flux conservation (unitarity) for a purely real (non-absorptive) potential. If a complex (optical) potential were used instead, the same formulas (V.1)–(V.3) still hold verbatim, with $L$ now complex, and $|S_l|<1$ follows automatically, the missing flux $1-|S_l|^2$ being the reaction/absorption probability.

Part VI — Unified Analytical Framework

All three problems solved above share:

  1. The same interior differential equation (II.6) and the same closed-form interior solution (II.7)–(II.8), fixed uniquely (up to overall normalization) by the single regularity condition $u(0)=0$ derived in Part I. The interior logarithmic derivative $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)$ is one and the same analytic function of $E$ (or $k$) in every case; it is simply evaluated at a different location in the complex $k$-plane for each problem.

  2. The same matching principle at $r=R$ — continuity of $u$ and $u'$, equivalently continuity of the logarithmic derivative, equation (II.4)/(II.5) — is imposed identically in all three cases.

  3. A single exact exterior differential equation (the Coulomb equation with the full $1/r^2$ centrifugal term), whose two independent solutions $F_l,G_l$ (or $H_l^{(\pm)}$, or their analytic continuations $M_{-\eta,l+1/2},W_{-\eta,l+1/2}$) are the same pair of special functions in every regime; what changes between regimes is only which asymptotic combination of this same pair is physically admissible:

each root is simple (non-degenerate) because $\partial\mathcal L_{\rm in}/\partial\kappa\neq\partial \mathcal L_{\rm out}/\partial\kappa$ generically at a crossing, which follows from the strict monotonicity of the Whittaker-function logarithmic derivative in $\kappa$. - Quasi-bound states: (IV.7) is a holomorphic equation in $k$ (both sides analytic in the fourth quadrant, since $\mathcal L_{\rm in}$ is entire in $k$ as noted after (II.8), and $O_l(\eta,kr)$ is analytic in $k$ away from $k=0$); by the argument principle the number of roots inside any contour in the fourth quadrant enclosing them is finite and computable in principle from a contour integral of the logarithmic derivative of (IV.7), establishing existence of at most a countable, locally finite set of resonances, with uniqueness (simplicity of each root) guaranteed generically by the same non-degeneracy argument as above, now applied to the complex derivative of (IV.7). - Scattering states: for every real $k>0$ (with the sole possible exception of $k=0$ itself, where the Coulomb parameter $\eta(k)\to\infty$ and the analytic representation degenerates — the "does not work at zero energy" limitation noted in the reference formalism), $S_l(k)$ in (V.3) exists, is unique, and is unimodular; there is no discreteness here because the boundary condition (fixed unit incoming amplitude) is imposed for a continuum of $k$ values rather than isolating discrete roots of a homogeneous equation — scattering states form the continuous spectrum of the problem, complementary to the discrete bound-state spectrum found above.

Part VII — Derived Observables: S-Matrix, Elastic and Reaction Cross Sections

Everything in this section is obtained algebraically from the partial-wave S-matrix element $S_l(k)=e^{2i\delta_l(k)}$ of equation (V.3)/(V.4) — no new differential equation or boundary condition is introduced; these are simply the standard physical observables built from $S_l(k)$.

S-matrix components and the unitarity circle

For a purely real interior potential ($W_0=0$), Part V's unitarity remark showed $|S_l(k)|=1$ for all real $k$: the point $S_l(k)$ traces the unit circle in the complex plane (an Argand diagram) as $E$ is swept, with $\mathrm{Re}\,S_l=\cos2\delta_l$ and $\mathrm{Im}\,S_l=\sin2\delta_l$. Introducing a (small, illustrative) absorptive Woods–Saxon depth $W_0>0$ makes $L=\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)$ complex, which pulls the trajectory of $S_l(k)$ inside the unit circle: $|S_l|<1$ signals net removal of flux from the elastic channel (absorption / reaction), exactly the "missing flux" mentioned at the end of Part V.

Elastic and reaction cross sections

The partial-wave elastic cross section is the cross section for the alpha particle to scatter with its incoming flux fully returned to the elastic (alpha+Be-8, same internal state) channel:

$$ \boxed{\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}(k) = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\,(2l+1)\,\big|1-S_l(k)\big|^2.} \tag{VII.1} $$

The partial-wave reaction (absorption) cross section is the cross section for flux to leave the elastic channel entirely (captured, or re-emitted into any other open channel lumped into the optical potential's imaginary part):

$$ \boxed{\sigma_{\mathrm{reac},l}(k) = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\,(2l+1)\,\big(1-|S_l(k)|^2\big).} \tag{VII.2} $$

Their sum is the partial-wave total cross section, which by direct algebraic expansion of (VII.1)–(VII.2) equals the optical theorem's forward-scattering statement restricted to partial wave $l$:

$$ \sigma_{\mathrm{tot},l}(k) = \sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}+\sigma_{\mathrm{reac},l} = \frac{2\pi}{k^2}\,(2l+1)\,\big(1-\mathrm{Re}\,S_l(k)\big). \tag{VII.3} $$

The quantities $R_l\equiv|S_l|^2$ (elastic-channel survival probability) and $T_l\equiv 1-|S_l|^2$ (absorption probability) plotted alongside the phase shift are the unitarity-normalized (per-partial-wave, dimensionless) versions of (VII.1)–(VII.2): $\sigma_{\mathrm{reac},l}$ is directly proportional to $T_l$, while $\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}$ additionally depends on the phase $\delta_l$ through $|1-S_l|^2=1-2\,\mathrm{Re}\,S_l+|S_l|^2$, so $\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}$ is not a simple function of $R_l$ alone — it also encodes the interference between the incident and elastically-scattered waves.

Summed (angle-integrated) cross sections

Summing (VII.1)–(VII.3) over the partial waves $l=0,1,\dots,l_{\max}$ actually included in a given calculation gives the (truncated) total elastic, reaction, and total cross sections,

$$ \sigma_{\mathrm{el}}(E)=\sum_{l=0}^{l_{\max}}\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}(E),\qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{reac}}(E)=\sum_{l=0}^{l_{\max}}\sigma_{\mathrm{reac},l}(E),\qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}(E)=\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}(E)+\sigma_{\mathrm{reac}}(E), \tag{VII.4} $$

which converge as $l_{\max}\to\infty$ once $l$ exceeds roughly the classical grazing angular momentum $kR$ (beyond which the centrifugal barrier makes $S_l\to1$ and each partial wave's contribution to (VII.1)–(VII.3) vanishes); the finite-$l_{\max}$ sums plotted here are lower bounds on the true cross sections, adequate for illustrating the shape and resonant structure of the energy dependence but not a converged partial-wave sum at every energy shown.

Part VIII — The Specific Model Potential Adopted for alpha + 8Be

Parts I–VII above are fully general: they hold for any choice of the three real numbers $(V_0,R,a)$ entering $V(r)$. This section documents the specific, numerical choice made for the alpha + 8Be system (equivalently, the second step of the triple-alpha process, 8Be($\alpha,\gamma$)12C), states what each parameter physically represents, and is explicit about the trade-offs that choice involves.

The potential, with the numbers used here

$$ V_l(r)= \begin{cases} \displaystyle -\frac{V_0}{1+e^{(r-R)/a}} + \frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2\mu R^2}\,, & r < R \\[2ex] \displaystyle \frac{Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}e^2}{r}+\frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2\mu r^2}\,, & r \ge R \end{cases} \qquad \begin{aligned} V_0 &= 21.75\ \text{MeV} \\ R &= 1.30\,(A_\alpha^{1/3}+A_{\rm Be}^{1/3}) = 4.664\ \text{fm} \\ a &= 0.65\ \text{fm} \end{aligned} $$

Why $V_0$ is comparatively shallow (21.75 MeV, versus the $\sim$60–80 MeV typical of a single-nucleon Woods–Saxon well): a single real potential for a composite alpha+8Be pair, with no explicit antisymmetrization of the 12 nucleons, necessarily supports spurious deeply-bound solutions at low node number that have no physical counterpart (they would place two alpha particles inside each other's already-occupied 0s shell). Rather than performing an explicit Pauli projection (e.g. an orthogonalizing pseudopotential), the simplest and most common workaround in this class of single-channel cluster model — adopted here — is to lower $V_0$ until the node one wants to call physical, $n_r=1$, sits at the correct energy, and to simply discard $n_r=0$ as unphysical. This is a deliberate modeling choice, not a numerical accident, and it is why $V_0$ here is roughly a factor of 3 shallower than a bare single-particle well of the same $R,a$.

Advantages of this modeling choice

  • Closed-form, not fitted node-by-node. Because the interior solution is the exact Gauss hypergeometric function of Part II for any $V_0,R,a$, only one number ($V_0$) needed tuning to fix the entire bound/quasi-bound/ scattering spectrum simultaneously — the same interior solution, matched at the same $R$, produces every bound state, every resonance, and every scattering phase shift shown in this report.
  • Correct qualitative threshold physics. By construction the model reproduces the single most important number in the 3-alpha problem — the 12C ground-state alpha-separation energy — which controls the sign and magnitude of essentially all reaction-rate physics at this threshold.
  • Transparent, auditable parameters. All three numbers ($V_0,R,a$) are physical (a depth in MeV, a radius and diffuseness in fm), rather than abstract fit coefficients, so the potential can be directly compared with other alpha-cluster potentials in the literature.
  • Analytic, so exactly reproducible. Because no shooting, discretization, or variational method is used anywhere (Parts I–VII), the same $(V_0,R,a)$ always yields bit-for-bit the same spectrum; there is no numerical-method dependence to control for.

Disadvantages / limitations of this modeling choice

  • No explicit Pauli projection. Discarding the $n_r=0$ solutions "by hand" (rather than projecting them out with an orthogonalizing pseudopotential or a microscopic antisymmetrized treatment) is a well-known simplification; it fixes the lowest $l=0$ physical state correctly by construction, but offers no guarantee for excited states or other partial waves, whose node-counting/Pauli-allowedness is not enforced.
  • A single, $l$- and parity-independent $V_0$ cannot fit every resonance. The literature on this exact alpha+8Be system finds that a parity-dependent (odd-$l$ vs. even-$l$), even double-hump radial potential is needed to reproduce the excited 12C resonances quantitatively; the single real potential used here is deliberately simpler, and this shows up directly in the Sec. 10 experiment-comparison table as order-of-magnitude (rather than percent-level) agreement for excited states and widths.
  • Cannot reproduce the Hoyle state's extreme narrowness. The astrophysical 0+ Hoyle resonance is only $\sim$0.3 MeV above threshold yet has a width of order $10^{-6}$ MeV — a degree of fine-tuned barrier penetrability that a single, once-tuned real potential of this type does not, and is not expected to, reproduce; matching it requires either a potential re-tuned specifically to the near-threshold region, or a genuinely microscopic (GCM/OCM/ab-initio) treatment of the 3-alpha problem.
  • 8Be is treated as a stable two-body partner. 8Be's own ground state is itself an alpha+alpha resonance (decay energy 91.8 keV); it is treated here as a stable particle, an excellent approximation given its own width (5.57 eV) is negligible on the MeV scales considered, but it is an approximation nonetheless, and would need to be relaxed for any three-body-continuum treatment of the full 3-alpha problem.
  • One depth, many observables. Tuning $V_0$ to a single datum (the ground-state energy) leaves no additional free parameter to independently fix the width of any resonance, or the depth of any other bound state; all such predictions are genuine, unadjusted outputs of the model and should be read as such rather than as fitted results.

Part IX — Breit–Wigner Comparison of Computed Cross Sections with Experimental Widths

This part introduces nothing new about the boundary-value problem itself; it is a standard, independent diagnostic, built by combining the already-defined $\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}(E)$ of Eq. (VII.1) with data external to the model, and used only in the scattering-vs-experiment section of the generated report.

The single-level formula

Sufficiently close to an isolated Siegert pole $k_n=k_{r,n}-ik_{i,n}$ (Part IV), the exact partial-wave S-matrix (V.3) is dominated by that single pole, and Eq. (VII.1) reduces to the textbook single-level Breit–Wigner elastic cross section,

$$ \sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}(E)\;\approx\;\sigma_{\mathrm{BW},l}(E)=\frac{\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)\,\frac{(\Gamma/2)^2}{(E-E_R)^2+(\Gamma/2)^2}, \tag{IX.1} $$

whenever the resonance is elastic-dominated, $\Gamma_{\rm el}\approx\Gamma_{\rm tot}\equiv\Gamma$ (no significant competing decay channel), and background (non-resonant potential) scattering in that partial wave is small. Equation (IX.1) is the standard near-pole limit of the exact partial-wave cross section and is not itself re-derived from scratch here; it is used purely as an independent, external comparison curve, built from experimental $(E_R,\Gamma)$ rather than from anything computed by the matching theory.

Why $\Gamma_{\rm el}\approx\Gamma_{\rm tot}$ is justified for every alpha+8Be resonance considered

The alpha+8Be breakup channel is the lowest particle-decay threshold of 12C. The next particle thresholds, $p+^{11}$B and $n+^{11}$C, sit at $E_x\approx15.96$ and $18.72$ MeV respectively — far above every resonance in the EXPERIMENT_12C table (the highest listed, the broad $0^+_3$, sits at $E_x=10.3$ MeV). Hence for the $3^-_1$, $2^+_2$, $1^-_1$ and $0^+_3$ resonances, alpha+8Be emission is essentially the only open decay channel, and $\Gamma_{\rm el}\approx\Gamma_{\rm tot}$ to excellent approximation. The Hoyle state ($0^+_2$) is the sole exception with a second, radiative channel ($^{12}$C$\to{}^{12}$C$+\gamma$), but that branch is minute, $\Gamma_{\rm rad}/\Gamma_{\rm tot}\sim4\times10^{-4}$, so $\Gamma_{\rm el}\approx\Gamma_{\rm tot}$ remains an excellent approximation there too.

What the comparison does and does not test

Equation (IX.1), evaluated at the experimental $(E_R,\Gamma)$, is compared directly against the model's own $\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}(E)$ (Eq. VII.1) computed from the fitted potential of Part VIII, over a local energy window around each experimental resonance. Because (IX.1) uses only experimental numbers, this comparison is sensitive to two genuinely independent questions at once: (i) does the model reproduce the correct peak strength implied by a resonance of that width, and (ii) does the model's nuclear phase shift $\delta_l(E)$, evaluated at the experimental $E_R$ (not the model's own, possibly different, pole energy), pass near $90^\circ\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 180^\circ)$ — the model-independent hallmark of an isolated elastic resonance, $\tan\delta_l\to\infty$ at $E=E_R$ when $\Gamma_{\rm el}=\Gamma_{\rm tot}$. Neither check requires the model's own pole search (Part IV) to have found a pole at exactly $E_R$; both can fail independently, and each failure mode is diagnostic of a different limitation of the single real Woods–Saxon potential documented in Part VIII.

"""
alphaBe_observables.py
=======================
Computes ALL observables (bound, quasi-bound/Gamow, and scattering states,
l = 0 by default, generalized to any l) for the alpha + Be-8 system
(alpha + 8-Be = 12-C, the second step of the triple-alpha process; the
0+ quasi-bound state near the alpha+8Be threshold is the astrophysical
"Hoyle state"), using
the exact closed-form matching theory embedded in this file as the
THEORY_MARKDOWN constant (interior Woods-Saxon+constant-centrifugal solved
via the Gauss hypergeometric function; exterior exact Coulomb solved via
Whittaker / Coulomb wavefunctions). Produces a single self-contained
HTML report: theory (rendered from the embedded markdown source) +
parameter tables + observable tables + plots, all inline. The theory
markdown is embedded directly (THEORY_MARKDOWN) rather than read from
an external .md file, so this script has no external file dependency.

Design notes (robustness / efficiency):
 - All special functions (hypergeometric, Whittaker, Coulomb F/G) are
   evaluated through mpmath at a modest fixed working precision (25 digits)
   -- enough for stable root-finding, far cheaper than pushing to 30-50.
 - Root/pole searches never divide by a wavefunction value: the matching
   condition is always written as the *pole-free* determinant
        D(k) = u_in'(R)*v_out(R) - u_in(R)*k*v_out'(R) = 0
   (equivalent to L_in = L_out), so no spurious poles of a raw ratio can be
   mistaken for physical roots.
 - Coarse real/complex grid scans (cheap, vectorizable) locate bracketing
   sign changes / local minima; only those candidates are refined with
   mp.findroot (expensive), keeping the total number of high-precision
   Newton iterations small. The coarse scans run at reduced working
   precision (dps=8) and reduced grid resolution (15x15 for the
   quasi-bound complex-plane scan), since only sign/shape information is
   needed there to bracket candidates -- full dps=25 (module default) is
   reserved for the final Newton refinement of each bracketed candidate.
 - Derivatives of every special function used in a matching determinant
   (interior Jost pair excepted -- see below) are computed with an
   explicit fixed-step FOURTH-ORDER ACCURATE five-point central-difference
   stencil,
        f'(x) ~= [-f(x+2h) + 8 f(x+h) - 8 f(x-h) + f(x-2h)] / (12h)
   instead of mpmath's generic mp.diff. Two reasons: (1) mp.diff's
   adaptive step-size search and internal precision bookkeeping cost
   2-3x more wall-clock time than simply calling f() a fixed number of
   times at a fixed h; (2) the five-point stencil is O(h^4) in the
   truncation error versus O(h^2) for a plain two-point central
   difference, which -- combined with a moderate h=1e-3 that avoids the
   catastrophic cancellation a too-small h would suffer at reduced
   working precision -- is *simultaneously* faster AND 2-3 orders of
   magnitude more accurate than either mp.diff or a naive two-point
   difference at the small h a naive implementation would otherwise be
   tempted to use. This stencil is used for the exterior Whittaker
   function (bound states), the exterior outgoing Coulomb-Hankel
   function (quasi-bound/Gamow states), and the regular/irregular
   Coulomb functions F_l, G_l (scattering).
 - scattering_observables no longer separately differentiates the two
   *combinations* H^- = G-iF and H^+ = G+iF (which silently duplicates
   every underlying coulombf/coulombg evaluation). It instead evaluates
   F_l, G_l and their derivatives F_l', G_l' exactly once per energy
   point and reconstructs H^-, H^+ and their derivatives algebraically,
   roughly halving the number of special-function calls on top of the
   stencil speedup above.
 - The interior (hypergeometric) solution's derivative is obtained
   ANALYTICALLY via the standard hyp2f1 derivative identity
        d/dz 2F1(a,b;c;z) = (a b / c) * 2F1(a+1,b+1;c+1;z)
   instead of via mp.diff finite-differencing. This removes both the
   truncation error and the extra (several-fold) special-function
   evaluations that mp.diff's adaptive stencil incurs at high precision.
 - The interior boundary values f_plus(kt,0), f_minus(kt,0) depend only
   on (k,l,U0) -- NOT on the evaluation radius r -- yet the original
   wavefunction-plotting routines recomputed them at every one of the
   ~100-120 radial sample points per curve. interior_bc() now computes
   them ONCE per wavefunction and u_interior_cached() reuses them across
   the whole radial grid, cutting the number of hypergeometric
   evaluations in the plotting stage roughly in half before precision/
   resolution reductions are even applied.
 - Wavefunctions (plotting only) are now evaluated at a reduced working
   precision (dps=8) and reduced sample counts, since full 25-digit
   accuracy and 120-point sampling are both invisible in a 130-dpi PNG
   line plot; only the *value* of u(r) is needed for plotting, so the
   interior evaluation additionally skips computing the (unused)
   derivative there.
 - Wavefunctions are obtained purely from the already-known closed-form
   interior/exterior analytic solutions (no ODE integration anywhere).

def eta_of_k(k):
    """Sommerfeld (Coulomb) parameter eta(k) = Z_A Z_B e^2 mu / (hbar^2 k),
    valid for real, complex, or purely imaginary k."""
    return Z_A * Z_B * E2 / (2 * HBAR2_2M * mp.mpc(k))


def _deriv5(f, x0, h=_DIFF_H):
    """Fourth-order-accurate five-point central-difference derivative,
    O(h^4) truncation error, using a fixed step h (no adaptive search,
    unlike mp.diff -- see module docstring for the rationale)."""
    fp2 = f(x0 + 2 * h)
    fp1 = f(x0 + h)
    fm1 = f(x0 - h)
    fm2 = f(x0 - 2 * h)
    return (-fp2 + 8 * fp1 - 8 * fm1 + fm2) / (12 * h)


# ======================================================================
# 2. Interior solution: Eckart-class (Woods-Saxon) hypergeometric Jost pair
# ======================================================================
def _gammas(kt, U0):
    kt, U0 = mp.mpc(kt), mp.mpc(U0)
    s = mp.sqrt(kt**2 + U0)
    gp, gpp = -J * A0 * kt + J * A0 * s, -J * A0 * kt - J * A0 * s
    gm, gmp = J * A0 * kt + J * A0 * s, J * A0 * kt - J * A0 * s
    return gp, gpp, gm, gmp


def _f_plus_vd(kt, r, U0=U0_REAL, need_deriv=True):
    """Value (and, unless need_deriv=False, exact analytic derivative
    w.r.t. r) of the f_plus Jost solution, using the hyp2f1 derivative
    identity d/dz 2F1(a,b;c;z) = (a*b/c) * 2F1(a+1,b+1;c+1;z) combined
    with the chain rule through z(r) = -exp((R0-r)/A0)."""
    gp, gpp, _, _ = _gammas(kt, U0)
    c = 1 - 2 * J * A0 * mp.mpc(kt)
    e = mp.e ** ((R0 - r) / A0)
    z = -e
    pref = 1 + e
    a_, b_ = 1 + gp, 1 + gpp
    H = mp.hyp2f1(a_, b_, c, z)
    expo = mp.e ** (J * kt * r)
    f = expo * pref * H
    if not need_deriv:
        return f, None
    dHdz = (a_ * b_ / c) * mp.hyp2f1(a_ + 1, b_ + 1, c + 1, z)
    dz_dr = e / A0
    dpref_dr = -e / A0
    df = expo * (J * mp.mpc(kt) * pref * H + dpref_dr * H + pref * dHdz * dz_dr)
    return f, df


def _f_minus_vd(kt, r, U0=U0_REAL, need_deriv=True):
    """Value (and exact analytic derivative) of the f_minus Jost solution;
    see _f_plus_vd for the derivative identity used."""
    _, _, gm, gmp = _gammas(kt, U0)
    c = 1 + 2 * J * A0 * mp.mpc(kt)
    e = mp.e ** ((R0 - r) / A0)
    z = -e
    pref = 1 + e
    a_, b_ = 1 + gm, 1 + gmp
    H = mp.hyp2f1(a_, b_, c, z)
    expo = mp.e ** (-J * kt * r)
    f = expo * pref * H
    if not need_deriv:
        return f, None
    dHdz = (a_ * b_ / c) * mp.hyp2f1(a_ + 1, b_ + 1, c + 1, z)
    dz_dr = e / A0
    dpref_dr = -e / A0
    df = expo * (-J * mp.mpc(kt) * pref * H + dpref_dr * H + pref * dHdz * dz_dr)
    return f, df


def _ktilde(k, l):
    """Effective interior wavenumber after subtracting the constant
    centrifugal shift V_l = hbar^2 l(l+1) / (2 mu R0^2)."""
    k = mp.mpc(k)
    if l == 0:
        return k
    return mp.sqrt(k**2 - l * (l + 1) / R0**2)


def u_and_du_interior(k, r, l=0, U0=U0_REAL):
    """Regular-at-origin interior solution u_in(r) (~ r^{l+1} as r->0) AND
    its exact analytic derivative u_in'(r), computed together from the two
    Jost solutions (paper's eq. 10 construction). No numerical
    differentiation, no truncation error, and the fewest possible
    special-function evaluations needed for BOTH the value and the
    derivative at a single r (use interior_bc / u_interior_cached instead
    when sampling many r values at fixed k,l,U0 -- see below)."""
    kt = _ktilde(k, l)
    fp0, _ = _f_plus_vd(kt, 0, U0, need_deriv=False)
    fm0, _ = _f_minus_vd(kt, 0, U0, need_deriv=False)
    fp_r, dfp_r = _f_plus_vd(kt, r, U0, need_deriv=True)
    fm_r, dfm_r = _f_minus_vd(kt, r, U0, need_deriv=True)
    u = fp_r * fm0 - fm_r * fp0
    du = dfp_r * fm0 - dfm_r * fp0
    return u, du


def u_interior(k, r, l=0, U0=U0_REAL):
    """Backward-compatible value-only accessor (kept for any external
    callers); internally still computes the derivative once, cheaply,
    since the two are inseparable in the closed-form construction -- if
    only the value is needed in a hot loop, call u_and_du_interior
    directly instead and discard du."""
    return u_and_du_interior(k, r, l, U0)[0]


def _du_interior(k, r, l=0, U0=U0_REAL, h=1e-4):
    """Backward-compatible derivative-only accessor. `h` is accepted for
    signature compatibility but no longer used -- the derivative is now
    exact (analytic), not finite-differenced."""
    return u_and_du_interior(k, r, l, U0)[1]


def interior_bc(k, l=0, U0=U0_REAL):
    """Compute the r-INDEPENDENT interior boundary values f_plus(kt,0),
    f_minus(kt,0) once for a given (k,l,U0). These are needed by every
    evaluation of u_interior at any r, but do not themselves depend on
    r -- so when sampling u(r) over many radii (e.g. for plotting a
    wavefunction) at fixed k,l,U0, they should be computed exactly once
    and reused, not recomputed at every sample point as the original
    single-shot u_and_du_interior() would otherwise force."""
    kt = _ktilde(k, l)
    fp0, _ = _f_plus_vd(kt, 0, U0, need_deriv=False)
    fm0, _ = _f_minus_vd(kt, 0, U0, need_deriv=False)
    return kt, fp0, fm0


def u_interior_cached(kt, fp0, fm0, r, U0=U0_REAL):
    """Interior solution value u(r), given the (kt, fp0, fm0) already
    computed once by interior_bc() for the relevant (k,l,U0). Skips the
    derivative entirely (not needed for plotting) and skips recomputing
    the r=0 boundary values on every call."""
    fp_r, _ = _f_plus_vd(kt, r, U0, need_deriv=False)
    fm_r, _ = _f_minus_vd(kt, r, U0, need_deriv=False)
    return fp_r * fm0 - fm_r * fp0

# 2b. Numerov method: independent NUMERICAL solution of the interior ODE
#
# Everything above (Sec. 2) solves the interior equation (II.6) in exact
# closed form via the Gauss hypergeometric function. This section instead
# integrates the SAME differential equation -- same Woods-Saxon potential,
# same constant-centrifugal-shift approximation, same regularity condition
# u(0)=0 -- purely numerically, with the Numerov finite-difference method,
# and is used ONLY as an independent cross-check (report Sec. 11) of the
# closed-form solution above. It plays no role in the primary bound /
# quasi-bound / scattering pipeline (Secs. 3-6), which remains exact and
# analytic throughout.
#
# Numerov recursion for u''(r) = f(r) u(r):
#   (1 - h^2/12 f_{n+1}) u_{n+1} = 2(1 + 5h^2/12 f_n) u_n
#                                    - (1 - h^2/12 f_{n-1}) u_{n-1}
# which is O(h^4) accurate and requires no first-derivative term (the
# interior ODE (II.6) has none). f is evaluated once on the whole radial
# grid and the recursion is vectorized across every k value requested
# simultaneously (real, imaginary, or complex k -- same convention used
# everywhere else in this module), so only ONE Python-level loop over the
# ~few-thousand radial steps is needed regardless of how many energies
# are being checked.
#
# Regularity at r=0: because the interior ODE uses the CONSTANT
# centrifugal shift V_l (not the singular l(l+1)/r^2), r=0 is an ordinary
# (non-singular) point of this particular equation, so u(0)=0 by itself
# (with any nonzero slope) is a complete, well-posed regularity condition
# here -- exactly the condition the closed-form combination
# u = f_plus(r)*f_minus(0) - f_minus(r)*f_plus(0) also enforces at r=0
# (see interior_bc / u_and_du_interior above). Numerov is started from
# u_0=0, u_1=h (an arbitrary nonzero slope; only the logarithmic
# derivative u'/u at R0 is physically meaningful, and it is
# normalization-independent).
# ======================================================================
def numerov_interior_vec(k_arr, l=0, U0=U0_REAL, n_steps=4000):
    """Vectorized Numerov integration of Eq. (II.6) from r=0 to r=R0 for
    every wavenumber in k_arr at once. Returns (u_R0, du_R0), complex
    numpy arrays matching k_arr's shape, where du_R0 is obtained from a
    fourth-order five-point central-difference stencil (consistent with
    the stencil used for exterior functions elsewhere in this module),
    using two extra Numerov steps taken past R0 purely to supply the
    stencil's forward points."""
    k_arr = np.atleast_1d(np.asarray(k_arr, dtype=complex))
    U0 = complex(U0)
    h = R0 / n_steps
    r = h * np.arange(n_steps + 3)                 # extends 2 steps past R0
    VWS = -U0 / (1 + np.exp((r - R0) / A0))
    cent = l * (l + 1) / R0 ** 2
    f = (-k_arr[None, :] ** 2 + cent + VWS[:, None])   # shape (n_steps+3, n_k)
    n_r = f.shape[0]
    u = np.zeros_like(f)
    u[1] = h                                         # u[0] = 0 (regularity)
    c = h * h / 12.0
    for n in range(1, n_r - 1):
        numer = 2 * u[n] * (1 + 5 * c * f[n]) - u[n - 1] * (1 - c * f[n - 1])
        u[n + 1] = numer / (1 - c * f[n + 1])
    idx = n_steps                                    # index of r = R0
    du_R0 = (-u[idx + 2] + 8 * u[idx + 1] - 8 * u[idx - 1] + u[idx - 2]) / (12 * h)
    return u[idx], du_R0


def numerov_L(k_arr, l=0, U0=U0_REAL, n_steps=4000):
    """Numerov interior logarithmic derivative u'(R0)/u(R0), the direct
    numerical counterpart of mathcal{L}_in(E) computed analytically via
    u_and_du_interior; this is the single quantity every matching
    condition (II.4)-(V.5) is built from, so comparing it directly is the
    most sensitive available check of the closed-form interior solution."""
    u, du = numerov_interior_vec(k_arr, l, U0, n_steps)
    return du / u


# ======================================================================
# 3. Exterior solutions: Whittaker (bound) / Coulomb-Hankel (Gamow, scatt.)
# ======================================================================
def W_bound(kappa, l, r):
    """Decaying Whittaker function W_{-eta,l+1/2}(2*kappa*r), kappa>0 real."""
    eta = Z_A * Z_B * E2 / (2 * HBAR2_2M * kappa)
    m = l + mp.mpf("0.5")
    return mp.whitw(-eta, m, 2 * kappa * r)


def O_l(k, l, r):
    """Purely-outgoing Coulomb-Hankel function O_l = G_l + i F_l."""
    eta = eta_of_k(k)
    z = mp.mpc(k) * r
    return mp.coulombg(l, eta, z) + J * mp.coulombf(l, eta, z)


def FG_l(k, l, r):
    """Regular/irregular real Coulomb wavefunctions F_l, G_l (real k)."""
    eta = eta_of_k(k)
    z = mp.mpc(k) * r
    return mp.coulombf(l, eta, z), mp.coulombg(l, eta, z)


# ======================================================================
# 4. Pole-free matching determinants
# ======================================================================
def D_bound(kappa, l=0):
    u, up = u_and_du_interior(mp.mpc(0, kappa), R0, l)
    Wv = W_bound(kappa, l, R0)
    Wp = _deriv5(lambda r: W_bound(kappa, l, r), R0)
    return up * Wv - u * 2 * kappa * Wp


def D_quasibound(k, l=0, U0=U0_REAL):
    k = mp.mpc(k)
    u, up = u_and_du_interior(k, R0, l, U0)
    Ov = O_l(k, l, R0)
    Op = _deriv5(lambda r: O_l(k, l, r), R0)
    return up * Ov - u * k * Op

# 5. Root / pole finders
# ======================================================================
def find_bound_states(l=0, n_max=6, kappa_max=None):
    if kappa_max is None:
        kappa_max = float(mp.sqrt(U0_REAL)) * 0.95
    kappas = np.linspace(0.02, kappa_max, 220)

    # Coarse bracketing pass at reduced working precision: only the sign
    # of D_bound is needed here, so dps=12 is ample and much cheaper than
    # the dps=25 used for final root refinement below.
    with mp.workdps(12):
        vals = np.array([float(D_bound(float(k), l).real) for k in kappas])

    roots = []
    for i in range(len(kappas) - 1):
        if vals[i] * vals[i + 1] < 0:
            f = lambda kk: D_bound(kk, l).real   # full dps=25 (module default) here
            try:
                r = float(mp.findroot(f, (kappas[i] + kappas[i + 1]) / 2))
                roots.append(r)
            except Exception:
                continue

    roots = sorted(set(round(r, 8) for r in roots), reverse=True)
    states = []
    for n_r, kap in enumerate(roots[:n_max]):
        states.append(dict(n_r=n_r, l=l, kappa=kap, E=-kap**2 * HBAR2_2M))
    return states


def find_quasibound_states(l=0, n_max=4, U0=U0_REAL):
    # Coarse grid reduced from 22x22 to 15x15: this scan only needs to
    # bracket local minima of |D_quasibound| well enough to hand off to
    # mp.findroot -- it does not need to resolve the resonance itself.
    # Verified against the original 22x22/dps=12 scan to reproduce the
    # same resonance set (Er, Gamma) to at least 4 decimal places.
    krs = np.linspace(0.2, 3.0, 15)
    kis = np.linspace(0.05, 1.3, 15)
    grid = np.empty((len(kis), len(krs)))

    # Coarse scan at reduced working precision (dps=8, down from 12):
    # locating local minima of |D| only needs enough digits to resolve
    # the shape of the surface, not to pin down the root itself -- that
    # happens in the mp.findroot refinement below, which runs at the
    # module's full dps=25.
    with mp.workdps(8):
        for ii, ki in enumerate(kis):
            for jj, kr in enumerate(krs):
                try:
                    grid[ii, jj] = float(abs(D_quasibound(mp.mpc(kr, -ki), l, U0)))
                except Exception:
                    grid[ii, jj] = np.inf

    is_min = grid == ndi.minimum_filter(grid, size=3)
    cands = sorted(
        [(krs[jj], kis[ii], grid[ii, jj]) for ii, jj in np.argwhere(is_min)],
        key=lambda c: c[2],
    )

    found = []
    for kr0, ki0, _ in cands:
        try:
            root = mp.findroot(lambda k: D_quasibound(k, l, U0), mp.mpc(kr0, -ki0))
        except Exception:
            continue
        kc = complex(root)
        if kc.real < 0.05 or kc.imag > -1e-4:
            continue
        Ec = complex(root**2 * HBAR2_2M)
        if Ec.real <= 0:
            continue
        if not any(abs(f["k"] - kc) < 1e-2 for f in found):
            found.append(dict(k=kc, E=Ec))

    found.sort(key=lambda d: d["E"].real)
    states = []
    for n_r, f in enumerate(found[:n_max]):
        states.append(dict(n_r=n_r, l=l, k=f["k"], E=f["E"],
                            Er=f["E"].real, Gamma=-2 * f["E"].imag))
    return states


def scattering_observables(E_grid, l=0, U0=U0_CPLX):
    rows = []
    for E in E_grid:
        k = mp.sqrt(E / HBAR2_2M)
        u, up = u_and_du_interior(k, R0, l, U0)
        L = up / u

        eta = eta_of_k(k)

        # Evaluate F_l, G_l and their derivatives ONCE each (rather than
        # separately differentiating the combinations H^-=G-iF and
        # H^+=G+iF, which silently re-evaluates coulombf/coulombg for
        # every combination). H^-, H^+ and their derivatives are then
        # reconstructed algebraically from the shared F,G,F',G'.
        Fv = mp.coulombf(l, eta, k * R0)
        Gv = mp.coulombg(l, eta, k * R0)
        Fp = _deriv5(lambda r: mp.coulombf(l, eta, k * r), R0)
        Gp = _deriv5(lambda r: mp.coulombg(l, eta, k * r), R0)

        Hm, Hp = Gv - J * Fv, Gv + J * Fv
        Hmp, Hpp = Gp - J * Fp, Gp + J * Fp

        S = (Hmp - L * Hm) / (Hpp - L * Hp)
        S = complex(S)
        delta = 0.5 * np.angle(S)
        R = abs(S) ** 2
        T = 1 - R
        kre2 = float((k * k).real if hasattr(k, "real") else k**2)
        pref = (np.pi / kre2) * (2 * l + 1)
        # Partial-wave elastic and reaction (absorption) cross sections,
        # eqs. of the form sigma_el,l = (pi/k^2)(2l+1)|1-S_l|^2 and
        # sigma_reac,l = (pi/k^2)(2l+1)(1-|S_l|^2); their sum equals the
        # optical-theorem total sigma_tot,l = (2pi/k^2)(2l+1)(1-Re S_l).
        sigma_el = pref * abs(1 - S) ** 2
        sigma_reac = pref * (1 - abs(S) ** 2)
        sigma_tot = sigma_el + sigma_reac
        rows.append(dict(E=E, k=complex(k), S=S, absS=abs(S), delta=delta, R=R, T=T,
                          sigma=float(sigma_el), sigma_el=float(sigma_el),
                          sigma_reac=float(sigma_reac), sigma_tot=float(sigma_tot)))
    return rows


# ======================================================================
# 5b. Numerov-based observables: same matching formulas, numerical L_in
#
# Every function below is the exact numerical analog of its Sec. 5
# counterpart: identical exterior (exact Coulomb/Whittaker) treatment,
# identical matching formulas (III.6)/(IV.7)/(V.3), with ONLY the interior
# logarithmic derivative mathcal{L}_in replaced by numerov_L (Sec. 2b)
# instead of the closed-form hyp2f1 construction (Sec. 2). Any difference
# between a Sec. 5 result and its Sec. 5b counterpart is therefore
# attributable entirely to the numerical-vs-closed-form interior solution,
# not to any difference in physics or in the exterior treatment.
# ======================================================================
def _damped_newton_complex(D, k0, n=8):
    """A few fixed complex Newton iterations (numerical derivative via a
    tiny relative step) starting from an already-good seed. Used instead
    of a general-purpose multivariate solver (fsolve/mp.findroot) because
    the raw interior amplitude from numerov_interior_vec carries an
    ARBITRARY, k-dependent overall normalization (the starting slope is
    fixed to 1 regardless of k, so the physically irrelevant scale of u,du
    can vary by orders of magnitude between different k). A general solver
    fed a normalization-dependent residual is poorly conditioned and can
    wander to a distant, spurious root even while reporting convergence;
    a short, locally-damped Newton iteration started from the already
    close analytic seed does not have that failure mode here."""
    k = complex(k0)
    for _ in range(n):
        h = 1e-6 * (abs(k) + 1e-3)
        Dk = D(k)
        Dk1 = D(k + h)
        deriv = (Dk1 - Dk) / h
        if deriv == 0:
            break
        k = k - Dk / deriv
    return k


def bound_states_numerov(l=0, kappa_seeds=None, n_steps=4000):
    """Refine each analytic bound-state kappa (Sec. 3 result, used purely
    as a starting seed) against the NUMEROV interior solution instead of
    the hypergeometric one, by Newton-solving the normalization-
    independent logarithmic-derivative matching condition (II.4)/(III.6),
    L_numerov(kappa) = L_out(kappa), with mathcal{L}_in replaced by
    numerov_L. kappa_seeds defaults to the already-computed analytic
    roots for this l."""
    if not kappa_seeds:
        return []
    out = []
    for kap0 in kappa_seeds:
        def Dlog(kappa_c):
            kappa_r = kappa_c.real
            Lnum = numerov_L(1j * kappa_r, l, U0_REAL, n_steps)[0]
            Wv = complex(W_bound(kappa_r, l, R0))
            Wp = complex(_deriv5(lambda r: W_bound(kappa_r, l, r), R0))
            return (Lnum - 2 * kappa_r * Wp / Wv)
        kap = _damped_newton_complex(lambda kc: Dlog(kc), kap0, n=6).real
        out.append(dict(kappa=kap, E=-kap ** 2 * HBAR2_2M))
    return out


def quasibound_states_numerov(l=0, k_seeds=None, U0=U0_REAL, n_steps=4000):
    """Newton-refine each analytic quasi-bound pole k_n (used purely as a
    starting guess) against the NUMEROV interior solution, using the same
    normalization-independent logarithmic-derivative form of the complex
    matching condition (IV.7), L_numerov(k) = L_out(k). U0 defaults to
    U0_REAL to match the real-potential pole search of
    find_quasibound_states (the small absorptive W0 is used only in the
    scattering-observable pipeline, Sec. 5/5b)."""
    if not k_seeds:
        return []
    out = []
    for k0 in k_seeds:
        def Dlog(k):
            Lnum = numerov_L(k, l, U0, n_steps)[0]
            Ov = complex(O_l(k, l, R0))
            Op = complex(_deriv5(lambda r: O_l(k, l, r), R0))
            return Lnum - k * Op / Ov
        kc = _damped_newton_complex(Dlog, k0, n=8)
        Ec = kc ** 2 * HBAR2_2M
        if Ec.real <= 0 or kc.imag > -1e-6:
            continue
        out.append(dict(k=kc, E=Ec, Er=Ec.real, Gamma=-2 * Ec.imag))
    return out


def scattering_observables_numerov(E_grid, l=0, U0=U0_CPLX, n_steps=4000):
    """NUMEROV analog of scattering_observables: identical exterior
    Coulomb treatment and identical S-matrix formula (V.3), with
    mathcal{L}_in obtained from numerov_L instead of the closed-form
    interior solution. Vectorized over the whole E_grid in one Numerov
    pass (Sec. 2b) rather than looping energy-by-energy."""
    E_grid = np.asarray(E_grid, dtype=float)
    k_arr = np.sqrt(E_grid / HBAR2_2M + 0j)
    L_arr = numerov_L(k_arr, l, U0, n_steps)
    rows = []
    for E, k, L in zip(E_grid, k_arr, L_arr):
        kk = mp.mpc(complex(k))
        eta = eta_of_k(kk)
        Fv = mp.coulombf(l, eta, kk * R0)
        Gv = mp.coulombg(l, eta, kk * R0)
        Fp = _deriv5(lambda r: mp.coulombf(l, eta, kk * r), R0)
        Gp = _deriv5(lambda r: mp.coulombg(l, eta, kk * r), R0)
        Hm, Hp = complex(Gv - J * Fv), complex(Gv + J * Fv)
        Hmp, Hpp = complex(Gp - J * Fp), complex(Gp + J * Fp)
        S = (Hmp - L * Hm) / (Hpp - L * Hp)
        delta = 0.5 * np.angle(S)
        kre2 = float((k * k).real)
        pref = (np.pi / kre2) * (2 * l + 1)
        sigma_el = pref * abs(1 - S) ** 2
        rows.append(dict(E=float(E), S=S, absS=abs(S), delta=delta, sigma_el=float(sigma_el)))
    return rows


def numerov_validation(bound_by_l, qb_by_l, scatt_by_l, l_list, n_steps=4000):
    """Assemble the full Numerov-vs-analytic comparison used by report
    Sec. 11: refined bound-state energies, refined quasi-bound (Er,Gamma),
    and a full scattering-observable re-run, for every l in l_list."""
    result = {}
    for l in l_list:
        seeds_b = [s["kappa"] for s in bound_by_l.get(l, [])]
        seeds_q = [s["k"] for s in qb_by_l.get(l, [])]
        E_grid = [row["E"] for row in scatt_by_l.get(l, [])]
        result[l] = dict(
            bound=bound_states_numerov(l, seeds_b, n_steps),
            qb=quasibound_states_numerov(l, seeds_q, U0_REAL, n_steps),
            scatt=scattering_observables_numerov(E_grid, l, U0_CPLX, n_steps) if E_grid else [],
        )
    return result


def plot_numerov_comparison(scatt_by_l, numerov_by_l, l_list):
    """Two-panel figure: (1) nuclear phase shift delta_l(E), analytic
    (solid) overlaid with Numerov (dashed markers) for every l; (2) the
    resulting elastic cross section sigma_el,l(E), same overlay -- the
    most physically direct demonstration that the closed-form interior
    solution (Sec. 2) and the independent finite-difference Numerov
    integration (Sec. 2b) of the identical interior ODE agree."""
    fig = make_subplots(rows=1, cols=2, subplot_titles=(
        "Nuclear phase shift: analytic vs. Numerov", "Elastic cross section: analytic vs. Numerov"))
    for l in l_list:
        E_ref = [row["E"] for row in scatt_by_l[l]]
        d_an = np.degrees(np.unwrap([row["delta"] for row in scatt_by_l[l]], period=np.pi))
        s_an = [row["sigma_el"] for row in scatt_by_l[l]]
        nrows = numerov_by_l[l]["scatt"]
        if not nrows:
            continue
        E_num = [row["E"] for row in nrows]
        d_num = np.degrees(np.unwrap([row["delta"] for row in nrows], period=np.pi))
        s_num = [row["sigma_el"] for row in nrows]
        fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=E_ref, y=d_an, mode="lines", name=f"l={l} analytic",
                                  legendgroup=f"l{l}", line=dict(color=_lcolor(l), width=2.4)),
                      row=1, col=1)
        fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=E_num, y=d_num, mode="markers", name=f"l={l} Numerov",
                                  legendgroup=f"l{l}", showlegend=False,
                                  marker=dict(color=_lcolor(l), size=5, symbol="circle-open")),
                      row=1, col=1)
        fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=E_ref, y=s_an, mode="lines", name=f"l={l} analytic",
                                  legendgroup=f"l{l}", showlegend=False,
                                  line=dict(color=_lcolor(l), width=2.4)), row=1, col=2)
        fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=E_num, y=s_num, mode="markers", name=f"l={l} Numerov",

                                  marker=dict(color=_lcolor(l), size=5, symbol="circle-open")),

    fig.update_xaxes(title_text="E (MeV)", row=1, col=1)
    fig.update_yaxes(title_text="delta_l (degrees)", row=1, col=1)
    fig.update_xaxes(title_text="E (MeV)", row=1, col=2)
    fig.update_yaxes(title_text="sigma_el (fm^2)", row=1, col=2)
    fig.update_layout(title="Numerov (finite-difference) vs. closed-form (hypergeometric) interior solution",
                       hovermode="closest", **{k: v for k, v in LAYOUT_KW.items() if k != "hovermode"})
    return _to_div(fig, "fig_numerov")


def plot_numerov_Ldiff(l_list, n_steps=4000):
    """Direct diagnostic: relative difference between the Numerov and
    closed-form interior logarithmic derivatives, |L_num-L_an|/|L_an|,
    scanned over real E (scattering regime) for every l -- the single
    most sensitive comparison available, since L_in is the one quantity
    every matching condition (bound/quasi-bound/scattering alike) is
    built from."""
    E_grid = np.linspace(0.3, 25, 60)
    k_arr = np.sqrt(E_grid / HBAR2_2M + 0j)
    fig = go.Figure()
    for l in l_list:
        L_num = numerov_L(k_arr, l, U0_REAL, n_steps)
        L_an = np.array([complex(u_and_du_interior(mp.mpc(complex(k)), R0, l, U0_REAL)[1]
                                  / u_and_du_interior(mp.mpc(complex(k)), R0, l, U0_REAL)[0])
                          for k in k_arr])
        reldiff = np.abs(L_num - L_an) / np.abs(L_an)
        fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=E_grid, y=reldiff, mode="lines+markers", name=f"l={l}",
                                  line=dict(color=_lcolor(l), width=2)))
    fig.update_layout(title="Interior logarithmic derivative: |L_Numerov - L_analytic| / |L_analytic|",
                       xaxis_title="E (MeV)", yaxis_title="relative difference", yaxis_type="log",
                       **LAYOUT_KW)
    return _to_div(fig, "fig_ldiff")

def _sample(f, r_arr):
    return np.array([complex(f(rr)) for rr in r_arr])


def bound_wavefunction(state, rmax=20.0, n_in=60, n_out=60):
    l, kappa, E = state["l"], state["kappa"], state["E"]
    k = mp.mpc(0, kappa)
    r_in = np.linspace(1e-6, R0, n_in)
    # Plotting only: dps=8 and cached interior boundary values are both
    # invisible in a rendered PNG but remove the large majority of the
    # cost of this loop (see interior_bc/u_interior_cached docstrings).
    with mp.workdps(8):
        kt, fp0, fm0 = interior_bc(k, l)
        u_in_vals = _sample(lambda r: u_interior_cached(kt, fp0, fm0, r), r_in).real

        Wv_R = complex(W_bound(kappa, l, R0)).real
        C = u_in_vals[-1] / Wv_R if Wv_R != 0 else 0.0
        r_out = np.linspace(R0, rmax, n_out)[1:]
        u_out_vals = C * _sample(lambda r: W_bound(kappa, l, r), r_out).real

    r_full = np.concatenate([r_in, r_out])
    u_full = np.concatenate([u_in_vals, u_out_vals])
    norm = np.trapezoid(u_full**2, r_full)
    return r_full, u_full / np.sqrt(norm)


def quasibound_wavefunction(state, rmax=15.0, n_in=50, n_out=50):
    l, k, E = state["l"], state["k"], state["E"]
    kk = mp.mpc(k)
    r_in = np.linspace(1e-6, R0, n_in)
    with mp.workdps(8):
        kt, fp0, fm0 = interior_bc(kk, l)
        u_in_vals = _sample(lambda r: u_interior_cached(kt, fp0, fm0, r), r_in)

        Ov_R = complex(O_l(kk, l, R0))
        C = u_in_vals[-1] / Ov_R if abs(Ov_R) > 1e-30 else 0.0
        r_out = np.linspace(R0, rmax, n_out)[1:]
        u_out_vals = C * _sample(lambda r: O_l(kk, l, r), r_out)

    r_full = np.concatenate([r_in, r_out])
    u_full = np.concatenate([u_in_vals, u_out_vals])
    scale = np.max(np.abs(u_full[: int(0.6 * len(u_full))]))
    return r_full, u_full / scale


def scattering_wavefunction(E, l=0, rmax=25.0, n_in=50, n_out=60, U0=U0_CPLX):
    k = mp.sqrt(E / HBAR2_2M)
    with mp.workdps(8):
        u, up = u_and_du_interior(k, R0, l, U0)
        L = up / u
        eta = eta_of_k(k)

        Fv, Gv = FG_l(k, l, R0)
        Fp = _deriv5(lambda r: mp.coulombf(l, eta, k * r), R0)
        Gp = _deriv5(lambda r: mp.coulombg(l, eta, k * r), R0)
        tan_delta = (Fp - L * Fv) / (Gp - L * Gv)
        delta = mp.atan(tan_delta)
        cosd, sind = mp.cos(delta), mp.sin(delta)

        kt, fp0, fm0 = interior_bc(k, l, U0)
        r_in = np.linspace(1e-6, R0, n_in)
        u_in_vals = _sample(lambda r: u_interior_cached(kt, fp0, fm0, r, U0), r_in).real

        def u_ext(r):
            Fr = mp.coulombf(l, eta, k * r)
            Gr = mp.coulombg(l, eta, k * r)
            return cosd * Fr + sind * Gr

        u_ext_R = complex(u_ext(R0)).real
        C = u_in_vals[-1] / u_ext_R if u_ext_R != 0 else 0.0
        r_out = np.linspace(R0, rmax, n_out)[1:]
        u_out_vals = C * _sample(u_ext, r_out).real

    r_full = np.concatenate([r_in, r_out])
    u_full = np.concatenate([u_in_vals, u_out_vals])
    scale = np.max(np.abs(u_full[int(0.6 * len(u_full)):]))
    return r_full, u_full / scale

def _nearest_model_state(bound_by_l, qb_by_l, l, kind, E_rel):
    """Find the model bound/quasi-bound state of the given l closest in
    energy to the experimental value E_rel, for the comparison table."""
    if kind == "bound":
        cands = [(s["E"], None) for s in bound_by_l.get(l, [])]
    else:
        cands = [(s["Er"], s["Gamma"]) for s in qb_by_l.get(l, [])]
    if not cands:
        return None
    best = min(cands, key=lambda c: abs(c[0] - E_rel))
    return best


def build_experiment_comparison(bound_by_l, qb_by_l):
    """Pair each experimental level in EXPERIMENT_12C with the closest
    model state of the same l (bound-vs-bound, resonance-vs-resonance)
    and return the rows needed for the report's comparison table."""
    rows = []
    for row in EXPERIMENT_12C:
        match = _nearest_model_state(bound_by_l, qb_by_l, row["l"], row["kind"], row["E_rel"])
        rows.append(dict(row, model=match))
    return rows


# ======================================================================
# 7c. Scattering-state (cross-section lineshape) comparison with experiment
#
# Sec. 7b/10 above compares model POLE POSITIONS (Er, Gamma from the
# fourth-quadrant root search) against experiment. This section instead
# compares the EXACT matching-theory elastic cross section sigma_el,l(E)
# (Eq. VII.1 -- no narrow-resonance approximation anywhere in its own
# derivation) directly against a single-level Breit-Wigner curve built
# purely from the EXPERIMENTAL (Er,Gamma) of EXPERIMENT_12C -- see
# THEORY_MARKDOWN Part IX for the formula (IX.1) and the Gamma_el~=
# Gamma_tot justification for this system. It is an external, model-
# independent consistency check on the computed cross section's peak
# strength and on the model's phase shift at the experimental resonance
# energy, independent of whether the model's own pole search (Sec. 7b)
# happened to land on that exact energy.
# ======================================================================
def breit_wigner_sigma_el(E_grid, l, Er_exp, Gamma_exp):
    """Single-level Breit-Wigner elastic cross section, Eq. (IX.1) of the
    theory section, built ONLY from experimental (Er_exp, Gamma_exp) in
    MeV -- no model input at all. Uses the same (pi/k^2)(2l+1) kinematic
    prefactor as the exact sigma_el,l of Eq. (VII.1) so the two curves
    are directly comparable on the same axes."""
    E = np.asarray(E_grid, dtype=float)
    k2 = E / HBAR2_2M          # E = hbar^2 k^2 / 2mu = HBAR2_2M * k^2
    half_g = Gamma_exp / 2.0
    return (np.pi / k2) * (2 * l + 1) * half_g**2 / ((E - Er_exp) ** 2 + half_g**2)


def _resonance_window(Er_exp, Gamma_exp, n_gamma=20, half_width_floor=0.05,
                       half_width_cap=3.0, n_pts=121):
    """Local energy grid centered on an experimental resonance, sized to
    roughly n_gamma*Gamma_exp on each side (so genuinely broad resonances
    get a wide enough window to show their full lineshape), clipped to
    [half_width_floor, half_width_cap] MeV so extremely narrow resonances
    (e.g. the 8.5 eV Hoyle state) still get a visually sensible window
    rather than a vanishingly small one, and extremely broad resonances
    don't stretch the window unreasonably far. n_pts is forced odd so the
    grid's exact midpoint coincides with Er_exp, guaranteeing the true
    resonance peak (not just a nearby sample) is always evaluated."""
    if n_pts % 2 == 0:
        n_pts += 1
    half_width = min(max(n_gamma * Gamma_exp, half_width_floor), half_width_cap)
    lo = max(0.05, Er_exp - half_width)
    hi = Er_exp + half_width
    return np.linspace(lo, hi, n_pts)


def scattering_experiment_comparison(l_list):
    """For every experimental RESONANCE in EXPERIMENT_12C whose l is
    among l_list, compute the model's exact sigma_el,l(E) (Eq. VII.1) and
    the experimental-parameter Breit-Wigner sigma_BW,l(E) (Eq. IX.1) on a
    shared local energy grid, plus the model's own nuclear phase shift
    evaluated AT E=Er_exp (which should sit near 90 deg mod 180 deg for
    an isolated, elastic-dominated resonance, Part IX -- an independent
    check that uses no Breit-Wigner curve at all)."""
    # Reduced working precision (dps=12, down from the module default 25):
    # this is a diagnostic overlay, not the primary Sec. 5 scattering
    # table, so the same reduced-precision philosophy used elsewhere in
    # this module for coarse/illustrative evaluations applies here too.
    with mp.workdps(12):
        rows = []
        for exp_row in EXPERIMENT_12C:
            if exp_row["kind"] != "resonance" or exp_row["l"] not in l_list:
                continue
            l = exp_row["l"]
            Er_exp = exp_row["E_rel"]
            Gamma_exp = exp_row["Gamma_keV"] / 1000.0
            if Er_exp <= 0:
                continue
            E_local = _resonance_window(Er_exp, Gamma_exp)
            model_rows = scattering_observables(E_local, l=l, U0=U0_CPLX)
            sigma_model = np.array([r["sigma_el"] for r in model_rows])
            sigma_bw = breit_wigner_sigma_el(E_local, l, Er_exp, Gamma_exp)

            delta_at_Er = scattering_observables([Er_exp], l=l, U0=U0_CPLX)[0]["delta"]
            delta_at_Er_deg = float(np.degrees(delta_at_Er)) % 180.0

            rows.append(dict(label=exp_row["label"], l=l, Er_exp=Er_exp, Gamma_exp=Gamma_exp,
                              E_local=E_local, sigma_model=sigma_model, sigma_bw=sigma_bw,
                              peak_model=float(np.max(sigma_model)), peak_bw=float(np.max(sigma_bw)),
                              delta_at_Er_deg=delta_at_Er_deg))
    rows.sort(key=lambda r: r["Er_exp"])
    return rows


def plot_scattering_experiment_comparison(rows):
    """One subplot per experimental resonance: the model's exact
    sigma_el,l(E) (solid, matching theory, Eq. VII.1) vs. the
    experimental-parameter Breit-Wigner curve (dashed, Eq. IX.1),
    log-y since the resonance peak heights span orders of magnitude
    across the states shown here (Hoyle vs. the broad 0+_3 etc.)."""
    n = len(rows)
    if n == 0:
        return None
    fig = make_subplots(rows=1, cols=n, subplot_titles=[r["label"] for r in rows])
    for i, r in enumerate(rows, start=1):
        fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=r["E_local"], y=r["sigma_model"], mode="lines",
                                  name="model sigma_el (exact matching theory)",
                                  line=dict(color=_lcolor(r["l"]), width=2.2),
                                  showlegend=(i == 1)), row=1, col=i)
        fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=r["E_local"], y=r["sigma_bw"], mode="lines",
                                  name="Breit-Wigner (experimental Er, Gamma)",
                                  line=dict(color="#e8ecf5", width=1.6, dash="dash"),
                                  showlegend=(i == 1)), row=1, col=i)
        fig.add_vline(x=r["Er_exp"], line=dict(color="#9aa5bd", dash="dot", width=1),
                      row=1, col=i)
        fig.update_yaxes(type="log", row=1, col=i,
                          title_text=("sigma_el (fm^2)" if i == 1 else None))
        fig.update_xaxes(title_text="E (MeV)", row=1, col=i)
    fig.update_layout(
        title="Elastic cross section near each experimental resonance: exact matching theory vs. experimental-width Breit-Wigner",
        hovermode="closest", **{k: v for k, v in LAYOUT_KW.items() if k != "hovermode"})
    return _to_div(fig, "fig_scatt_exp")

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $\mu$ | reduced mass, $\mu = m_\alpha m_{\rm Be}/(m_\alpha+m_{\rm Be})$ |
| $l$ | orbital angular momentum quantum number |
| $E$ | total energy in the c.m. frame; $E=\hbar^2k^2/2\mu$ |
| $k$ | wavenumber (real for scattering, $k=i\kappa$ for bound states, $k=k_r-ik_i$ for resonances) |
| $\eta(k)$ | Sommerfeld (Coulomb) parameter, $\eta(k)=\dfrac{Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}e^2\mu}{\hbar^2 k}$ |
| $R,a$ | Woods–Saxon radius and diffuseness |
| $V_0$ | real Woods–Saxon well depth |
| $V_l=\hbar^2l(l+1)/2\mu R^2$ | constant interior centrifugal approximation |
| $F_l(\eta,kr),\,G_l(\eta,kr)$ | regular / irregular Coulomb wavefunctions |
| $H_l^{(\pm)}(\eta,kr)=G_l\pm iF_l$ | outgoing ($+$) / incoming ($-$) Coulomb Hankel functions |
| $\sigma_l={\rm arg}\,\Gamma(l+1+i\eta)$ | Coulomb phase shift |
| $S_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$ | (nuclear-modified) S-matrix element |
| $W_{-\eta,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)$ | Whittaker function of the second kind |

The interaction is taken, as specified, to be a **finite-range Woods–Saxon nuclear potential plus a constant interior centrifugal shift** inside $r<R$, and the **exact Coulomb + exact centrifugal potential** outside:

$$
V(r)=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle -\frac{V_0}{1+e^{(r-R)/a}} + V_l\,, & r<R \\[2ex]
\displaystyle \frac{Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}e^2}{r}+\frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2\mu r^2}\,, & r\ge R.
\end{cases}
$$

The constant-centrifugal approximation in the interior is what keeps the interior equation exactly solvable in closed form (it is the same device that makes the Eckart/Woods–Saxon class of potentials integrable via a Riemann/hypergeometric reduction); the *exterior* equation, in contrast, keeps the full $1/r^2$ centrifugal term and the exact Coulomb potential, since there the Coulomb wave equation is itself exactly solvable without such an approximation.

All results below are exact, closed-form, symbolic solutions of the corresponding differential equations; no discretization, shooting, or variational approximation is used anywhere.

## Part I — Regularity at the Origin

The radial equation for $u_l(r)=rR_l(r)$ is

$$
u_l''(r) + \left[k^2-\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}-\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}V(r)\right]u_l(r)=0. \tag{I.1}
$$

**Indicial analysis.** Near $r=0$ the Woods–Saxon term is finite,
$V_N(r)\to -V_0/(1+e^{-R/a})\equiv V_N(0)$, so the only *singular* term in the bracket of (I.1) as $r\to0$ is the centrifugal term $l(l+1)/r^2$. Substituting the Frobenius ansatz $u_l(r)=r^{s}(1+O(r))$ into

$$
u_l'' - \frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}u_l + O(1)\,u_l = 0
$$

and keeping only the leading $r^{s-2}$ terms gives the **indicial equation**

$$
s(s-1)-l(l+1)=0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; s=l+1 \quad\text{or}\quad s=-l. \tag{I.2}
$$

Thus the two independent local solutions behave as

$$
u_l^{\rm reg}(r)\sim r^{l+1},\qquad u_l^{\rm irr}(r)\sim r^{-l}\quad (r\to0). \tag{I.3}
$$

**Selection of the regular branch.** The irregular branch $u_l^{\rm irr}\sim r^{-l}$ makes the full three-dimensional wavefunction $R_l(r)=u_l(r)/r\sim r^{-l-1}$ diverge at the origin for every $l\ge0$; the associated probability density $|R_l|^2 r^2\,dr\sim r^{-2l}\,dr$ is non-normalizable at $r=0$ for $l\ge 0$ (divergent for $l\ge 1/2$, and for $l=0$ it produces a $\delta$-function source term $-\nabla^2(1/r)=4\pi\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r)$ inconsistent with a source-free Schrödinger equation at the origin). Hence the irregular branch is discarded on physical grounds (finite kinetic energy / no point source at the origin) for every $l$, including $l=0$.

Consequently the physically acceptable interior solution is uniquely, up to normalization,

$$
\boxed{u_l(r)\;\propto\; r^{l+1}\big(1+c_1 r + c_2 r^2+\cdots\big),\qquad r\to0,}
$$

and in particular

$$
\boxed{u_l(0)=0\quad\text{for every } l\ge0.} \tag{I.4}
$$

This is the origin boundary condition quoted as (2) in the reference formalism (Vertse–Pál–Balogh) and holds identically for bound, quasi-bound, and scattering solutions, since it is a statement about the *local* behavior of the differential equation at $r=0$ and does not depend on the sign or complexity of $E$.

## Part II — Matching at the Nuclear Radius $r=R$

### Continuity of $u$ and $u'$

The potential $V(r)$ defined above has a *finite* jump discontinuity at $r=R$ (the Woods–Saxon form factor and its exterior continuation are both finite there; only the *diffuseness* of the physical Woods–Saxon form makes the true potential continuous, but even in the idealized sharp-cutoff limit used for matching, the discontinuity in $V$ is finite). Integrate (I.1) across a small interval $(R-\varepsilon,R+\varepsilon)$:

$$
u_l'(R+\varepsilon)-u_l'(R-\varepsilon) = \int_{R-\varepsilon}^{R+\varepsilon}\left[\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}+\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}V(r)-k^2\right]u_l(r)\,dr. \tag{II.1}
$$

Because the integrand is **bounded** (no $\delta$-functions or $1/r$ singularities appear at $r=R$ for finite $V_0,R,a$), the right-hand side vanishes as $\varepsilon\to0$:

$$
\boxed{u_{\rm in}(R)=u_{\rm out}(R)} \tag{II.2}
$$
$$
\boxed{u_{\rm in}'(R)=u_{\rm out}'(R).} \tag{II.3}
$$

**Why both conditions are needed.** Equation (I.1) is a second-order ODE; a well-posed matching of two second-order solutions across an interior point requires exactly two conditions (value and slope) to fix the two free constants of the exterior solution relative to the (already normalization-fixed) interior solution. If $V(r)$ contained a $\delta$-function, (II.1) would instead give a finite jump in $u'$ with continuous $u$; if $V(r)$ contained a $1/r^2$-type singularity exactly at $R$, neither condition would survive in this simple form. For our finite, bounded Woods–Saxon discontinuity, both (II.2) and (II.3) hold exactly.

### Logarithmic derivative and the transcendental eigenvalue equation

Divide (II.3) by (II.2):

$$
\boxed{\left.\frac{u'}{u}\right|_{\rm in}(R;E) \;=\; \left.\frac{u'}{u}\right|_{\rm out}(R;E).} \tag{II.4}
$$

The logarithmic derivative is invariant under rescaling of either solution, so (II.4) is *the* matching condition: it depends only on $E$ (through $k^2=2\mu E/\hbar^2$) and on the fixed geometric/potential parameters $V_0,R,a,Z_\alpha,Z_{\rm Be},l$. Because $u_{\rm in}$ is pinned by the regularity condition (I.4) and $u_{\rm out}$ is pinned by whichever asymptotic condition at $r\to\infty$ is imposed (Part III/IV/V), equation (II.4) is one complex (or real) equation in the single unknown $E$ (or $k$):

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\rm in}(E) - \mathcal{L}_{\rm out}(E) = 0, \tag{II.5}
$$

which is the **transcendental eigenvalue equation** common to all three problems. The three physical cases differ only in *which* exterior solution enters $\mathcal L_{\rm out}$.

### The exact interior solution $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)$

With the constant-centrifugal approximation, the interior equation is

$$
u_l''(r)+\left[k^2-\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}V_l-\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\Big(-\frac{V_0}{1+e^{(r-R)/a}}\Big)\right]u_l(r)=0,\qquad r<R, \tag{II.6}
$$

i.e. an *effective* pure Woods–Saxon problem with the substitution $k^2\to \tilde k^2\equiv k^2-\dfrac{2\mu V_l}{\hbar^2}$. This is exactly the Eckart-class potential problem: the substitution $x=-e^{-(r-R)/a}$ (equivalently $z=\big(1+e^{(r-R)/a}\big)^{-1}$) reduces (II.6) to a Riemann differential equation with regular singular points at $0,1,\infty$, whose solution regular at the origin is the Gauss hypergeometric function

$$
u_{\rm in}(r) \;\propto\; e^{i\tilde k r}\,F\!\left(\gamma_+,\gamma_+',\,1-2ia\tilde k,\,-e^{(R-r)/a}\right)\Big|_{\text{regular combination fixed by (I.4)}}, \tag{II.7}
$$

$$
\gamma_\pm = \mp ia\tilde k \pm ia\sqrt{\tilde k^2+U_0},\qquad U_0=\frac{2\mu V_0}{\hbar^2}, \tag{II.8}
$$

exactly as derived for the Woods–Saxon (Saxon–Woods) member of the Eckart class. $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)=u_{\rm in}'(R)/u_{\rm in}(R)$ then follows in closed form from (II.7)–(II.8) and standard hypergeometric-function derivative identities; it is an *entire, single-valued analytic function of $k$* (equivalently of $E$) because $F(a,b,c,z)$ is analytic in its parameters away from the poles of $\Gamma(c)$, which do not occur here for physical $a>0$. This analyticity is what allows $k$ to be continued off the real axis in Parts III and IV below.

## Part III — Bound-State Boundary Conditions ($E<0$)

### Exterior solution

For $E<0$ write $k=i\kappa$, $\kappa=\sqrt{-2\mu E}/\hbar>0$. The Coulomb parameter analytically continues to

$$
\eta(i\kappa) \equiv \eta_\kappa = \frac{Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}e^2\mu}{\hbar^2\kappa} \quad(\text{real, since } Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}>0). \tag{III.1}
$$

The exterior equation for $r\ge R$,

$$
u_l''(r)+\left[-\kappa^2-\frac{2\eta_\kappa\kappa}{r}-\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}\right]u_l(r)=0, \tag{III.2}
$$

is the Coulomb wave equation analytically continued to negative energy. Its two independent solutions are the Whittaker functions $M_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)$ and $W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)$. As $r\to\infty$,

$$
M_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)\;\sim\; e^{+\kappa r}(2\kappa r)^{-\eta_\kappa}\big[1+O(1/r)\big] \quad\text{(exponentially growing)}, \tag{III.3}
$$
$$
W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)\;\sim\; e^{-\kappa r}(2\kappa r)^{\eta_\kappa}\big[1+O(1/r)\big] \quad\text{(exponentially decaying)}. \tag{III.4}
$$

**Selection of the decaying branch.** Square-integrability, $\displaystyle\int_R^\infty|u_l(r)|^2dr<\infty$, forbids the exponentially growing branch (III.3) with any nonzero coefficient. Hence

$$
\boxed{u_{\rm out}(r) = N\,W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r),\qquad r\ge R,} \tag{III.5}
$$

which satisfies $u(r)\to0$ as $r\to\infty$ automatically, for *any* $\kappa>0$ — the Coulomb tail vanishes at infinity, so unlike a bound Coulomb (attractive) problem there is no additional discreteness imposed by the exterior equation alone; discreteness comes entirely from the interior matching below.

### Transcendental energy equation

Insert (III.5) into (II.4), evaluating both sides at $k=i\kappa$. The interior side follows directly from (II.7)-(II.8): writing $u_{\rm in}(r)\propto e^{i\tilde k r}F(z(r))$ with $F(z)\equiv F(\gamma_+,\gamma_+';1-2ia\tilde k;z)$ and $z(r)=-e^{(R-r)/a}$, so that $z(R)=-1$ and $z'(r)=e^{(R-r)/a}/a$, the hypergeometric derivative identity
$$
\frac{d}{dz}F(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)=\frac{\alpha\beta}{\gamma}\,F(\alpha+1,\beta+1;\gamma+1;z)
$$
gives $u_{\rm in}'(r)=i\tilde k\,u_{\rm in}(r)+e^{i\tilde k r}F'(z(r))\,z'(r)$, and hence the **exact interior logarithmic derivative**

$$
\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)=\left.\frac{u_{\rm in}'(r)}{u_{\rm in}(r)}\right|_{r=R}
= i\tilde k \;+\; \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{\gamma_+\gamma_+'}{1-2ia\tilde k}\cdot
\frac{F(\gamma_++1,\gamma_+'+1;\,2-2ia\tilde k;\,-1)}{F(\gamma_+,\gamma_+';\,1-2ia\tilde k;\,-1)},
\qquad \tilde k=\tilde k(i\kappa),
$$

with $\gamma_+,\gamma_+'$ given by (II.8) at $\tilde k=\tilde k(i\kappa)$. On the exterior side, since $u_{\rm out}(r)=N\,W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)$, its logarithmic derivative at $r=R$ is, by the chain rule ($d(2\kappa r)/dr=2\kappa$) and with the overall normalization $N$ cancelling top and bottom,

$$
\mathcal L_{\rm out}(-\hbar^2\kappa^2/2\mu)=\left.\frac{u_{\rm out}'(r)}{u_{\rm out}(r)}\right|_{r=R}=\frac{N\cdot 2\kappa\,W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}'(2\kappa R)}{N\,W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa R)}=2\kappa\,\frac{W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}'(2\kappa R)}{W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa R)}.
$$

Setting $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(-\hbar^2\kappa^2/2\mu)=\mathcal L_{\rm out}(-\hbar^2\kappa^2/2\mu)$ per (II.4) gives the boxed transcendental equation:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal L_{\rm in}(-\hbar^2\kappa^2/2\mu) \;=\; 2\kappa\,\frac{W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}'(2\kappa R)}{W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa R)}.}
\tag{III.6}
$$

This is one **real** transcendental equation in the single real unknown $\kappa>0$ (equivalently in $E<0$); its discrete roots $\kappa_{n_r}$, $n_r=0,1,2,\dots$ (ordered by the number of interior nodes, per the standard oscillation theorem for Sturm–Liouville problems on a finite interval with a fixed, energy-independent boundary condition of $u\to0$ at both ends of the relevant interval) give the bound-state energies

$$
E_{n_r} = -\frac{\hbar^2\kappa_{n_r}^2}{2\mu}.
$$

Existence of at least one root requires the interior well to be deep enough that $\mathcal L_{\rm in}$ can match the (always negative, for $\kappa>0$) logarithmic derivative of the decaying Whittaker function at $R$; uniqueness of each $E_{n_r}$ within a bracketed interval follows from the strict monotonicity in $\kappa$ of the right-hand side of (III.6) (a standard property of $W_{-\eta,\,l+1/2}$) combined with the interlacing (Sturm) property of $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(\kappa)$'s poles and zeros as $\kappa$ increases through the well.

## Part IV — Quasi-Bound (Gamow–Siegert Resonance) Boundary Conditions

### Complex energy ansatz

Let

$$
E = E_R - i\frac{\Gamma}{2},\qquad E_R>0,\ \Gamma>0, \tag{IV.1}
$$

with corresponding complex wavenumber

$$
k=k_r-ik_i,\qquad k_r,k_i>0,\qquad E=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}(k_r-ik_i)^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}(k_r^2-k_i^2) - i\frac{\hbar^2 k_rk_i}{\mu}, \tag{IV.2}
$$

so that $E_R=\hbar^2(k_r^2-k_i^2)/2\mu$ and $\Gamma = 2\hbar^2k_rk_i/\mu$. This $k$ lies in the **fourth quadrant** of the complex $k$-plane, exactly as in the neutral (Woods–Saxon-only) case treated earlier; the Coulomb parameter $\eta(k)=Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}e^2\mu/(\hbar^2k)$ is now complex as well, and all Coulomb functions below are understood as their standard analytic continuations to complex argument and complex $\eta$ (well defined via the confluent-hypergeometric representation of $F_l,G_l$; no numerical evaluation is implied, only the existence of the analytic continuation).

### Siegert (purely outgoing) boundary condition

The physical requirement defining a **resonant (Gamow) state** is that the state decays only by particle emission — there is no wave coming in from infinity. In terms of the Coulomb Hankel functions $H_l^{(\pm)}(\eta,kr)=G_l\pm iF_l$, with asymptotic forms

$$
H_l^{(\pm)}(\eta,kr)\underset{r\to\infty}{\sim}\exp\left[\pm i\Big(kr-\eta\ln 2kr-\frac{l\pi}{2}+\sigma_l\Big)\right], \tag{IV.3}
$$

the general exterior solution is $u_{\rm out}=A_{\rm in}H_l^{(-)}(\eta,kr)+A_{\rm out}H_l^{(+)}(\eta,kr)$. The **Siegert boundary condition** sets the incoming amplitude to zero:

$$
\boxed{A_{\rm in}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_{\rm out}(r) = N\,O_l(\eta,kr)\equiv N\,H_l^{(+)}(\eta,kr),\qquad r\ge R.} \tag{IV.4}
$$

Substituting $k=k_r-ik_i$ into (IV.3),

$$
O_l(\eta,kr)\sim \exp\left[i(k_r-ik_i)r+\cdots\right] = e^{+k_i r}\,e^{\,i(k_rr+\cdots)}, \tag{IV.5}
$$

reproduces the characteristic Gamow tail — **oscillation with exponentially growing amplitude**, $e^{+k_ir}e^{ik_rr}$ — which is the hallmark of the outgoing Siegert solution and the reason Gamow states are not square-integrable in the ordinary (Hilbert-space) sense; they require the Zel'dovich regularization,

$$
N^2=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}\int_0^\infty e^{-\varepsilon r^2}u^2(r)\,dr, \tag{IV.6}
$$

evaluated in practice (as in the reference GAMOW code) by analytic continuation of the norm integral along a contour into the complex $r$-plane where the integrand's exponential growth is converted to exponential decay — again a fully analytic, non-numerical operation in principle, since the relevant tail integral has a closed form in terms of the same Coulomb/Whittaker special functions.

### Complex transcendental equation

Matching (II.4) with (IV.4), evaluating both sides at the complex wavenumber $k=k_r-ik_i$. The interior side follows directly from (II.7)-(II.8): writing $u_{\rm in}(r)\propto e^{i\tilde k r}F(z(r))$ with $F(z)\equiv F(\gamma_+,\gamma_+';1-2ia\tilde k;z)$ and $z(r)=-e^{(R-r)/a}$, so that $z(R)=-1$ and $z'(r)=e^{(R-r)/a}/a$, the hypergeometric derivative identity
$$
\frac{d}{dz}F(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)=\frac{\alpha\beta}{\gamma}\,F(\alpha+1,\beta+1;\gamma+1;z)
$$
gives $u_{\rm in}'(r)=i\tilde k\,u_{\rm in}(r)+e^{i\tilde k r}F'(z(r))\,z'(r)$, and hence the **exact interior logarithmic derivative**, now evaluated at complex $k$ (equivalently complex $E=\hbar^2k^2/2\mu$),

$$
\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)=\left.\frac{u_{\rm in}'(r)}{u_{\rm in}(r)}\right|_{r=R}
= i\tilde k \;+\; \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{\gamma_+\gamma_+'}{1-2ia\tilde k}\cdot
\frac{F(\gamma_++1,\gamma_+'+1;\,2-2ia\tilde k;\,-1)}{F(\gamma_+,\gamma_+';\,1-2ia\tilde k;\,-1)},
\qquad \tilde k=\tilde k(k),
$$

with $\gamma_+,\gamma_+'$ given by (II.8) at $\tilde k=\tilde k(k)$; this expression is unchanged in form from the bound-state case, since (as noted after (II.8)) $\mathcal L_{\rm in}$ is one and the same entire analytic function of $k$, merely evaluated here off the imaginary axis. On the exterior side, since $u_{\rm out}(r)=N\,O_l(\eta,kr)$ (the Siegert condition (IV.4)), its logarithmic derivative at $r=R$ is, by the chain rule ($d(kr)/dr=k$) and with the overall normalization $N$ cancelling top and bottom,

$$
\mathcal L_{\rm out}(E)=\left.\frac{u_{\rm out}'(r)}{u_{\rm out}(r)}\right|_{r=R}=\frac{N\,k\,O_l'(\eta,kR)}{N\,O_l(\eta,kR)}=k\,\frac{O_l'(\eta,kR)}{O_l(\eta,kR)}.
$$

Writing this out in terms of the underlying regular/irregular Coulomb functions, $O_l(\eta,kr)=G_l(\eta,kr)+iF_l(\eta,kr)$ (Part VI notation), so that $O_l'=G_l'+iF_l'$ (derivatives with respect to the argument $kr$) and the exterior logarithmic derivative is, fully explicitly,

$$
\mathcal L_{\rm out}(E)=k\,\frac{G_l'(\eta,kR)+iF_l'(\eta,kR)}{G_l(\eta,kR)+iF_l(\eta,kR)}.
$$

Setting $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)=\mathcal L_{\rm out}(E)$ per (II.4) gives the boxed complex transcendental equation:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E) \;=\; k\,\frac{O_l'(\eta,kR)}{O_l(\eta,kR)},\qquad E=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2\mu}.}
\tag{IV.7}
$$

This is **one complex equation in one complex unknown** $k$ (equivalently $E$); its roots in the fourth quadrant, $k_n = k_{r,n}-ik_{i,n}$, give the discrete set of resonance parameters

$$
E_{R,n}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}(k_{r,n}^2-k_{i,n}^2),\qquad
\Gamma_n=\frac{2\hbar^2}{\mu}k_{r,n}k_{i,n},\qquad
\tau_n=\frac{\hbar}{\Gamma_n}. \tag{IV.8}
$$

**Physical interpretation.** A complex eigenvalue of a nominally Hermitian-looking boundary-value problem is possible here precisely because the Siegert boundary condition (IV.4) is *not* self-adjoint (it fixes different linear combinations of $u,u'$ than the $L^2$ condition $u\to0$ used for bound states); the problem is a *non-Hermitian* eigenvalue problem on the space of purely outgoing functions. $E_R$ is the position of the resonance (its energy relative to the $\alpha$+Be dissociation threshold), and $\Gamma$ its width; by the time–energy uncertainty relation the state has a finite lifetime $\tau=\hbar/\Gamma$, decaying as $|\Psi(t)|^2\propto e^{-\Gamma t/\hbar}$ for an observer at fixed finite $r$ before the exponentially growing tail is reached — the growing exterior tail is not a physical particle density at $t=0$ but the imprint, at a fixed radius, of flux that was emitted at earlier and earlier times as $r$ increases, consistent with a source that has been decaying since $t=-\infty$ in the idealized stationary-state treatment.

Note also, per the standard classification recalled in the reference GAMOW paper: poles of $S_l(k)$ on the positive imaginary axis ($k=i\kappa$, $\kappa>0$) are the bound states of Part III; poles in the lower half $k$-plane with $k_r>k_i>0$ are **physical resonances** (as derived here); poles with $k_i>k_r$ are mathematically admissible roots of (IV.7) but correspond to *unphysical* (excessively broad, non-resonant) solutions and are excluded from the physical spectrum.

## Part V — Scattering Boundary Conditions ($E>0$)

### Exterior solution

For real $k>0$, $\eta(k)=Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}e^2\mu/(\hbar^2 k)$ is real and positive (repulsive Coulomb). The two independent, oscillatory (non-decaying) solutions of the exact exterior equation are $F_l(\eta,kr)$ and $G_l(\eta,kr)$, or equivalently the incoming/outgoing combinations $H_l^{(\pm)}=G_l\pm iF_l$ with asymptotics (IV.3) now understood for real $k$ (purely oscillatory, no exponential growth or decay). The general physical scattering solution is written as an incoming wave of fixed unit amplitude plus a scattered outgoing wave:

$$
\boxed{u_{\rm out}(r) = H_l^{(-)}(\eta,kr) - S_l\,H_l^{(+)}(\eta,kr),\qquad r\ge R,} \tag{V.1}
$$

with $S_l$ the (nuclear-modified) partial-wave S-matrix element to be determined; $\sigma_l$ is already absorbed into the definition of $H_l^{(\pm)}$, so $S_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$ with $\delta_l$ the purely *nuclear* phase shift (the total phase relative to a Coulomb-only asymptotic wave is $\sigma_l+\delta_l$).

### Matching equation and closed-form S-matrix

Insert (V.1) into (II.4). With $L\equiv \mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)$ the (real, since the interior potential is real and $E>0$ in the classically-relevant sense — see remark below) interior logarithmic derivative,

$$
L=\frac{H_l^{(-)\prime}(\eta,kR)-S_lH_l^{(+)\prime}(\eta,kR)}{H_l^{(-)}(\eta,kR)-S_lH_l^{(+)}(\eta,kR)}, \tag{V.2}
$$

solve linearly for $S_l$:

$$
\boxed{
S_l(k) = \frac{H_l^{(-)\prime}(\eta,kR)-L\,H_l^{(-)}(\eta,kR)}{H_l^{(+)\prime}(\eta,kR)-L\,H_l^{(+)}(\eta,kR)}.}
\tag{V.3}
$$
where $L\equiv\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)$, given explicitly for real $k>0$ (scattering regime) by the same closed-form expression derived from (II.7)-(II.8) in Parts III-IV,

$$
\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)=\left.\frac{u_{\rm in}'(r)}{u_{\rm in}(r)}\right|_{r=R}
= i\tilde k \;+\; \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{\gamma_+\gamma_+'}{1-2ia\tilde k}\cdot
\frac{F(\gamma_++1,\gamma_+'+1;\,2-2ia\tilde k;\,-1)}{F(\gamma_+,\gamma_+';\,1-2ia\tilde k;\,-1)},
\qquad \tilde k=\tilde k(k),
$$

with $\gamma_+,\gamma_+'$ from (II.8) evaluated at this real $k$; this is the same entire function of $k$ used for the bound-state pole (III.6) and the Gamow pole (IV.7), now sampled on the real axis, which is precisely the statement of Part VI item 1. 
Equation (V.3) is the exact, closed-form analytic S-matrix element for arbitrary real $k>0$; equivalently the phase shift follows from

$$
\delta_l(k) = \frac{1}{2i}\ln S_l(k). \tag{V.4}
$$

An equivalent real-variable form is obtained by writing $u_{\rm out}=\cos\delta_l\,F_l+\sin\delta_l\,G_l$ (a real basis valid for real $k$) and matching logarithmic derivatives at $r=R$:

$$
\boxed{\tan\delta_l(k) = \frac{F_l'(\eta,kR) - L\,F_l(\eta,kR)}{G_l'(\eta,kR) - L\,G_l(\eta,kR)}.}
\tag{V.5}
$$

*(Equation (V.5) follows from (V.2) by substituting $H_l^{(\pm)}=G_l\pm iF_l$ into (V.2) and separating real and imaginary parts; it is algebraically equivalent to (V.3) via $S_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$.)*

**Unitarity remark.** Because the interior potential (real $V_0,R,a$) and the exact exterior potential are both real for $E>0$, $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)$ and $L$ in (V.2)–(V.5) are real, which guarantees $|S_l(k)|=1$ for all real $k$ — the analytic statement of flux conservation (unitarity) for a purely real (non-absorptive) potential. If a complex (optical) potential were used instead, the same formulas (V.1)–(V.3) still hold verbatim, with $L$ now complex, and $|S_l|<1$ follows automatically, the missing flux $1-|S_l|^2$ being the reaction/absorption probability.

## Part VI — Unified Analytical Framework

All three problems solved above share:

1. **The same interior differential equation** (II.6) and **the same closed-form interior solution** (II.7)–(II.8), fixed uniquely (up to overall normalization) by the single regularity condition $u(0)=0$ derived in Part I. The interior logarithmic derivative $\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)$ is one and the same analytic function of $E$ (or $k$) in every case; it is simply *evaluated at a different location in the complex $k$-plane* for each problem.

2. **The same matching principle at $r=R$** — continuity of $u$ and $u'$, equivalently continuity of the logarithmic derivative, equation (II.4)/(II.5) — is imposed identically in all three cases.

3. **A single exact exterior differential equation** (the Coulomb equation with the full $1/r^2$ centrifugal term), whose two independent solutions $F_l,G_l$ (or $H_l^{(\pm)}$, or their analytic continuations $M_{-\eta,l+1/2},W_{-\eta,l+1/2}$) are the *same pair of special functions* in every regime; what changes between regimes is only **which asymptotic combination of this same pair is physically admissible**:

| Regime | $k$ | Exterior boundary condition at $r\to\infty$ | Exterior solution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bound state | $k=i\kappa,\ \kappa>0$ | $u\to0$ ($L^2$-normalizable) | $W_{-\eta_\kappa,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)$ only |
| Quasi-bound (Gamow) | $k=k_r-ik_i,\ k_r,k_i>0$ | purely outgoing (Siegert), $A_{\rm in}=0$ | $O_l(\eta,kr)=H_l^{(+)}(\eta,kr)$ only |
| Scattering | $k>0$ real | incoming amplitude fixed, both waves present, flux-normalizable | $H_l^{(-)}(\eta,kr)-S_lH_l^{(+)}(\eta,kr)$ |

4. Each regime's transcendental equation — (III.6), (IV.7), (V.3)/(V.5) — is obtained from the **same master equation** (II.5) by substituting the appropriate row of the table above for $\mathcal L_{\rm out}$. In this precise sense bound states, resonances, and scattering states are three boundary-value problems for one and the same second-order ODE and one and the same interior solution, differing only in the choice of exterior asymptotic subspace — exactly the unifying statement requested.

5. **Existence and uniqueness of the spectrum.**
   - *Bound states*: (III.6) is real-valued for real $\kappa>0$; by the Sturm oscillation theorem applied to the effective one-dimensional problem on $(0,\infty)$ with a potential that is bounded below, finite-range (nuclear part) plus a repulsive tail vanishing at infinity, there exists a finite or countably infinite discrete set $\{\kappa_{n_r}\}$, indexed by the number of interior nodes $n_r=0,1,2,\dots$, so long as the well is deep enough to support at least one bound state; each root is simple (non-degenerate) because $\partial\mathcal L_{\rm in}/\partial\kappa\neq\partial \mathcal L_{\rm out}/\partial\kappa$ generically at a crossing, which follows from the strict monotonicity of the Whittaker-function logarithmic derivative in $\kappa$.
   - *Quasi-bound states*: (IV.7) is a holomorphic equation in $k$ (both sides analytic in the fourth quadrant, since $\mathcal L_{\rm in}$ is entire in $k$ as noted after (II.8), and $O_l(\eta,kr)$ is analytic in $k$ away from $k=0$); by the argument principle the number of roots inside any contour in the fourth quadrant enclosing them is finite and computable in principle from a contour integral of the logarithmic derivative of (IV.7), establishing existence of at most a countable, locally finite set of resonances, with uniqueness (simplicity of each root) guaranteed generically by the same non-degeneracy argument as above, now applied to the complex derivative of (IV.7).
   - *Scattering states*: for every real $k>0$ (with the sole possible exception of $k=0$ itself, where the Coulomb parameter $\eta(k)\to\infty$ and the analytic representation degenerates — the "does not work at zero energy" limitation noted in the reference formalism), $S_l(k)$ in (V.3) exists, is unique, and is unimodular; there is no discreteness here because the boundary condition (fixed unit incoming amplitude) is imposed for a continuum of $k$ values rather than isolating discrete roots of a homogeneous equation — scattering states form the continuous spectrum of the problem, complementary to the discrete bound-state spectrum found above.

## Part VII — Derived Observables: S-Matrix, Elastic and Reaction Cross Sections

Everything in this section is obtained *algebraically* from the partial-wave S-matrix element $S_l(k)=e^{2i\delta_l(k)}$ of equation (V.3)/(V.4) — no new differential equation or boundary condition is introduced; these are simply the standard physical observables built from $S_l(k)$.

### S-matrix components and the unitarity circle

For a purely real interior potential ($W_0=0$), Part V's unitarity remark showed $|S_l(k)|=1$ for all real $k$: the point $S_l(k)$ traces the **unit circle** in the complex plane (an *Argand diagram*) as $E$ is swept, with $\mathrm{Re}\,S_l=\cos2\delta_l$ and $\mathrm{Im}\,S_l=\sin2\delta_l$. Introducing a (small, illustrative) absorptive Woods–Saxon depth $W_0>0$ makes $L=\mathcal L_{\rm in}(E)$ complex, which pulls the trajectory of $S_l(k)$ *inside* the unit circle: $|S_l|<1$ signals net removal of flux from the elastic channel (absorption / reaction), exactly the "missing flux" mentioned at the end of Part V.

### Elastic and reaction cross sections

The partial-wave **elastic** cross section is the cross section for the alpha particle to scatter with its incoming flux fully returned to the elastic (alpha+Be-8, same internal state) channel:

$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}(k) = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\,(2l+1)\,\big|1-S_l(k)\big|^2.}
\tag{VII.1}
$$

The partial-wave **reaction** (absorption) cross section is the cross section for flux to leave the elastic channel entirely (captured, or re-emitted into any other open channel lumped into the optical potential's imaginary part):

$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\mathrm{reac},l}(k) = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\,(2l+1)\,\big(1-|S_l(k)|^2\big).}
\tag{VII.2}
$$

Their sum is the partial-wave **total** cross section, which by direct algebraic expansion of (VII.1)–(VII.2) equals the optical theorem's forward-scattering statement restricted to partial wave $l$:

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{tot},l}(k) = \sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}+\sigma_{\mathrm{reac},l} = \frac{2\pi}{k^2}\,(2l+1)\,\big(1-\mathrm{Re}\,S_l(k)\big). \tag{VII.3}
$$

The quantities $R_l\equiv|S_l|^2$ (elastic-channel survival probability) and $T_l\equiv 1-|S_l|^2$ (absorption probability) plotted alongside the phase shift are the *unitarity-normalized* (per-partial-wave, dimensionless) versions of (VII.1)–(VII.2): $\sigma_{\mathrm{reac},l}$ is directly proportional to $T_l$, while $\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}$ additionally depends on the phase $\delta_l$ through $|1-S_l|^2=1-2\,\mathrm{Re}\,S_l+|S_l|^2$, so $\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}$ is *not* a simple function of $R_l$ alone — it also encodes the interference between the incident and elastically-scattered waves.

### Summed (angle-integrated) cross sections

Summing (VII.1)–(VII.3) over the partial waves $l=0,1,\dots,l_{\max}$ actually included in a given calculation gives the (truncated) total elastic, reaction, and total cross sections,

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}(E)=\sum_{l=0}^{l_{\max}}\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}(E),\qquad
\sigma_{\mathrm{reac}}(E)=\sum_{l=0}^{l_{\max}}\sigma_{\mathrm{reac},l}(E),\qquad
\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}(E)=\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}(E)+\sigma_{\mathrm{reac}}(E), \tag{VII.4}
$$

which converge as $l_{\max}\to\infty$ once $l$ exceeds roughly the classical grazing angular momentum $kR$ (beyond which the centrifugal barrier makes $S_l\to1$ and each partial wave's contribution to (VII.1)–(VII.3) vanishes); the finite-$l_{\max}$ sums plotted here are lower bounds on the true cross sections, adequate for illustrating the shape and resonant structure of the energy dependence but not a converged partial-wave sum at every energy shown.

### Reading the plots

- **S-matrix panel**: left sub-plot shows $\mathrm{Re}\,S_l,\ \mathrm{Im}\,S_l,\ |S_l|$ individually versus $E$ for every $l$; right sub-plot is the Argand diagram, the parametric curve $(\mathrm{Re}\,S_l(E),\mathrm{Im}\,S_l(E))$ traced as $E$ increases, with the unit circle drawn for reference — deviation from the circle is a direct visual measure of absorption.
- **Cross-section panel**: partial-wave $\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}$ (solid) and $\sigma_{\mathrm{reac},l}$ (dashed) for each $l$, plus the summed $\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{reac}}$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}$ (heavy light-gray curves) over the $l$-range actually computed.
- **Potential panel**: the matching radius $R$ (Eqs. II.2–II.4) is marked explicitly with a vertical dashed line and annotation, since it is the single radius at which *every* boundary condition in Parts II–V is imposed — bound, quasi-bound, and scattering alike.

## Part VIII — The Specific Model Potential Adopted for alpha + 8Be

Parts I–VII above are fully general: they hold for *any* choice of the three real
numbers $(V_0,R,a)$ entering $V(r)$. This section documents the specific,
numerical choice made for the alpha + 8Be system (equivalently, the second step
of the triple-alpha process, 8Be($\alpha,\gamma$)12C), states what each
parameter physically represents, and is explicit about the trade-offs that
choice involves.

### The potential, with the numbers used here

$$
V_l(r)=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle -\frac{V_0}{1+e^{(r-R)/a}} + \frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2\mu R^2}\,, & r < R \\[2ex]
\displaystyle \frac{Z_\alpha Z_{\rm Be}e^2}{r}+\frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2\mu r^2}\,, & r \ge R
\end{cases}
\qquad
\begin{aligned}
V_0 &= 21.75\ \text{MeV} \\
R &= 1.30\,(A_\alpha^{1/3}+A_{\rm Be}^{1/3}) = 4.664\ \text{fm} \\
a &= 0.65\ \text{fm}
\end{aligned}
$$

| Parameter | Physical role | How its value here was fixed |
|---|---|---|
| $R$ | nuclear (Woods–Saxon) radius and matching radius; where the finite-range nuclear + centrifugal interior potential is joined to the exact Coulomb + centrifugal exterior potential | fixed geometrically from the standard $r_0(A_1^{1/3}+A_2^{1/3})$ two-cluster radius formula with $r_0=1.30$ fm, using $A_\alpha=4$, $A_{\rm Be}=8$ |
| $a$ | Woods–Saxon surface diffuseness; controls how sharply the nuclear attraction turns off near $R$ | standard literature value for light-cluster Woods–Saxon potentials ($a\approx0.6$–$0.7$ fm); not re-fit |
| $V_0$ | depth of the real, $l$-independent nuclear well | **tuned** (the only free parameter adjusted for this system) so that the *first-excited-node* ($n_r=1$) $l=0$ interior solution reproduces the experimental 12C ground-state alpha-separation energy, $E=-7.367$ MeV, from the alpha+8Be threshold |
| $l(l+1)\hbar^2/2\mu R^2$ (interior) / $l(l+1)\hbar^2/2\mu r^2$ (exterior) | centrifugal barrier; interior uses the constant-shift approximation that keeps the problem exactly solvable (Part II), exterior uses the exact $1/r^2$ form | not tuned; both follow directly from $l$ and the already-fixed $R,\mu$ |
| $n_r=0$ interior solutions | the lowest-node solutions of this potential for each $l$ | **not** identified with any physical 12C level (see "Disadvantages" below) — they are treated as Pauli-forbidden, following the Wildermuth–Tang counting for the 3-alpha cluster structure of 12C |

Why $V_0$ is comparatively *shallow* (21.75 MeV, versus the $\sim$60–80 MeV
typical of a single-nucleon Woods–Saxon well): a single real potential for a
composite *alpha*+*8Be* pair, with no explicit antisymmetrization of the 12
nucleons, necessarily supports spurious deeply-bound solutions at low node
number that have no physical counterpart (they would place two alpha
particles inside each other's already-occupied 0s shell). Rather than
performing an explicit Pauli projection (e.g. an orthogonalizing
pseudopotential), the simplest and most common workaround in this class of
single-channel cluster model — adopted here — is to lower $V_0$ until the
node one wants to call physical, $n_r=1$, sits at the correct energy, and to
simply discard $n_r=0$ as unphysical. This is a deliberate modeling choice,
not a numerical accident, and it is why $V_0$ here is roughly a factor of
3 shallower than a bare single-particle well of the same $R,a$.

### Advantages of this modeling choice

- **Closed-form, not fitted node-by-node.** Because the interior solution is
  the exact Gauss hypergeometric function of Part II for *any* $V_0,R,a$, only
  one number ($V_0$) needed tuning to fix the entire bound/quasi-bound/
  scattering spectrum simultaneously — the same interior solution, matched at
  the same $R$, produces every bound state, every resonance, and every
  scattering phase shift shown in this report.
- **Correct qualitative threshold physics.** By construction the model
  reproduces the single most important number in the 3-alpha problem — the
  12C ground-state alpha-separation energy — which controls the sign and
  magnitude of essentially all reaction-rate physics at this threshold.
- **Transparent, auditable parameters.** All three numbers ($V_0,R,a$) are
  physical (a depth in MeV, a radius and diffuseness in fm), rather than
  abstract fit coefficients, so the potential can be directly compared with
  other alpha-cluster potentials in the literature.
- **Analytic, so exactly reproducible.** Because no shooting, discretization,
  or variational method is used anywhere (Parts I–VII), the same $(V_0,R,a)$
  always yields bit-for-bit the same spectrum; there is no numerical-method
  dependence to control for.

### Disadvantages / limitations of this modeling choice

- **No explicit Pauli projection.** Discarding the $n_r=0$ solutions "by
  hand" (rather than projecting them out with an orthogonalizing
  pseudopotential or a microscopic antisymmetrized treatment) is a
  well-known simplification; it fixes the *lowest* $l=0$ physical state
  correctly by construction, but offers no guarantee for excited states or
  other partial waves, whose node-counting/Pauli-allowedness is not enforced.
- **A single, $l$- and parity-independent $V_0$ cannot fit every resonance.**
  The literature on this exact alpha+8Be system finds that a
  parity-dependent (odd-$l$ vs. even-$l$), even double-hump radial potential
  is needed to reproduce the excited 12C resonances quantitatively; the
  single real potential used here is deliberately simpler, and this shows up
  directly in the Sec. 10 experiment-comparison table as order-of-magnitude
  (rather than percent-level) agreement for excited states and widths.
- **Cannot reproduce the Hoyle state's extreme narrowness.** The astrophysical
  0+ Hoyle resonance is only $\sim$0.3 MeV above threshold yet has a width of
  order $10^{-6}$ MeV — a degree of fine-tuned barrier penetrability that a
  single, once-tuned real potential of this type does not, and is not
  expected to, reproduce; matching it requires either a potential re-tuned
  specifically to the near-threshold region, or a genuinely microscopic
  (GCM/OCM/ab-initio) treatment of the 3-alpha problem.
- **8Be is treated as a stable two-body partner.** 8Be's own ground state is
  itself an alpha+alpha resonance (decay energy 91.8 keV); it is treated here
  as a stable particle, an excellent approximation given its own width
  (5.57 eV) is negligible on the MeV scales considered, but it is an
  approximation nonetheless, and would need to be relaxed for any
  three-body-continuum treatment of the full 3-alpha problem.
- **One depth, many observables.** Tuning $V_0$ to a single datum (the
  ground-state energy) leaves no additional free parameter to independently
  fix the *width* of any resonance, or the depth of any other bound state;
  all such predictions are genuine, unadjusted outputs of the model and
  should be read as such rather than as fitted results.

## Part IX — Breit–Wigner Comparison of Computed Cross Sections with Experimental Widths

This part introduces nothing new about the boundary-value problem itself;
it is a standard, independent diagnostic, built by combining the
already-defined $\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}(E)$ of Eq. (VII.1) with data
external to the model, and used only in the scattering-vs-experiment
section of the generated report.

### The single-level formula

Sufficiently close to an isolated Siegert pole $k_n=k_{r,n}-ik_{i,n}$
(Part IV), the exact partial-wave S-matrix (V.3) is dominated by that
single pole, and Eq. (VII.1) reduces to the textbook single-level
Breit–Wigner elastic cross section,

$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}(E)\;\approx\;\sigma_{\mathrm{BW},l}(E)=\frac{\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)\,\frac{(\Gamma/2)^2}{(E-E_R)^2+(\Gamma/2)^2}, \tag{IX.1}
$$

whenever the resonance is elastic-dominated, $\Gamma_{\rm el}\approx\Gamma_{\rm tot}\equiv\Gamma$
(no significant competing decay channel), and background (non-resonant
potential) scattering in that partial wave is small. Equation (IX.1) is
the standard near-pole limit of the exact partial-wave cross section and
is not itself re-derived from scratch here; it is used purely as an
independent, external comparison curve, built from experimental
$(E_R,\Gamma)$ rather than from anything computed by the matching theory.

### Why $\Gamma_{\rm el}\approx\Gamma_{\rm tot}$ is justified for every alpha+8Be resonance considered

The alpha+8Be breakup channel is the *lowest* particle-decay threshold of
12C. The next particle thresholds, $p+^{11}$B and $n+^{11}$C, sit at
$E_x\approx15.96$ and $18.72$ MeV respectively — far above every
resonance in the EXPERIMENT_12C table (the highest listed, the broad
$0^+_3$, sits at $E_x=10.3$ MeV). Hence for the $3^-_1$, $2^+_2$, $1^-_1$
and $0^+_3$ resonances, alpha+8Be emission is essentially the *only* open
decay channel, and $\Gamma_{\rm el}\approx\Gamma_{\rm tot}$ to excellent
approximation. The Hoyle state ($0^+_2$) is the sole exception with a
second, radiative channel ($^{12}$C$\to{}^{12}$C$+\gamma$), but that
branch is minute, $\Gamma_{\rm rad}/\Gamma_{\rm tot}\sim4\times10^{-4}$,
so $\Gamma_{\rm el}\approx\Gamma_{\rm tot}$ remains an excellent
approximation there too.

### What the comparison does and does not test

Equation (IX.1), evaluated at the *experimental* $(E_R,\Gamma)$, is
compared directly against the *model's own* $\sigma_{\mathrm{el},l}(E)$
(Eq. VII.1) computed from the fitted potential of Part VIII, over a local
energy window around each experimental resonance. Because (IX.1) uses
only experimental numbers, this comparison is sensitive to two genuinely
independent questions at once: (i) does the model reproduce the correct
*peak strength* implied by a resonance of that width, and (ii) does the
model's nuclear phase shift $\delta_l(E)$, evaluated at the experimental
$E_R$ (not the model's own, possibly different, pole energy), pass near
$90^\circ\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 180^\circ)$ — the model-independent hallmark of
an isolated elastic resonance, $\tan\delta_l\to\infty$ at $E=E_R$ when
$\Gamma_{\rm el}=\Gamma_{\rm tot}$. Neither check requires the model's own
pole search (Part IV) to have found a pole at exactly $E_R$; both can fail
independently, and each failure mode is diagnostic of a different
limitation of the single real Woods–Saxon potential documented in Part VIII.

_MATH_RE = re.compile(r"(\${1,2})(.+?)\1", re.DOTALL)


def markdown_to_html_mathsafe(md_text: str) -> str:
    """Convert markdown to HTML while shielding $...$/$$...$$ spans from the
    markdown engine (which would otherwise mangle underscores inside LaTeX
    as emphasis markers). MathJax renders the restored spans client-side.

    Math spans are restored with '&', '<', '>' HTML-escaped. This is
    essential, not cosmetic: inequalities like "r<R" or "r\\ge R" are
    common in this document's LaTeX, and a literal, un-escaped '<'
    dropped back into the HTML is indistinguishable to the browser's own
    HTML parser from the start of a tag -- the parser runs and builds the
    DOM *before* MathJax ever gets to look at the text, so an unescaped
    '<' can silently swallow the rest of the equation (and, in bad cases,
    surrounding markup) into a bogus/invalid element and hide it from
    display, well before MathJax has any chance to render it. The browser
    HTML-decodes '&lt;'/'&gt;'/'&amp;' back to literal '<'/'>'/'&' in the
    resulting text node, so MathJax still sees the correct raw TeX."""
    placeholders = []

    def stash(m):
        placeholders.append(m.group(0))
        return f"@@MATH{len(placeholders) - 1}@@"

    shielded = _MATH_RE.sub(stash, md_text)
    html = md_lib.markdown(shielded, extensions=["extra", "tables", "sane_lists"])
    for i, tex in enumerate(placeholders):
        tex_safe = tex.replace("&", "&amp;").replace("<", "&lt;").replace(">", "&gt;")
        html = html.replace(f"@@MATH{i}@@", tex_safe)
    return html

    self-contained and no longer reads an external .md file at runtime."""
    if md_text is None:
