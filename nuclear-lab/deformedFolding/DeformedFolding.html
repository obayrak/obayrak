Heavy-ion optical potentials

The double folding model

The real part of the nucleus–nucleus optical potential, built from the ground-state densities of both nuclei and an effective nucleon–nucleon interaction. This page explains the formalism for spherical and axially deformed nuclei, computes the potential in your browser with several independent numerical methods, and benchmarks the results against BiFold, DFPOT and closed-form solutions.

The folding integral

$$U_F(R)=\int\!\!\int \rho_P(\mathbf r_1)\,\rho_T(\mathbf r_2)\,v_{NN}(\mathbf s,\rho,E)\,d^3r_1\,d^3r_2$$

The antisymmetrised potential splits into a direct term and a knock-on exchange term:

$$U_F = U_D + U_{EX}$$

For spherical densities the convolution theorem reduces the 6-D integral to one-dimensional Fourier–Bessel transforms:

$$U_D(R)=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int_0^\infty \tilde\rho_P(q)\,\tilde\rho_T(q)\,\tilde v_D(q)\,j_0(qR)\,q^2\,dq$$
$$\tilde f(q)=4\pi\int_0^\infty f(r)\,j_0(qr)\,r^2\,dr$$

Effective NN interaction: M3Y

The M3Y interaction (Bertsch et al. 1977) is a sum of Yukawa terms fitted to G-matrix elements of the Reid or Paris NN potential. With the BiFold/DFPOT convention \(Y(r)=\frac{V_0}{a}\frac{e^{-ar}}{r}\), the transform is analytic: \(\tilde Y(q)=\frac{4\pi V_0}{a\,(a^2+q^2)}\).

Zero-range exchange

The knock-on exchange is replaced by a pseudo-potential \(\hat J_{00}(E)\,\delta(\mathbf s)\), so \(U_{EX}\) becomes a single convolution of the two densities:

$$\hat J_{00}^{\rm Reid}=-276\,(1-0.005\,E/A_P)\ \ {\rm MeV\,fm^3}$$
$$\hat J_{00}^{\rm Paris}=-590\,(1-0.002\,E/A_P)\ \ {\rm MeV\,fm^3}$$
where
\(\hat J_{00}(E)\)
strength (volume integral) of the zero-range exchange pseudo-potential, MeV fm³
\(\delta(\mathbf s)\)
Dirac delta: the exchange acts only when the two nucleons are at the same point
\(E\)
laboratory bombarding energy (MeV); \(E/A_P\) is the energy per projectile nucleon
\(A_P\)
projectile mass number

The exchange potential is then a single overlap integral of the two densities:

$$U_{EX}^{ZR}(R)=\hat J_{00}(E)\int\rho_P(\mathbf r)\,\rho_T(\mathbf R-\mathbf r)\,d^3r$$

Finite-range exchange (local approximation)

Using the Campi–Bouyssy density matrix \(\rho(\mathbf r,\mathbf r+\mathbf s)\approx\rho(r)\,\hat j_1(k_F(r)s)\):

$$U_{EX}(R)=4\pi\!\int_0^\infty\! G(R,s)\,v_{EX}(s)\,j_0\!\Big(\frac{k(R)s}{M}\Big)s^2ds$$
$$k^2(R)=\frac{2m_uM}{\hbar^2}\big[E_{cm}-U_D(R)-U_{EX}(R)-V_C(R)\big]$$
where
\(G(R,s)\)
overlap of the two mixed densities: \(G=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int q^2j_0(qR)F_P(q,s)F_T(q,s)\,dq\), with \(F(q,s)=4\pi\int\rho(r)\,\hat j_1(k_F(r)s)\,j_0(qr)\,r^2dr\)
\(\hat j_1(x)\)
\(3(\sin x-x\cos x)/x^3\), the Slater function of the density-matrix expansion
\(k_F(r)\)
local Fermi momentum of the nucleus at radius \(r\) (fm⁻¹)
\(v_{EX}(s)\)
exchange part of the M3Y interaction (sum of Yukawa terms, MeV)
\(k(R)\)
local momentum of relative motion (fm⁻¹); it depends on \(U_{EX}\) itself, so the equation is solved self-consistently
\(M=\frac{A_PA_T}{A_P+A_T}\)
reduced mass number
\(m_u\)
atomic mass unit, 931.494 MeV/c²; \(\hbar c=197.327\) MeV fm
\(E_{cm}=E\,\frac{A_T}{A_P+A_T}\)
centre-of-mass energy (MeV)
\(V_C(R)\)
Coulomb potential (see below)
and in kF
\(C_S\)
surface coefficient, 1/4 or 1/36
\(\nabla\rho,\ \nabla^2\rho\)
gradient and Laplacian of the density; they correct \(k_F\) in the nuclear surface

Because \(k(R)\) contains \(U_{EX}(R)\) itself, this is a nonlinear equation at every \(R\). The local Fermi momentum includes surface corrections: \(k_F^2=\big(\tfrac{3\pi^2}{2}\rho\big)^{2/3}+\tfrac{5C_S(\nabla\rho)^2}{3\rho^2}+\tfrac{5\nabla^2\rho}{36\rho}\).

Density dependence

Medium effects enter through a factor multiplying the M3Y interaction, evaluated in the frozen-density approximation \(\rho=\rho_P+\rho_T\):

$$v(\rho,E,s)=g(E)\,F(\rho)\,v(s),\qquad F(\rho)=C\,\big[1+\alpha e^{-\beta\rho}-\gamma\rho^{\,n}\big],\qquad g(E)=1-c_E\,E/A_P$$
where
\(v(s)\)
the density-independent M3Y interaction (Reid or Paris)
\(\rho=\rho_P(\mathbf r_1)+\rho_T(\mathbf r_2)\)
local density felt by the nucleon pair (frozen-density approximation), fm⁻³
\(F(\rho)\)
density-dependent factor that reproduces nuclear-matter saturation
\(C,\ \alpha,\ \beta,\ \gamma,\ n\)
parameters of each form (table below); \(\beta\) in fm³, \(\gamma\) in fm³ⁿ
\(g(E)\)
linear energy-dependence factor
\(c_E\)
0.002 for Reid, 0.003 for Paris

Here \(c_E=0.002\) for Reid and \(0.003\) for Paris. Because \(e^{-\beta(\rho_P+\rho_T)}=e^{-\beta\rho_P}e^{-\beta\rho_T}\) and \((\rho_P+\rho_T)^n\) expands binomially, every integer-\(n\) form separates into a short sum of density-independent foldings. This code builds that sum automatically.

Parameters from Khoa, Satchler & von Oertzen, PRC 56 (1997) 954 and Khoa & von Oertzen, PLB 342 (1995) 6, as tabulated in BiFold. K is the nuclear-matter incompressibility each form produces.

Coulomb potential

The Coulomb interaction between the two nuclei is added to the nuclear folding potential. Here \(\rho\) in the Coulomb formulas is the charge density \(\rho_{ch}(r)\) of each nucleus, in units of e fm⁻³. It is normalised to the nuclear charge, not the mass number:

$$\int \rho_{ch}(\mathbf r)\,d^3r = 4\pi\int_0^\infty \rho_{ch}(r)\,r^2dr = Z$$
where
\(\rho_{ch}(r)\)
charge density: the distribution of the nucleus's electric charge, in units of the proton charge e per fm³. It is carried by the protons, so it integrates to \(Z\), not \(A\)
\(Z\)
proton number (atomic number) of the nucleus
\(r\)
distance from the centre of that nucleus (fm)

The matter density \(\rho(r)=\rho_n(r)+\rho_p(r)\) used in the nuclear folding is normalised to \(A\). The charge density can be obtained from it in three ways, in increasing order of accuracy:

  • Scaled matter density (used by both calculators): \(\rho_{ch}(r)=\dfrac{Z}{A}\,\rho(r)\). This assumes protons and neutrons have the same shape.
  • Point-proton density: \(\rho_{ch}(r)=\rho_p(r)\), for example the proton column of a RIPL-3 HFB table, normalised to \(Z\).
  • Folded with the proton charge form factor: \(\tilde\rho_{ch}(q)=\tilde\rho_p(q)\,G_E^p(q)\), with \(G_E^p(q)\approx e^{-q^2a_p^2/4}\) and \(a_p\simeq0.65\) fm. This reproduces the measured charge radius, \(\langle r^2\rangle_{ch}\approx\langle r^2\rangle_p+0.77\) fm².

Outside the region where the two charge clouds overlap, every choice gives the point-charge result \(Z_PZ_Te^2/R\). The choice only matters at small \(R\), well inside the barrier. Throughout, \(e^2=\hbar c/137.036=1.439964\) MeV fm.

Double-folded Coulomb potential

The Coulomb potential is a double folding with \(v(s)=e^2/s\) in place of the NN interaction:

$$V_C(R)=\int\!\!\int \rho^{P}_{ch}(\mathbf r_1)\,\rho^{T}_{ch}(\mathbf r_2)\,\frac{e^2}{|\mathbf R+\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1|}\,d^3r_1\,d^3r_2$$
where
\(V_C(R)\)
Coulomb potential energy between the two nuclei (MeV)
\(\rho^{P}_{ch},\ \rho^{T}_{ch}\)
charge densities of projectile and target, normalised to \(Z_P\) and \(Z_T\); in both calculators \(\rho^{P,T}_{ch}=(Z/A)\,\rho_{P,T}\)
\(e^2\)
squared elementary charge, \(e^2=\hbar c/137.036=1.439964\) MeV fm
\(|\mathbf R+\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1|\)
distance between two charge elements, one in each nucleus

Step 1: potential of the target charge cloud. For a spherical distribution, Gauss's law gives the electrostatic potential at distance \(x\) from the target centre:

$$\phi_T(x)=4\pi e^2\Big[\frac1x\int_0^x\rho^{T}_{ch}(r)\,r^2dr+\int_x^\infty\rho^{T}_{ch}(r)\,r\,dr\Big]$$
where
\(\phi_T(x)\)
potential energy (MeV) of a unit charge at distance \(x\) from the target centre
\(x\)
distance from the target centre (fm)
\(4\pi\int_0^x\rho^T_{ch}r^2dr\)
charge enclosed inside radius \(x\)

The first term is the charge enclosed inside \(x\), acting like a point charge; the second is the outer shells, each contributing a constant potential. For \(x\) beyond the charge cloud, \(\phi_T(x)\to Z_Te^2/x\).

Step 2: fold with the projectile. With bipolar coordinates the remaining 3-D integral becomes one-dimensional:

$$V_C(R)=\frac{2\pi}{R}\int_0^\infty \rho^{P}_{ch}(r)\,r\,\big[Y(R+r)-Y(|R-r|)\big]\,dr$$
$$Y(y)=\int_0^y x\,\phi_T(x)\,dx$$
where
\(r\)
distance of a projectile charge element from the projectile centre (fm)
\(Y(y)\)
running integral of \(x\,\phi_T(x)\), in MeV fm², tabulated once
\(R+r,\ |R-r|\)
largest and smallest distance between that charge element and the target centre, over all orientations

Equivalent momentum-space form. Because the Fourier transform of \(1/s\) is \(4\pi/q^2\):

$$V_C(R)=\frac{2e^2}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \tilde\rho^{P}_{ch}(q)\,\tilde\rho^{T}_{ch}(q)\,j_0(qR)\,dq$$
where
\(\tilde\rho^{P,T}_{ch}(q)\)
charge form factors (Fourier–Bessel transforms of the charge densities)

Here \(\tilde\rho(q)=4\pi\int\rho(r)j_0(qr)r^2dr\) and \(\tilde\rho(0)=Z\). The \(1/q^2\) cancels the \(q^2\) of the volume element, so the integrand stays finite at \(q=0\).

Numerics in this code. Steps 1 and 2 use composite Gauss–Legendre quadrature (8 points per 0.5 fm panel). \(Y(y)\) is accumulated on a 0.01 fm grid and interpolated with cubic Hermite polynomials. The result is checked against \(Z_PZ_Te^2/R\) at large \(R\).

Uniformly charged sphere

The usual approximation treats the projectile as a point charge moving through the field of a uniformly charged sphere of radius \(R_C\):

$$\rho_{ch}(r)=\frac{3Z}{4\pi R_C^3}\,\Theta(R_C-r)$$
where
\(\Theta(R_C-r)\)
step function: 1 inside the sphere (\(r\lt R_C\)), 0 outside
\(R_C\)
radius of the uniformly charged sphere (fm)
\(3Z/4\pi R_C^3\)
constant charge density, so that the total charge is \(Z\)
$$V_C(R)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{Z_PZ_Te^2}{2R_C}\Big(3-\dfrac{R^2}{R_C^2}\Big), & R\lt R_C\\[2ex] \dfrac{Z_PZ_Te^2}{R}, & R\ge R_C\end{cases}$$
where
\(Z_P,\ Z_T\)
proton numbers of projectile and target
\(R\)
centre-to-centre distance (fm)

This form comes directly from \(\phi(x)\) on the left with a constant density. Inside the sphere the potential is a parabola, outside it is the point-charge potential. The potential and its first derivative are both continuous at \(R=R_C\), and \(V_C(0)=\tfrac32\,Z_PZ_Te^2/R_C\).

The Coulomb radius is taken as

$$R_C=r_c\,\big(A_P^{1/3}+A_T^{1/3}\big)$$
where
\(r_c\)
Coulomb radius parameter (fm), the "rc coefficient" in the calculator
\(A_P,\ A_T\)
mass numbers of projectile and target

with \(r_c\) typically 1.2–1.4 fm (this is the "rc coefficient" in the calculator). Alternatively, \(R_C\) can be matched to a measured rms charge radius, since a uniform sphere has \(\langle r^2\rangle=\tfrac35R_C^2\), so \(R_C=\sqrt{5/3}\,r_{rms}\).

Example. For α + ⁴⁰Ca with \(r_c=1.2\) fm: \(R_C=1.2\,(4^{1/3}+40^{1/3})=6.008\) fm. Then \(V_C(0)=\tfrac32\cdot 2\cdot20\cdot1.439964/6.008=14.38\) MeV, which is the VC(0) value in the calculator's potential table. At \(R=10\) fm, \(V_C=5.76\) MeV.

Which to use? The two forms differ only where the nuclei overlap, \(R\lesssim R_C\). For α + ⁴⁰Ca (Gaussian α, 2pF ⁴⁰Ca, \(\rho_{ch}=(Z/A)\rho\), \(r_c=1.2\) fm) the folded potential is 20.16 MeV at \(R=0\), against 14.38 MeV for the sphere. The difference falls to 0.3 % at 6 fm and 0.01 % at the barrier radius, about 8 fm. The folded form matters for the potential in the nuclear interior, for example in finite-range exchange, where \(V_C(R)\) enters the local momentum \(k(R)\).

Derived observables

  • Barrier height \(V_B\), radius \(R_B\) and curvature \(\hbar\omega=\hbar\sqrt{-V''(R_B)/\mu}\) of \(U_F+V_C+\frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu R^2}\).
  • Volume integral per nucleon pair \(J_V/(A_PA_T)=\frac{4\pi}{A_PA_T}\int U(R)R^2dR\), and the rms radius of the potential.
  • A Woods–Saxon fit \(-V_0/[1+e^{(R-R_0)/a}]\) by Levenberg–Marquardt, for use in codes that need a parametrised form.

Deformed nuclei

For axially deformed nuclei the potential depends on the orientation of both symmetry axes, \(\hat e_P\) and \(\hat e_T\), relative to \(\hat R\). The Deformed densities tab shows the multipole expansion of one deformed nucleus. The Deformed calculator tab expands both densities in multipoles about their own axes, folds every pair of multipoles once, and rebuilds \(U\) for any orientation from a short table of radial functions. It has its own nuclei, interaction and numerics, and its density models are those of the Deformed densities tab, which can send the density it shows to the projectile or the target. With both nuclei spherical the result equals the spherical potential of the Calculator tab, which runs the spherical solver unchanged.

Multipole expansion of a deformed density

$$\rho(\mathbf r)=\sum_\lambda \rho_\lambda(r)\,Y_{\lambda0}(\hat r\cdot\hat e),\qquad \rho_\lambda(r)=2\pi\!\int_{-1}^{1}\!\rho(r,\theta')\,Y_{\lambda0}(\theta')\,d\cos\theta'$$

The shapes are deformed in the radius, \(R(\theta')=R_0\,[1+\sum_\lambda\beta_\lambda Y_{\lambda0}(\theta')]\), or in the diffuseness, with \(\beta_2,\beta_3,\beta_4,\beta_6\). The \(\theta'\) integral uses Gauss–Legendre nodes, and \(\lambda_{max}\) is set by a tolerance on the reconstructed density at off-node angles. A spherical density has \(\rho_0=\sqrt{4\pi}\,\rho\) and no other multipoles.

$$\tilde\rho(\mathbf q)=\sqrt{4\pi}\sum_\lambda(-i)^\lambda\,\tilde\rho_\lambda(q)\,Y_{\lambda0}(\hat q\cdot\hat e),\qquad \tilde\rho_\lambda(q)=\sqrt{4\pi}\!\int_0^\infty\!\rho_\lambda(r)\,j_\lambda(qr)\,r^2dr$$

So \(\tilde\rho_0\) is the spherical form factor used above, \(\tilde\rho_0(0)=A\), and \(\tilde\rho_\lambda(q)\propto q^\lambda\) as \(q\to0\). The intrinsic moments and effective deformations are

$$Q_\lambda=\int_0^\infty r^{\lambda+2}\rho_\lambda(r)\,dr,\qquad \beta_\lambda^{\rm eff}=\frac{4\pi\,Q_\lambda}{3A\,\bar R^{\lambda}},\quad \bar R=\sqrt{5/3}\;r_{rms},\qquad Q_0=\sqrt{16\pi/5}\;Q_2$$

Odd \(\beta_\lambda\) move the centre of mass, \(\langle z\rangle=Q_1/(A\sqrt{3/4\pi})\). With the c.m. correction (on by default) a \(\beta_1\) is solved so that \(Q_1=0\).

The Deformed densities tab plots \(\rho(x,z')\) in a plane through the axis, the \(\rho_\lambda(r)\) with their first-order forms \(-\beta_\lambda R_0\,d\rho/dr\), the moments \(Q_\lambda\) and \(\beta_\lambda^{\rm eff}\), and the convergence of the multipole sum at off-node angles.

Coupling the two nuclei

The convolution theorem turns the folding integral into a finite sum over the multipoles of the two nuclei:

$$U(R;\hat e_P,\hat e_T)=\sum_{\lambda_P\lambda_T L} U_{\lambda_P\lambda_T;L}(R)\;A_{\lambda_P\lambda_T;L}(\theta_P,\theta_T,\phi)$$
$$U_{\lambda_P\lambda_T;L}(R)=\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int_0^\infty \tilde v(q)\,\tilde\rho^P_{\lambda_P}(q)\,\tilde\rho^T_{\lambda_T}(q)\,j_L(qR)\,q^2dq$$
$$A_{\lambda_P\lambda_T;L}=4\pi(2L+1)\,(-1)^{\frac{\lambda_P-\lambda_T+L}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_P&\lambda_T&L\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\sum_{\mu\ge0}(2-\delta_{\mu0})(-1)^\mu\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_P&\lambda_T&L\\\mu&-\mu&0\end{pmatrix}Y_{\lambda_P\mu}(\theta_P,0)\,Y_{\lambda_T\mu}(\theta_T,0)\cos\mu\phi$$

Only terms with \(\lambda_P+\lambda_T+L\) even and \(|\lambda_P-\lambda_T|\le L\le\lambda_P+\lambda_T\) contribute. \(\mathbf R=\mathbf R_P-\mathbf R_T\) points from the target to the projectile; \(\theta_P\) and \(\theta_T\) are measured from \(+\hat R\), and \(\phi\) is the azimuth of \(\hat e_T\) relative to \(\hat e_P\). At \(\theta_T=0\) the target tip faces the projectile; the projectile tip faces the target at \(\theta_P=180^\circ\). Special cases:

$$A_{00;0}=1,\qquad A_{0\lambda;\lambda}=\sqrt{4\pi}\,Y_{\lambda0}(\theta_T),\qquad A_{\lambda0;\lambda}=(-1)^\lambda\sqrt{4\pi}\,Y_{\lambda0}(\theta_P)$$

\(U_{00;0}\) is the potential of the orientation-averaged densities \(\rho_0/\sqrt{4\pi}\), and the average of \(U\) over both orientations is exactly \(U_{00;0}\). Zero-range exchange uses the same table with \(\tilde v(q)\to\hat J_{00}(E)\).

Density dependence: nonlinear projection

The density-dependent factor is used in separable form, \(\rho_P\rho_T\,F(\rho_P+\rho_T)=\sum_k c_k\,g^P_k(\rho_P)\,g^T_k(\rho_T)\): the exponential factorises, and \((\rho_P+\rho_T)^n\) is expanded binomially. Each \(g_k\) is applied to the deformed density point by point, and only then projected:

$$g_{k,\lambda}(r)=2\pi\int_{-1}^{1} g_k\big(\rho(r,\theta')\big)\,Y_{\lambda0}(\theta')\,d\cos\theta'\qquad\big(\text{not } g_k(\rho_\lambda)\big)$$

Every separable term therefore has its own multipoles and its own \(\lambda_{max}\), and the coupling formula is applied term by term. The orientation dependence of the density dependence is then exact within the frozen-density approximation. Tip and side orientations probe different overlap densities at the same \(R\), so the ratio of the DDM3Y1 and M3Y potentials depends on the orientation; for Benchmark Case 6 at \(R=10\) fm it runs from 0.888 to 1.013.

Coulomb multipoles

The charge densities are \((Z/A)\,\rho_\lambda\). They are coupled with the same angular factors, with \(4\pi e^2/q^2\) in place of \(\tilde v(q)\):

$$V^C_{\lambda_P\lambda_T;L}(R)=\frac{2e^2}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \tilde\rho^{P,ch}_{\lambda_P}(q)\,\tilde\rho^{T,ch}_{\lambda_T}(q)\,j_L(qR)\,dq$$

The integrand is regular at \(q=0\) because \(\tilde\rho_\lambda\propto q^\lambda\). Outside the overlap only \(L=\lambda_P+\lambda_T\) survives, and that term falls as \(R^{-(L+1)}\). For a spherical projectile, at first order in \(\beta\) with a sharp surface:

$$V^C_\lambda(R)=\frac{3Z_PZ_Te^2R_c^\lambda\beta_\lambda}{(2\lambda+1)\,R^{\lambda+1}}\,Y_{\lambda0}(\theta_T),\qquad R>R_P+R_T$$

With the uniform-sphere option the Coulomb potential stays spherical (monopole only).

Finite-range exchange with a deformed local Fermi momentum

The local Fermi momentum of each nucleus is evaluated on the deformed density, with the full 3-D gradient and Laplacian:

$$k_F^2(\mathbf r)=\Big(\tfrac{3\pi^2}{2}\rho\Big)^{2/3}+\frac{5C_S}{3}\frac{|\nabla\rho|^2}{\rho^2}+\frac{5}{36}\frac{\nabla^2\rho}{\rho}$$
$$|\nabla\rho|^2=\rho_r^2+\frac{\rho_\theta^2}{r^2},\qquad \nabla^2\rho=\rho_{rr}+\frac{2}{r}\rho_r+\frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\,\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,\rho_\theta)$$

The products \(\rho(\mathbf r)\,\hat j_1(k_F(\mathbf r)s)\) are projected on multipoles for every \((r,s)\), so \(G(R,s)\) has the same two-centre multipole expansion as the direct term. The local momentum is solved self-consistently at every \(R\) and orientation:

$$U_{EX}=4\pi\!\int_0^\infty\! G(R,s;\hat e_P,\hat e_T)\,v_{EX}(s)\,j_0\!\Big(\frac{k(R)s}{M}\Big)s^2ds,\qquad k^2(R)=\frac{2m_uM}{\hbar^2}\big[E_{cm}-U_D-U_{EX}-V_C\big]$$

For speed, \(j_0(\kappa s)\) is expanded in Chebyshev polynomials of \(\kappa\in[0,\kappa_{cap}]\). Each multipole term then becomes a short table in \(\kappa\), and a new orientation costs one sum and one secant solve per \(R\). Because \(k(R)\) depends on the orientation, \(U_{EX}\) is not linear in the multipoles, and its orientation average is not \(U_{EX;00;0}\).

Convention note. The spherical solver, like BiFold, uses \(\rho''\) where the formula has \(\nabla^2\rho=\rho''+2\rho'/r\). The deformed solver offers both forms, with the full Laplacian as the default. For Cases 3 and 4 the two forms differ by 2.0 and 2.9 MeV in \(U_{EX}\) near \(R=0\).

Shortcuts, measured. Each new orientation uses the exact local \(k_F(\mathbf r)\) and solves \(k(R)\) again; the Chebyshev tables reproduce the direct evaluation of \(G\) to \(10^{-12}\) MeV, so they are not an approximation. The spherical solver takes 11.5 s on the same grids. S1 costs 1–2 % in \(U_{EX}\) at 8 fm and is not used. S2 is used where an exact result would need an orientation average over both nuclei: the multipole table, the \(U_{000}\) curve and the first-order test of finite-range runs, and the rotor form factors when both nuclei are deformed. For the rotor form factors of this system its error in \(F_k^{EX}\) is 0.06–0.19 MeV between 10 and 14 fm, but 3.4 MeV at 8 fm and up to 9 MeV over \(R\ge4\) fm for \(k=2\), and its relative size grows with \(k\), because the orientation dependence of \(k(R)\) has multipoles of its own. With one deformed nucleus the form factors are projected exactly.

Coupled-channel form factors

For coupled channels with one deformed nucleus (rotor, 0⁺ partner), the partner is averaged over its orientations, and the potential is expanded about the axis of the deformed nucleus:

$$\langle U\rangle(R,\theta')=\sum_k F_k(R)\,\sqrt{4\pi}\,Y_{k0}(\theta')=\sum_k F_k(R)\sqrt{2k+1}\,P_k(\cos\theta')$$

Here \(\theta'\) is the angle between the symmetry axis and the direction to the partner. From the special cases of \(A\), \(F_k=U_{0k;k}\) for a deformed target and \(F_k=U_{k0;k}\) for a deformed projectile; the factor \((-1)^k\) turns \(\theta_P\) into \(\theta'=\pi-\theta_P\). For finite-range exchange with a spherical partner, \(F_k\) is the Gauss–Legendre projection of the exact \(U_{EX}(R,\theta')\). At first order a deformed Woods–Saxon gives

$$F_k(R)\simeq-\frac{\delta_k}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\,\frac{dU_0}{dR},\qquad \delta_k=\beta_kR_0$$

This is FRESCO's SHAPE = 10 form, which is why the Deformed calculator writes \(F_k\) for TYPE = 10/11, SHAPE = 7 with \(P(k)=N_R\). For ¹⁶O + ²⁰⁸Pb with \(\beta_2=0.01\), the folded \(F_2\) is 0.925–0.953 times this estimate at \(R=10\)–13 fm. The reaction-code panel of the Deformed calculator lists the input conventions that have not been checked against a FRESCO or ECIS run. With both nuclei deformed, the exchange part of \(F_k\) uses \(k(R)\) from the monopole problem; its measured error is given in the finite-range panel.

Numerical analysis

Robust numerics for folding integrals

BiFold and DFPOT each use one quadrature scheme on one mesh. This solver implements several independent routes to the same integral, so each result can be checked against the others and against closed-form cases.

1 · Momentum space (primary)

Density form factors \(\tilde\rho(q)\) are computed once per nucleus. NN form factors are analytic for Yukawa and Gaussian terms, which removes the \(1/r\) singularity at the origin. Mesh-based codes have to integrate that singularity numerically. The back-transform to \(R\) uses the same quadrature.

  • Composite Gauss–Legendre on 0.25 fm panels with 8 nodes, exact for polynomials of degree 15 on each panel. This is the default.
  • Composite Simpson on a uniform mesh (the BiFold scheme), O(h⁴).
  • Filon–Simpson for the oscillatory \(\sin(qr)\) kernel, which stays accurate when \(qh\) is not small.

2 · Coordinate space (independent check)

The 6-D integral is reduced with bipolar coordinates, without any Fourier transform:

$$\Phi_T(x)=\frac{2\pi}{x}\!\int_0^\infty\!\rho_T(r)\,r\,[W(x{+}r)-W(|x{-}r|)]\,dr,\quad W(s)=\!\int_0^s\!t\,v(t)\,dt$$
$$U(R)=\frac{2\pi}{R}\!\int_0^\infty\!\rho_P(r)\,r\,[Y(R{+}r)-Y(|R{-}r|)]\,dr,\quad Y(y)=\!\int_0^y\!x\,\Phi_T(x)\,dx$$

\(W\) is analytic for Yukawa, Gaussian and Coulomb forces. The inner integral is split at the kink \(r=x\) so that Gauss–Legendre keeps its full order. \(Y\) is accumulated with 6-point Gauss rules and evaluated by cubic Hermite interpolation.

3 · Self-consistent exchange

BiFold iterates \(U_{EX}\leftarrow\Phi(U_{EX})\) a fixed number of times (8 by default). Here each \(R\) is solved as the scalar root problem \(h(U)=U-\Phi(U)=0\):

  • secant (quasi-Newton) updates, warm-started from the converged value at the previous mesh point;
  • a Picard step as a safeguard when an update is non-finite or jumps more than 50 MeV;
  • stopping at \(|h|<10^{-10}\max(1,|U|)\). The run reports the mean iteration count and the largest residual.

4 · Error control

  • Mesh doubling. The calculator runs every case twice, with the step halved and the cut-offs widened by a quarter on the second run. The largest difference is reported as an error estimate; for Simpson it is also Richardson-extrapolated, \(U^*=(16U_{h/2}-U_h)/15\).
  • Analytic benchmark. For Gaussian densities and a Gaussian force the potential is exact, with \(1/\gamma=1/a_1+1/a_2+1/b\):
    $$U(R)=c_1c_2v_0\,\frac{\pi^{9/2}}{(a_1a_2b)^{3/2}}\Big(\frac{\gamma}{\pi}\Big)^{3/2}e^{-\gamma R^2}$$
  • Normalisation. \(\int\rho\,d^3r=A\) is enforced by 10-point Gauss–Legendre out to 25 fm.
  • Cross-method agreement. The momentum-space and coordinate-space results must agree to the quadrature tolerance.
