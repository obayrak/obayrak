Fusion with couplings to collective excitations of the target. CCFULL: the coupled-channels code of Hagino, Rowley and Kruppa (1999), rewritten here. Theory and numerical methods

Everything the page computes, from the potential to the last check. Equations are numbered in the order they are used.

1Radial problem and units

For relative motion of a projectile (\(A_{p}\), \(Z_{p}\)) and a target (\(A_{t}\), \(Z_{t}\)) with orbital angular momentum l, the reduced radial wave function u(r) obeys

\[ u''(r) = f(r)\,u(r), \qquad f(r) = \frac{l(l+1)}{r^2} + K\,\bigl[V(r) - E\bigr], \qquad K = \frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2} \](1)

with the total potential

\[ V(r) = V_N(r) + V_C(r) + V_{\text{surf}}(r) + V_{so}(r) . \](2)

Masses come from atomic mass excesses Δ (in keV), subtracting the electrons:

\[ M = A\,u + \Delta - Z\,m_e, \qquad \mu = \frac{M_p M_t}{M_p + M_t}, \qquad K = \frac{2\mu c^2}{(\hbar c)^2}\times s_K \](3)

where \(s_K\) is the “2μ/ħ² scale” in Numerics (1 for CODATA values; 0.9997376 reproduces the constants of the 1982 paper). All radii use the FRESCO convention

\[ R_x = r_x\bigl(A_p^{1/3} + A_t^{1/3}\bigr), \qquad x = C,\ v,\ s,\ so . \](4)

2Potentials

Coulomb

A uniformly charged sphere of radius \(R_{C}\), a point charge outside:

\[ V_C(r) = Z_pZ_te^2 \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{2R_C}\Bigl(3 - \dfrac{r^2}{R_C^2}\Bigr), & r < R_C \\[2mm] \dfrac{1}{r}, & r \ge R_C \end{cases} \](5)

\(V_{C}\) and its first derivative are continuous at \(R_{C}\). Outside \(R_{C}\) it is analytic, which the complex contour of §4 needs.

Nuclear volume term (FRESCO TYPE = 1)

\(V_{N}\)(r) = −V f(r), with RH = (r − R)/a and E = exp(RH). Only this term is scaled by the depth factor λ during continuation (§6).

Imaginary (absorptive) part

As in FRESCO and GAMOW, each potential term can carry an imaginary strength. With the volume shape \(f\) and the surface shape \(g\) of the menu,

\[ V(r) \;\to\; V(r) + i\,\bigl[-W_v\,f(r; R_w, a_w) + W_D\,g(r; R_D, a_D)\bigr], \](7)

so \(W_v, W_D > 0\) remove flux. The Hamiltonian is then non-Hermitian: bound states move off the real axis (\(\mathrm{Im}\,E \approx \langle u|\,\mathrm{Im}\,V\,|u\rangle\) for weak absorption) and resonances get an extra absorption width. The imaginary part is analytic, so it is continued onto the rotated ray like the real part; the matching radius is placed beyond every Woods–Saxon radius (\(R, R_s, R_{so}, R_w, R_D\)) so that no pole of the potential lies in the swept wedge. The state is found with \(W\) switched off and then followed while \(W\) is switched on in steps (shown in the Pole trajectory tab). Quantities that assume a real potential (ANC, R-matrix widths, flux width) are not computed.

3Bound states, resonances and the S-matrix

The regular solution behaves as \(u \approx r^{l+1}\) at the origin. Beyond the nuclear range, with wave number and Sommerfeld parameter

\[ k = \sqrt{K E}, \qquad \eta = \frac{Z_pZ_te^2 K}{2k}, \](8)

a bound state (\(E < 0,\ k = i\kappa\)) decays as the Whittaker function \(W_{-\eta,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)\), and a Gamow (quasi-bound) state is purely outgoing:

\[ u(r) \propto H_l^{+}(\eta, kr) \sim \exp\!\Bigl[i\Bigl(kr - \eta\ln 2kr - \frac{l\pi}{2} + \sigma_l\Bigr)\Bigr], \qquad E = E_r - \frac{i}{2}\Gamma . \](9)

Both conditions can hold only at discrete poles of the S-matrix. A resonance pole has \(k\) in the fourth quadrant, so \(u\) grows like \(e^{|\mathrm{Im}\,k|\,r}\) on the real axis and cannot be normalised with \(|u|^2\). The half-life is \(T_{1/2} = \hbar\ln 2/\Gamma\).

where \(N_b\) counts all bound states of that \(l\), including Pauli-forbidden ones of a deep cluster potential.

Extremely narrow

Γ/Er ≲ 10⁻¹² (α decay): Im E is rounding noise in every pole method and the phase shift cannot resolve the jump. The flux width (21) is used; its accuracy is limited by the WKB start of H⁺ (\(\approx 10^{-5}\)).

Very broad

Γ ≳ Er: the node count is ambiguous, the Breit–Wigner parameters differ from the pole, and the state may lie above the barrier. The pole is still exact; its physical meaning is weaker.

Near threshold

κ or k → 0: wave functions extend far out. The contour length is chosen by the separation criterion (14); bound states within ~10 keV of a neutral threshold need very long rays and are taken from Sturm counting in the spectral method.

Deep wells

Large \(k_{\max}\) inside (α + ²⁰⁸Pb: \(\approx 6\) fm⁻¹): Numerov needs \(k_{\max}h \ll 1\) (default \(h = 0.005\) fm); GAMOW at \(h = 0.1\) fm is then 13 keV off.

4Complex-scaling solver

  1. Interior. Numerov outward on \(r_j = jh\) up to the matching radius \(R_m = \max(R_C, R, 1.5\ \mathrm{fm})\), at complex \(E\):
    \[ \Bigl(1 - \frac{h^2}{12}f_{j+1}\Bigr)u_{j+1} = 2\Bigl(1 + \frac{5h^2}{12}f_j\Bigr)u_j - \Bigl(1 - \frac{h^2}{12}f_{j-1}\Bigr)u_{j-1} \](12)
    Start \(u_0 = 0,\ u_1 = h^{l+1}\bigl[1 + \frac{S\,h}{2(l+1)}\bigr]\) with \(S = \lim_{r\to0} r f(r)\), the first-order term of \(u = r^{l+1}(1 + c\,r + \dots)\), non-zero for \(1/r\) potentials (Yukawa, point Coulomb); in the first step the finite limit \((fu)(0)\) replaces the singular term (\(= 2\) for \(l = 1\), \(= S\) for \(l = 0\)). Without the first-order term a Yukawa state converges only as \(O(h)\) (the deuteron preset was \(6\times10^{-7}\) MeV off at the default step; now \(2\times10^{-9}\)).
  2. Exterior on a rotated ray. With \(r(s) = R_m + s\,e^{i\theta}\) the equation becomes
    \[ \frac{d^2u}{ds^2} = e^{2i\theta} f\bigl(r(s)\bigr)\,u \](13)
    The outgoing (or decaying) wave is the one that decays along the ray, so Numerov is run inward from \(s = S\) with \(u(S) = 0,\ u(S - h) = 1\); the growing inward solution is the physical one and the recursion is stable. The full potential, nuclear tail included, is used on the ray.
  3. Contour length. S is the smallest length with
    \[ \int_0^S \mathrm{Re}\sqrt{e^{2i\theta} f(r(s))}\;ds \ \ge\ 20, \](14)
    so the two exterior solutions separate by at least \(e^{40}\); S is never below the “Min. contour” value and never above 2500 fm.
  4. Derivatives. A Numerov-consistent one-sided formula, accurate to \(O(h^6)\):
    \[ u'_N = \frac{u_N - u_{N-1}}{h} + h\sum_{k=0}^{4} c_k f_{N-k}u_{N-k}, \quad c = \Bigl(\frac{367}{1440}, \frac{3}{8}, -\frac{47}{240}, \frac{29}{360}, -\frac{7}{480}\Bigr) \](15)
    and the mirror formula on the ray, divided by \(e^{i\theta}\).
  5. Pole condition. A scaled Wronskian, which has no poles where u vanishes:
    \[ W(E) = \frac{u_i'(R_m)\,u_e(R_m) - u_e'(R_m)\,u_i(R_m)}{\lVert(u_i, u_i')\rVert\;\lVert(u_e, u_e')\rVert} = 0 \](16)
    solved by complex secant, steps limited to \(2 + |E|/2\) MeV, stopping when \(|\Delta E| < 10^{-12}\max(1, |E|)\).

Why the contour is allowed. Moving the exterior from the real axis to the ray sweeps the wedge \(\mathrm{Re}\,r \ge R_m,\ 0 \le \arg(r - R_m) \le \theta\). Every form in §2 is analytic there (Woods–Saxon poles sit at \(R + i\pi a(2m+1)\), on the line \(\mathrm{Re}\,r = R \le R_m\)). The ray must satisfy \(-\arg k < \theta < \pi/2\); the default \(\theta = 0.9\) rad covers \(\Gamma\) up to several times \(E_r\). Gaussian forms blow up on rays steeper than 45°, so \(\theta\) is capped at 0.7 rad when any Gaussian term is present.

Discretised continuum. The finite contour with \(u(S) = 0\) also produces “box” solutions along \(\arg E \approx -2\theta\). They move when \(\theta\) or \(S\) changes, which is how the scan tab and the checks reject them.

Pinpoints of the contour. (i) θ must satisfy \(-\arg k < \theta < \pi/2\); the default 0.9 rad covers \(\Gamma\) up to about \(5E_r\). (ii) A finite contour with \(u(S) = 0\) adds discretised continuum (“box”) solutions along \(\arg E \approx -2\theta\); a true pole does not move when θ or S changes, which is how every scan and check separates them. (iii) Im E of a bound state comes out at the level of \(10^{-10}\)–\(10^{-9}\) MeV, the numerical floor of the method; widths below \(20\times\) that floor are reported from the flux formula. (iv) The derivative (15) is accurate to \(O(h^6)\), so the matching error is dominated by the Numerov solution itself, \(O(h^4)\).

5Normalisation of a non-normalisable state

The Gamow state is normalised with the c-product (no complex conjugate), with the outer part taken along the ray where the integrand decays:

\[ \mathcal{N} = \int_0^{R_m} u^2(r)\,dr \;+\; e^{i\theta}\int_0^{S} u^2\bigl(R_m + s e^{i\theta}\bigr)\,ds, \qquad u \to u/\sqrt{\mathcal{N}} \](17)

Both integrals use Simpson’s rule. By Cauchy’s theorem the result does not depend on θ, and it equals the Zel’dovich regularised norm

\[ \mathcal{N} = \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^+}\int_0^\infty e^{-\varepsilon r^2}\,u^2(r)\,dr . \](18)

The overall sign is fixed by \(\mathrm{Re}\,u(h) > 0\). For a bound state \(\mathcal N\) is the ordinary \(\int|u|^2dr\), which the checks compute separately on the real axis. On the real axis the plotted wave function is continued outward for a resonance (the Gamow tail grows, so this is stable) and inward from far away for a bound state, starting where \(\int\kappa\,dr \ge 18\) beyond the turning point.

Berggren completeness and expectation values

Bound states, the resonances enclosed by the contour and the scattering states on the contour itself form a complete set (Berggren), with the c-product as metric:

\[ \sum_b |u_b)(u_b| + \sum_{r} |u_r)(u_r| + \int_{L^+}|u_k)(u_k|\,dk = \mathbb 1, \qquad (u_a|u_b) = \int u_a\,u_b\,dr\ \ \text{(no conjugation)}. \](19)

Expectation values and transition matrix elements use the same product, \(\langle O\rangle = \int u\,O\,u\,dr\), taken along the contour; they are complex for Gamow states. A common reading is that \({\rm Re}\langle O\rangle\) is the value and \({\rm Im}\langle O\rangle\) its uncertainty from the decay; this is meaningful when \(|{\rm Im}| \ll |{\rm Re}|\), i.e. for narrow states. The complex rms radius and the complex B(EL) on this page follow this rule.

6Finding the (n, l) state without a guess

Bound: Sturm bisection

At real E < 0 the number of nodes of the regular solution on (0, ∞) equals the number of bound states below E. Integrating far into the forbidden region (until |u| has grown by e⁴⁰) and bisecting between the well bottom and −10⁻³ MeV brackets the state with exactly n nodes to 10⁻¹¹ relative; one secant step on (16) then polishes it.

Unbound: depth continuation

The volume depth is scaled, \(V \to \lambda V\), until \((n, l)\) is bound at \(E \le -1\) MeV. \(\lambda\) is then lowered back to 1 while the pole is followed: linear predictor in \(\lambda\), secant corrector, step ×1.5 on success and ÷2 on rejection. A step is accepted when the corrector lands within \(0.3|\Delta E_{\rm last}| + 0.01 + 0.001|E|\) MeV of the prediction and \(\mathrm{Im}\,E \le 0\).

When it cannot work

A neutral s-wave passes through threshold as a virtual state (k on the negative imaginary axis), which the contour cannot represent, and \(V \le 0\) cannot be deepened. The page then uses a complex secant from the start energy in the GAMOW tab.

The label n therefore means “the pole connected to the bound state with n nodes”. For narrow states it equals the node count of Re u inside the barrier; for broad states that count can differ, and the check is shown as a warning, not a failure. Wildermuth’s rule \(2n + l = G\) selects Pauli-allowed cluster states (\(G = 4\) for ⁸Be and the ¹²C ground band, 8 for ²⁰Ne, 22 for ²¹²Po).

Absorptive potentials

With an imaginary part the Hamiltonian is non-Hermitian and Sturm counting no longer applies. The state is first found with \(W = 0\) (Sturm bisection or depth continuation) and then followed in up to ~10 adaptive steps while \(W\) is scaled from 0 to its full value (shown in the Pole trajectory tab). The spectral method takes its (n, l) label from this followed pole and polishes the nearest θ-stable eigenvalue.

Pinpoints. Depth continuation follows one pole; if two poles approach each other (an exceptional point) the step size shrinks and the page reports that the pole was lost rather than jumping to another state. The label n is the node number of the bound state the pole is connected to, which equals the node count inside the barrier only for narrow states. Deep cluster potentials contain Pauli-forbidden states, so n must be chosen with the Wildermuth rule \(2n + l = G\).

8ANC, reduced widths and R-matrix

Asymptotic normalisation coefficient (bound states)

Beyond the nuclear range a bound state is proportional to the decaying Whittaker function. Its coefficient is the ANC:

\[ u(r) \xrightarrow{r\to\infty} C\,W_{-\eta,\,l+1/2}(2\kappa r), \qquad W \simeq e^{-\kappa r}(2\kappa r)^{-\eta}\Bigl[1 + \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(l+1+\eta)_n(\eta-l)_n}{n!\,(-2\kappa r)^n}\Bigr] \](23)

\(W\) is started from this series far out and integrated inward with the full exterior potential; \(C = u(r)/W(r)\) is averaged over an 8 fm window beyond the channel radius, and its spread (\(\approx 10^{-12}\) for the presets) shows that \(u\) has reached its asymptotic form. \(C^2\) (fm⁻¹) enters direct-capture S-factors at astrophysical energies.

Reduced width and R-matrix width

At a channel radius \(a_c\) beyond the nuclear range, with the regular solution at \(E_r\) normalised inside \(a_c\):

\[ \gamma^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu a_c}\,\frac{u^2(a_c)}{\int_0^{a_c}u^2\,dr}, \qquad \gamma_W^2 = \frac{3\hbar^2}{2\mu a_c^2}, \qquad \theta^2 = \frac{\gamma^2}{\gamma_W^2} \](24)

\(\theta^2\) close to 1 marks a pronounced cluster state. With the penetrability and shift function of the full exterior potential, \(P_l = ka_c/|H^+(a_c)|^2\) and \(S_l = a_c\,\frac{d}{dr}\ln|H^+|\big|_{a_c}\), the formal and observed (Thomas) widths are

\[ \Gamma_{\rm formal} = 2P_l\gamma^2, \qquad \Gamma_{\rm obs} = \frac{2P_l\gamma^2}{1 + \gamma^2\,dS_l/dE} \](25)

\(\Gamma_{\rm obs}\) is an independent check of the pole width: it reproduces the Hoyle-type, ⁸Be and ¹⁷O widths to 10⁻⁴ or better, while the formal width is 20–80 % too large near threshold. For resonances the c-product radius \(\langle r^2\rangle^{1/2} = \bigl(\int u^2r^2\,dr\bigr)^{1/2}\) along the contour is also given; it is complex, with a small imaginary part for a narrow state.

Pinpoints. \(\gamma^2\) and \(\theta^2\) depend on the channel radius \(a_c\); \(\Gamma_{\rm obs}\) depends on it much less because \(P_l\) and \(S_l\) compensate. Choose \(a_c\) beyond the nuclear range (the automatic choice is where \(|V_N| < 1\%\)). The ANC depends strongly on the binding energy and on the radius and diffuseness of the potential; it is related to the spectroscopic factor by \(C^2 = S_F\,b^2\), with \(b\) the single-particle ANC computed here for a normalised cluster wave function (\(S_F = 1\)). ANC, R-matrix widths and the flux width assume a real potential and are not computed with absorption.

9Fit to experiment

With one parameter \(p\) (depth, radius or diffuseness) the secant method solves \(E_r(p) = E_{\rm exp}\). With two parameters \(\mathbf p\), Newton’s method with a finite-difference Jacobian \(J\) solves \(\bigl(E_r - E_{\rm exp},\ \ln\Gamma - \ln\Gamma_{\rm exp}\bigr) = 0\), halving steps that do not reduce the residual. Parameter uncertainties follow from the experimental ones,

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf p) = J^{-1}\,\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\sigma_E^2,\ (\sigma_\Gamma/\Gamma)^2\bigr)\,J^{-\mathsf T}, \qquad \sigma_p = \sigma_E\big/\lvert dE_r/dp\rvert \ \text{(one parameter)} . \](26)

Pinpoints. Depth, radius and diffuseness are strongly correlated (the classic \(VR^n \approx\) const ambiguity), so two-parameter fits to E and Γ typically show a correlation near −1: the individual uncertainties are meaningful only together with it. The width is fitted at fixed energy (inner secant on the first parameter at every step); if Γexp cannot be reached by the chosen second parameter, the page reports the closest width. The choice of n (Pauli-allowed band) changes the fitted depth completely.

10GAMOW method (Vertse, Pál, Balogh 1982)

Re-implemented as an independent solver for the comparison tab. It needs a starting energy and iterates Newton corrections.

  1. Interior start. Near the origin \(u = \sum_i C_i r^{l+1+i}\). With \(a_i = C_ih^i,\ T_1 = (W_1 - k^2)h^2,\ T_2 = W_2h\):
    \[ a_{i+2} = \frac{T_2a_{i+1} + T_1a_i}{(i+2)(2l+i+3)}, \qquad a_0 = 1,\ a_1 = \frac{T_2}{2l+2}, \qquad u(m) = (mh)^{l+1}\sum_{i=0}^{4}a_im^i \](27)
    for the first \(n_b = \sqrt{l(l+1)/12} + 1\) points, then Fox–Goodwin (Numerov) outward to \(R_m + 3h\). (The paper prints \(2l + 1\) in \(a_1\); the recurrence gives \(2l + 2\).)
  2. Exterior. \(V_N\) is cut to zero beyond \(R_M\). On the steepest-descent line \(r = R_M + t\,e^{i\varphi}\), \(\varphi = \pi/2 - \arg k\), the solution starts from the asymptotic Coulomb series
    \[ O_l(z) \simeq e^{i(z - \eta\ln 2z)}\Bigl[1 + \sum_{n\ge1}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(i\eta + l + j)(i\eta - l + j - 1)}{j\,(2iz)}\Bigr], \quad z = kr \](28)
    at the far end \(t = T = \min\bigl(\max(25/|k|,\,3|\eta|^2/|k|,\,2.4),\ 80/|k|\bigr)\), step \(0.12/|k|\), and is integrated inward to \(R_M\); then from \(R_M\) inward on the real axis to \(R_m - 3h\).
  3. Matching radius. \(R_I\) is the inner turning point of the barrier (its top if \(E_r\) is above it); \(R_m = \mathrm{INST}\cdot h\) with INST even and \(R_I - R_m = 2\text{–}3\,h\).
  4. Mismatch and correction. Seven-point derivatives give \(L = L_i - L_e\). With \(u\) Zel’dovich-normalised along the real axis and the line, \(\partial L/\partial E = -K/u^2(R_m)\), so
    \[ u'(i) = \frac{u_{i+3}-u_{i-3}}{60h} - \frac{3(u_{i+2}-u_{i-2})}{20h} + \frac{3(u_{i+1}-u_{i-1})}{4h} \](29)
    \[ \Delta E = \frac{L\,u^2(R_m)}{K} \](30)
  5. Wrong node number. If the node count \(N\) (origin included) differs from the request, \(\Delta E = \pm D_1/K\) with \(D_1 = K_{\max}^2/(N + 1)\), or \(3\pi^2/R_I^2\) for \(N = 1\).

Implementation differences from the 1982 FORTRAN: Numerov replaces Milne’s method on the line, an O(h⁶) Numerov-consistent derivative replaces interpolation formula (33) at \(R_{M}\), and RK4 supplies u(\(R_{M}\) − h). With these, the published test run is reproduced iteration by iteration, and Table 3 is reproduced at the paper’s step sizes.

11Spectral complex scaling (suggested third method)

Instead of integrating at one trial energy at a time, the whole exterior-complex-scaled Hamiltonian is discretised once and diagonalised. Every bound state and resonance in the window then appears as an eigenvalue, without a start energy or continuation path.

  1. Domains. Real-axis pieces \([0, R_C]\) and \([R_C, R_m]\) (kinks of \(V_C\) or a square well sit on domain ends), then two pieces on the ray \(r = R_m + s\,e^{i\theta}\): \(s \in [0, 12\ \mathrm{fm}]\) and \([12\ \mathrm{fm}, S]\). On each piece \(u\) is represented on \(N + 1\) Chebyshev–Gauss–Lobatto points with the Chebyshev differentiation matrix \(D\):
    \[ x_j = \cos\frac{\pi j}{N}, \qquad \Bigl(-\frac{1}{K}\,e^{-2i\vartheta}D^2 + V + \frac{l(l+1)}{Kr^2}\Bigr)u_j = E\,u_j \](31)
    with \(\vartheta = 0\) on the real pieces and \(\vartheta = \theta\) on the ray. \(N\) is raised to \(2.5\,k_{\max}L + 16\) points (\(\le 120\)) on deep, long pieces.
  2. Boundary and interface conditions. \(u(0) = 0\), \(u(R_m + S\,e^{i\theta}) = 0\), and at every interface \(u\) is shared and \(u'\) is continuous (with \(du/dr = e^{-i\theta}du/ds\) on the ray). The interface rows contain no \(E\), so the interface values are eliminated exactly, leaving an ordinary complex eigenproblem \(Hu = Eu\) of size ≈ 120–220.
  3. Eigenvalues. Householder reduction to Hessenberg form, then shifted complex QR with Givens rotations and Wilkinson shifts (checked against LAPACK to 10⁻¹⁴).
  4. Physical or not. The matrix is rebuilt with θ − 0.15 rad. Poles of the S-matrix do not move; discretised continuum eigenvalues lie along arg \(E \approx -2\theta\) and do. An eigenvalue is kept if it moves by less than 10⁻⁵(1 + |E|).
  5. Labelling (n, l). Bound states are labelled by Sturm counting (exact); resonances by the node count of the eigenvector (inverse iteration, then barycentric Chebyshev interpolation or the real-axis Numerov solution inside the barrier).
  6. Finest resolution. The selected eigenvalue starts the Numerov complex-scaling secant of §4, which converges in a few steps to 10⁻¹² MeV, and all checks of §7 are run on the polished state.

12Scattering at real energy

S-matrix, phase shifts, cross sections and wave functions

For \(E > 0\) the regular solution is integrated through the nuclear region and, with only Coulomb and centrifugal terms left, outward beyond the outer turning point (stable, because it grows through any barrier). There it is matched to the Coulomb functions, computed by Steed’s continued-fraction method (CF1 for \(F'/F\), CF2 for \(H^{+\prime}/H^+\), Wronskian \(F'G - FG' = 1\)), which agree with mpmath to \(10^{-15}\):

\[ u \propto H^- - S_l\,H^+, \qquad S_l = \frac{u'H^- - u\,H^{-\prime}}{u'H^+ - u\,H^{+\prime}}, \qquad \delta_l = \tfrac12\arg S_l . \](32)

For a real potential \(|S_l| = 1\) to \(10^{-16}\); the phase shifts agree with an independent DOP853 + mpmath calculation to \(10^{-9}\) rad. The partial cross sections and the Breit–Wigner parameters follow from

\[ \sigma_l^{\rm el} = \frac{\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)\,|1 - S_l|^2, \quad \sigma_l^{\rm R} = \frac{\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)\,(1 - |S_l|^2), \quad \delta_l(E_R) = \bigl(m+\tfrac12\bigr)\pi,\ \ \Gamma_{\rm BW} = \frac{2}{d\delta_l/dE}\Big|_{E_R} . \](33)

Summing over partial waves gives the total nuclear elastic and reaction cross sections, \(\sigma^{\rm el} = \sum_{l=0}^{l_{\max}}\sigma_l^{\rm el}\) and \(\sigma^{\rm R} = \sum_{l}\sigma_l^{\rm R} = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_l(2l+1)T_l\), with transmission coefficients \(T_l = 1 - |S_l|^2\) (the input of Hauser–Feshbach calculations); \(l_{\max}\) should exceed \(k R_{\rm nucl}\) so that the omitted partial waves are negligible.

The scattering wave function at a real energy is the same regular solution, divided by its asymptotic amplitude: \(u(r) = \frac{1}{A}u_{\rm num}(r)\) with \(u_{\rm num} \to A\,\frac{H^- - S_lH^+}{2i}\), then multiplied by \(e^{-i\,{\rm Re}\,\delta_l}\) so that \(u \to \cos\delta_l F_l + \sin\delta_l G_l\). The overall sign of \(F_l, G_l\) from Steed’s method is fixed by integrating the point-Coulomb regular solution from the origin. The probability inside the matching radius, \(P_{\rm in}(E) = \int_0^{R_m}|u|^2dr\), peaks at a narrow resonance with height \(\propto 1/\Gamma\) (\(\approx 10^{5}\) for the Hoyle-type state).

Angular distributions

With the Coulomb phases \(\sigma_l = \arg\Gamma(l+1+i\eta)\) (Lanczos log-Gamma, agreeing with mpmath to \(10^{-14}\)) the elastic amplitude is \(f = f_C + f_N\),

\[ f_C(\theta) = -\frac{\eta}{2k\sin^2(\theta/2)}\,e^{-i\eta\ln\sin^2(\theta/2) + 2i\sigma_0}, \qquad f_N(\theta) = \frac{1}{2ik}\sum_{l=0}^{l_{\max}}(2l+1)\,e^{2i\sigma_l}\,(S_l - 1)\,P_l(\cos\theta), \](34)

and \(d\sigma/d\Omega = |f|^2\). For identical particles of spin \(s\) (spin-independent interaction)

\[ \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(\theta)|^2 + |f(\pi-\theta)|^2 + \frac{2(-1)^{2s}}{2s+1}\,\mathrm{Re}\bigl[f(\theta)f^*(\pi-\theta)\bigr], \](35)

which for \(s = 0\) is \(|f(\theta) + f(\pi-\theta)|^2\): odd \(l\) cancel and the Mott interference appears around 90°. Ratios are taken to Rutherford, or to Mott (the same formula with \(f_C\) only). \(l_{\max}\) is chosen automatically once \(|S_l - 1|\) stays below \(10^{-8}\) (\(10^{-6}\) beyond the grazing \(l \approx kR\)). Checks: with no nuclear potential the ratio is 1 to \(10^{-7}\); the symmetrised Coulomb amplitude reproduces the analytic Mott formula to \(3\times10^{-7}\); for neutrons \(2\pi\int\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\sin\theta\,d\theta = \sum_l\sigma_l^{\rm el}\) to \(7\times10^{-7}\). In the laboratory frame (elastic, target at rest) \(\tan\theta_{\rm lab} = \sin\theta/(\cos\theta + \gamma)\), \(\gamma = m_p/m_t\), and \(d\sigma/d\Omega_{\rm lab} = d\sigma/d\Omega\,(1+\gamma^2+2\gamma\cos\theta)^{3/2}/|1+\gamma\cos\theta|\).

Excitation functions. At fixed angles \(d\sigma/d\Omega(E)\) is evaluated from (34)–(35) with all \(S_l\) recomputed at every energy (the Numerov models are built once per \(l\), so 150 energies take about 0.3 s for α + α). A laboratory angle is converted with \(\theta = \theta_{\rm lab} + \arcsin(\gamma\sin\theta_{\rm lab})\) (forward branch, \(\gamma = m_p/m_t \le 1\); \(\theta = 2\theta_{\rm lab}\) for identical particles), and \(E_{\rm lab} = E\,(m_p + m_t)/m_t\).

Low-energy parameters

Below the first resonance the phase shift is described by the (Coulomb-modified) effective-range expansion

\[ K_l(E) = k^{2l+1}\,w_l(\eta)\,\Bigl[C_0^2(\eta)\cot\delta_l + 2\eta\,h(\eta)\Bigr] = -\frac{1}{a_l} + \frac{r_l}{2}k^2 - P_lk^4 + \dots, \quad C_0^2 = \frac{2\pi\eta}{e^{2\pi\eta}-1},\ \ w_l = \prod_{n=1}^{l}\Bigl(1+\frac{\eta^2}{n^2}\Bigr),\ \ h(\eta) = \mathrm{Re}\,\psi(1+i\eta) - \ln\eta \](36)

For \(\eta = 0\) it reduces to \(k^{2l+1}\cot\delta_l\). Because \(\tan\delta_l \sim e^{-2\pi\eta}\) near threshold (10⁻⁵¹ for α + ⁸Be at 10 keV), \(\tan\delta_l = -W(F,u)/W(G,u)\) is evaluated at the nuclear surface, with \(F\) integrated outward from the origin and \(G\) inward from Steed’s values beyond the turning point, both stable directions; this agrees with the S-matrix route to \(4\times10^{-9}\) where both work. \(h(\eta)\) uses the digamma series (asymptotic series for \(\eta > 7\)), accurate to \(3\times10^{-11}\). A least-squares fit in \(k^2\) gives \(a_l, r_l, P_l\). For an uncharged s-wave the expansion also gives the pole nearest threshold from \(-1/a + rk^2/2 = ik\): for n + ¹⁶O it predicts the weakly bound 1/2⁺-like state at −6.591 keV, against −6.602 keV from the bound-state solver.

For narrow resonances \(E_R\) and \(\Gamma_{\rm BW}\) coincide with the pole (Hoyle-type: \(\Gamma_{\rm BW} = 7.9296\) eV vs 7.9293 eV; ⁸Be: 5.9012 vs 5.8998 eV). For broad ones they differ, because the Breit–Wigner parameters include the energy dependence of the background and the penetrability, while the pole does not.

Pinpoints of the scattering module. (i) Cross sections are nuclear (Coulomb-subtracted) partial or summed cross sections; the Coulomb elastic cross section itself diverges at θ → 0 and is only shown inside dσ/dΩ. (ii) Phase shifts are defined modulo π; the Levinson offset fixes the absolute value. (iii) \(1 - |S_l|^2\) below about \(5\times10^{-10}\) is rounding noise of \(S\) and is set to zero. (iv) Near threshold \(\tan\delta \sim e^{-2\pi\eta}\) is below the precision of \(S\); the effective-range module therefore matches under the barrier. (v) Resonances narrower than about \(10^{-12}E_r\) cannot be resolved on the real axis at all. (vi) Identical particles: only the angular distributions are symmetrised; partial and summed cross sections are not (for spin-0 bosons use even \(l\) only). (vii) \(l_{\max}\) must exceed the grazing value \(kR\); the angular module stops automatically once \(|S_l - 1|\) is negligible.

Direct radiative capture

Away from resonances the capture proceeds directly from the scattering state into a bound state. With the scattering wave \(u_{l_i}\) normalised to unit asymptotic amplitude, \(u_{l_i} \to \cos\delta\,F_{l_i} + \sin\delta\,G_{l_i}\), the bound state \(u_f\) normalised to 1, and the effective charge \(e_L\) of the EM chapter, Fermi’s golden rule with the incoming plane wave gives

\[ \sigma_{EL}(E) = \frac{8\pi(L+1)}{L[(2L+1)!!]^2}\,\frac{k_\gamma^{2L+1}}{\hbar v}\,\frac{e^2e_L^2}{k^2}\,(2L+1)\sum_{l_i}(2l_i+1)\,\langle l_i\,0\,L\,0|l_f\,0\rangle^2\,\Bigl|\int_0^\infty u_f\,r^L\,u_{l_i}\,dr\Bigr|^2 \times S_F\,\frac{2J_f+1}{(2j_p+1)(2j_t+1)(2l_f+1)}, \](39)

with \(E_\gamma = E - E_b\) (\(E_b < 0\)), \(k_\gamma = E_\gamma/\hbar c\), \(\hbar v = \hbar^2k/\mu\), and different \(l_i\) adding incoherently after the angular integration. Both waves come from the same potential; the overlap is taken on the model grid inside \(R_m\) and on the exterior grid out to where the bound state has decayed by \(e^{-18}\), and the scattering wave is normalised by continuing it beyond the outer turning point and matching to \(F, G\). Checks: an independent DOP853 + mpmath implementation agrees to \(3\times10^{-5}\) (n + p → d, E1) and \(5\times10^{-6}\) (³He + α → ⁷Be, E1 and E2); by detailed balance the deuteron model gives a photodisintegration peak of 2.67 mb at \(E_\gamma \approx 4.2\) MeV, 3–16 % above the zero-range formula with effective-range correction, the excess being the p-wave final-state attraction of the central potential.

Pinpoints. (i) For \(Z_p/A_p = Z_t/A_t\) (α + ¹²C, d + α) \(e_1 = 0\) and only E2 survives, which is why ¹²C(α,γ) and d(α,γ)⁶Li are so small. (ii) The same central potential serves the bound and the scattering states; real analyses often fit the scattering potential separately to phase shifts. (iii) The low-energy S-factor of loosely bound states is dominated by the tail: \(S(0) \propto C^2\) (peripheral capture), which is why the ANC is the key quantity for ⁸B and ⁷Be. (iv) M1 transitions, spin–orbit splitting of the scattering waves and antisymmetrisation are not included.

14α-decay

In the cluster model an α emitter is an α particle bound in a deep potential of the daughter and tunnelling through the Coulomb barrier. With one geometry for all emitters, the half-life follows from the width of the quasi-bound state and a preformation factor:

\[ T_{1/2} = \frac{\hbar\ln 2}{P_\alpha\,\Gamma}, \qquad \Gamma = \frac{\hbar^2}{\mu}\,\frac{k\,|u(R)|^2}{|H^+(R)|^2\int_0^R|u|^2dr} . \](44)

Quantum number. The four nucleons of the α particle occupy oscillator shells above the daughter’s Fermi surface. With \(N_{\rm osc}\) the major shell of the last two protons and of the last two neutrons of the parent (\(\le 50\): 3, 51–82: 4, 83–126: 5, 127–184: 6),

\[ G = 2n + l = 2N_{\rm osc}(Z) + 2N_{\rm osc}(N), \](45)

so \(G = 20\) for \(N \le 126\) and \(G = 22\) above the shell closure in the actinide region; this jump is what produces the drop of \(T_{1/2}\) across \(N = 126\). Because \(2n + l\) has the parity of \(l\), decays with odd \(l\) use the lowest Pauli-allowed value \(G + 1\).

Depth fit at the real energy. For each emitter the depth \(V\) is chosen so that the state lies exactly at \(Q_\alpha\). At the real energy \(E = Q_\alpha\) the interior solution has exactly \(n\) nodes only in a window \(V_n < V < V_{n+1}\); inside it the interior log-derivative at \(R_m\) falls monotonically from \(+\infty\) to \(-\infty\), while that of the barrier-decaying outgoing solution varies slowly. Bisection on their difference converges in about 40 steps and reproduces the depths of the pole solver to \(10^{-6}\) MeV. The width then follows from the flux formula, because Im E of such narrow states (\(10^{-14}\)–\(10^{-40}\) MeV) is far below what any pole method can resolve.

Geiger–Nuttall law. The barrier penetrability gives \(\ln T_{1/2} \approx 2\pi\eta + \text{const} \propto Z_d/\sqrt{Q_\alpha}\), so

\[ \log_{10}T_{1/2} = a\,\frac{Z_d}{\sqrt{Q_\alpha}} + b \qquad \text{(Geiger–Nuttall, Viola–Seaborg form)}, \](46)

a straight line per isotopic chain over more than 20 decades. The tab fits \(a\) and \(b\) to both the calculated and the measured half-lives.

Pinpoints. (i) \(P_\alpha\) absorbs everything the two-body model leaves out (α formation, configuration mixing); fitted values of 10⁻²–1 depend strongly on the radius and diffuseness, so the geometry and \(P_\alpha\) must be quoted together. (ii) Around \(N = 126\) and \(Z = 82\) the deviations grow: \(P_\alpha\) is smaller at closed shells. (iii) Odd-A and odd-odd emitters with \(l > 0\) or a change of internal structure are hindered; enter their \(l\) and expect larger deviations. (iv) Electron screening (a few 10⁻³ in T½) and deformation are not included. (v) Partial α half-lives must be used when other decay branches exist. (vi) Half-lives are partial α half-lives, \(T_{1/2}/b_\alpha\).

Data. The tab carries the 684 ground-state α emitters of NUBASE2020 (F. G. Kondev, M. Wang, W. J. Huang, S. Naimi, G. Audi, Chin. Phys. C 45, 030001 (2021)) with a numerical α branch: \(Q_\alpha = \Delta(A,Z) - \Delta(A-4,Z-2) - \Delta({}^4{\rm He})\) from the ground-state mass excesses (identical to AME2020), the partial half-life \(T_{1/2}/b_\alpha\), the daughter mass excess for the reduced mass, and the smallest orbital angular momentum allowed by the ground-state spins and parities, \(|J_p - J_d| \le l \le J_p + J_d\) with \((-1)^l = \pi_p\pi_d\). Values derived from systematics (#) and uncertain branches can be excluded. Decays of odd nuclei often go mainly to excited daughter states with a smaller \(l\); the ground-state-to-ground-state \(l_{\min}\) and Q then overestimate the hindrance.

Deformation and fine structure

For an even–even emitter with a deformed daughter, decays also feed the excited members \(I = 2, 4, \dots\) of the daughter’s ground-state rotational band, with \(l = I\). With the deformed radius \(R(\gamma) = R_0[1 + \beta_2Y_{20}(\gamma) + \beta_4Y_{40}(\gamma)]\), \(\gamma\) the angle between the α direction and the symmetry axis, the default method is the deformed barrier of Bohr, Fröman and Mottelson. The α particle is formed isotropically in the body-fixed frame and tunnels along each direction with the WKB probability \(P(\gamma) = e^{-2\int\kappa\,dr}\) through \(V_N(r - R(\gamma)) + V_C(r,\gamma)\). Projecting the outgoing amplitude on the channel functions \(\propto P_l(\cos\gamma)\) gives

\[ \Gamma_I \propto (2l+1)\Bigl|\int_{-1}^{1}\!\sqrt{P(\gamma)}\,P_l(\cos\gamma)\,d\!\cos\gamma\Bigr|^2 \times \frac{P_l(Q_\alpha - E_I)}{P_0(Q_\alpha)}, \qquad \frac{\Gamma_{\rm def}}{\Gamma_{\rm sph}} = \frac{1}{4}\sum_I(\dots), \](47)

where the last factor corrects for the rotational energy and the centrifugal barrier of channel \(l\) (evaluated at the direction where \(P_2 = 0\)). By Parseval’s relation the sum reduces to 4 for a spherical nucleus, so the total width is the spherical flux width times the bracket. The deformed Coulomb potential is taken to first order in \(\beta_\lambda\), plus the \(\beta_2^2\) term in \(\lambda = 2\) (as in CCFULL); the nuclear part is exact. Hindrance factors are \(\mathrm{HF}_I = T_{1/2}^{\rm exp}(I)/T_{1/2}^{\rm calc}(I)\) for the partial half-lives.

Full coupled channels. The exact alternative solves

\[ \Bigl[-\frac{d^2}{dr^2} + \frac{l(l+1)}{r^2} - k_I^2\Bigr]u_I + \frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\sum_{I'}\sum_\lambda V_\lambda(r)\,\sqrt{(2l+1)(2l'+1)}\begin{pmatrix}l&\lambda&l'\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}^{\!2}u_{I'} = 0, \qquad k_I^2 = \frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}(Q_\alpha - E_I), \](48)

with \(V(r,\gamma) = \sum_\lambda V_\lambda(r)P_\lambda(\cos\gamma)\) projected by 48-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature. Regular solutions are integrated outward, outgoing solutions inward from far outside (matrix Numerov), and the depth is found where the matching matrix becomes singular; the partial widths follow from the outgoing amplitudes. Without deformation it reproduces the single-channel depth to \(3\times10^{-8}\) and the flux width to 0.1 %. In deep cluster potentials, however, the Wildermuth condition makes the channels \(l = 0, 2, 4\) nearly degenerate inside the nucleus (three quasi-bound roots within 0.7 MeV of depth for ²³⁸U), and deformation mixes them strongly: depending on the root, the 2⁺ branch ranges from 2 % to 84 %. Without formation amplitudes from a microscopic calculation the full solution is therefore not unique; the tab lists all roots with their channel weights as a diagnostic, and uses the deformed-barrier method for the branching ratios.

Checks and pinpoints. For \(\beta = 0\) the deformed-barrier method gives only the 0⁺ channel and \(\Gamma_{\rm def} = \Gamma_{\rm sph}\) exactly. For ²³⁸U, ²³⁴U, ²⁴⁰Pu and ²⁴⁴Cm it reproduces the 2⁺ branches (20–28 %) to a few per cent; the 4⁺ branches are overestimated by factors 4–60, the known 4⁺ hindrance that depends sensitively on β₄ and on structure not contained in the model. The level energies, deformations and measured branchings of the built-in list are quoted from memory of ENSDF and deformation tables and should be checked; Qα and T½ come from NUBASE2020. Odd-A emitters (favoured and unfavoured decays, \(K\) quantum numbers) are not covered.

15Fusion barrier and cross section

Below and around the Coulomb barrier, fusion is a tunnelling problem: whatever flux passes the barrier and reaches the inner region is assumed to fuse. In the incoming-wave boundary condition (IWBC, as in CCFULL) the radial wave is purely incoming at the bottom \(r_A\) of the pocket of \(V_{\rm eff}(r) = V_N + V_C + \hbar^2l(l+1)/2\mu r^2\):

\[ \begin{gathered} u_l(r) \simeq \frac{1}{\sqrt{k_l(r)}}\exp\!\Bigl(-i\!\int^{r}k_l\,dr'\Bigr)\ \ (r \approx r_A), \\ u_l \to \tfrac{i}{2}\bigl(H^-_l - S_lH^+_l\bigr), \\ T_l = 1 - |S_l|^2, \end{gathered} \](49)

integrated outward with the shared Numerov routine and matched to Coulomb functions beyond the outer turning point. The fusion cross section and the mean angular momentum are

\[ \sigma_{\rm fus}(E) = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_l(2l+1)\,T_l(E), \qquad \langle l\rangle = \frac{\sum_l l\,(2l+1)T_l}{\sum_l(2l+1)T_l}, \](50)

with only even \(l\) and a factor 2 for identical bosons. Alternatively, with a short-range imaginary potential, \(T_l = 1 - |S_l|^2\) from the ordinary S-matrix. Rowley, Satchler and Stelson’s barrier distribution and Wong’s parabolic-barrier formula, with \(V_B\), \(R_B\) and \(\hbar\omega = \hbar\sqrt{|V''(R_B)|/\mu}\) from the s-wave barrier, are

\[ \begin{gathered} D(E) = \frac{d^2(E\sigma_{\rm fus})}{dE^2}, \\ \sigma_{\rm Wong} = \frac{\hbar\omega R_B^2}{2E}\ln\Bigl[1 + e^{2\pi(E - V_B)/\hbar\omega}\Bigr], \\ D_{\rm Wong} = \pi R_B^2\,\frac{2\pi}{\hbar\omega}\,\frac{e^x}{(1+e^x)^2},\ x = \frac{2\pi(E-V_B)}{\hbar\omega}, \end{gathered} \](51)

and far above the barrier \(\sigma \to \pi R_B^2(1 - V_B/E)\). For a single barrier \(D(E)\) is one peak at \(V_B\) of width \(\approx\hbar\omega\) and area \(\pi R_B^2\).

Checks. For ¹⁶O + ²⁰⁸Pb the s-wave transmission agrees with the WKB (Kemble) formula \(T = [1 + e^{2\int\kappa\,dr}]^{-1}\) to 4–5 % (the accuracy of WKB); IWBC and a short-range imaginary potential give the same σ to 1 % across the barrier; Wong’s formula agrees to 1–5 % at and above the barrier and overestimates increasingly below it (× 16 at 10 MeV below), the known failure of the parabolic approximation.

Coupled channels: CCFULL

Couplings of the relative motion to collective excitations of the target (rotational band \(0^+, 2^+, \dots\) or vibrational phonons) are treated as in CCFULL (Hagino, Rowley and Kruppa 1999), rewritten here line by line. In the isocentrifugal approximation the angular momentum \(J\) is the same in all channels, and

\[ \Bigl[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{d^2}{dr^2} + \frac{J(J+1)\hbar^2}{2\mu r^2} + V_N^{(0)}(r) + \frac{Z_pZ_te^2}{r} + \epsilon_n - E\Bigr]u_n + \sum_m V_{nm}(r)\,u_m = 0 . \](52)

The nuclear coupling is included to all orders: with \(R = r_0(A_p^{1/3} + A_t^{1/3})\) and the coupling operator \(\hat O\) (for a rotor \(\langle I|\hat O|I'\rangle = R_T\sum_{\lambda=2,4}\beta_\lambda\sqrt{\tfrac{(2I+1)(2\lambda+1)(2I'+1)}{4\pi}}\begin{pmatrix}I&\lambda&I'\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}^2\), for a vibrator \(R_T\beta_\lambda(a + a^\dagger)/\sqrt{4\pi}\)), diagonalised as \(\hat O = \sum_k\lambda_k|k\rangle\langle k|\),

\[ \begin{gathered} V^{(N)}_{nm}(r) = \sum_k\langle n|k\rangle\,V_N(r - \lambda_k)\,\langle k|m\rangle - V_N^{(0)}(r)\,\delta_{nm}, \\ V^{(C)}_{nm} = \sum_\lambda\frac{3Z_pZ_te^2}{2\lambda+1}\frac{R_T^\lambda}{r^{\lambda+1}}\Bigl(\beta_\lambda + \tfrac{2}{7}\sqrt{\tfrac{5}{\pi}}\beta_2^2\delta_{\lambda2} + \tfrac{9}{7\sqrt\pi}\beta_2^2\delta_{\lambda4}\Bigr)\langle n|Y_{\lambda0}|m\rangle . \end{gathered} \](53)

A purely incoming wave \(u_n = T_n e^{-ik_nr}\) with local wave numbers is imposed at the minimum of the bare \(J = 0\) pocket; the \(N\) independent solutions are started with one Runge–Kutta step, propagated with the inverse-free matrix Numerov recurrence and re-orthogonalised once at the barrier, then matched at \(R_{\max} \mp dr\) to \(H^\pm_J(k_nr)\) with channel energies \(E - V_{nn}(R_{\max})\). With \(B\) the matrix of incoming amplitudes,

\[ P_J(E) = \sum_n\frac{k_n(r_{\min})}{k}\,\bigl|(B^{-1})_{n1}\bigr|^2, \qquad \sigma_{\rm fus} = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_J(2J+1)P_J . \](54)

Constants are CCFULL’s (\(\hbar c = 197.329\) MeV fm, nucleon mass 938 MeV, \(e^2 = \hbar c/137\)). The JavaScript version reproduces the original Fortran CCFULL (Numerov method of the version by Z. Liao and K. Hagino) for its test deck to \(3\times10^{-4}\) for ¹⁶O + ¹⁵⁴Sm with 3 and 6 rotational levels, \(3\times10^{-5}\) for ¹⁶O + ¹⁴⁴Sm with an octupole phonon and \(6\times10^{-5}\) without couplings: the agreement is limited by the 4–5 printed digits of the Fortran output, and is \(10^{-8}\)–\(10^{-10}\) at and above the barrier.

Projectile excitation, two target modes, anharmonic couplings and transfer

The coupled-channels basis is the product of the intrinsic states of the target (rotor or vibrator, mode 1), of a second vibrational mode of the target, and of the projectile (rotor or vibrator), in CCFULL’s order \(|n_P\,n_T\,n_{T2}\rangle\). The combined nuclear coupling operator is diagonalised in this basis, so the nuclear coupling remains of all orders for all modes together; the Coulomb couplings and excitation energies add mode by mode:

\[ \begin{gathered} \hat O = \hat O_T\otimes1\otimes1 + 1\otimes\hat O_{T2}\otimes1 + 1\otimes1\otimes\hat O_P, \\ \epsilon_{n_Pn_Tn_{T2}} = \epsilon_{n_P} + \epsilon_{n_T} + \epsilon_{n_{T2}}, \\ \hat O_P = R_P\,\beta_\lambda^P(a + a^\dagger)/\sqrt{4\pi}\ \text{or}\ R_P(\beta_2^PY_{20} + \beta_4^PY_{40}), \end{gathered} \](55)

with \(R_P = r_PA_p^{1/3}\). All projectile–target combinations are included; for the two target modes, states with both modes excited (mutual excitations) are included only on request. Anharmonic couplings replace the harmonic matrix elements \(\beta\sqrt{\max(n,n')}\) of a multi-phonon target by given nuclear and Coulomb values \(\beta_N(n,n'), \beta_C(n,n')\) (including diagonal reorientation terms, whose Coulomb part has \(\lambda = 2\)) and the multi-phonon energies by given values. Transfer is one extra channel coupled to the entrance channel only, as in CCFULL:

\[ V_{1,\rm tr}(r) = F_{\rm tr}\,\frac{dV_N}{dr}, \qquad V_{\rm tr,tr}(r) = V_{11}(r) - Q + \bigl[(Z_p + q)(Z_t - q) - Z_pZ_t\bigr]\frac{e^2}{r}, \](56)

with the transferred charge \(q\) (0 for neutron transfer), whose Coulomb functions use the charges of the transfer partition. These couplings also enter the R-matrix and the four propagators, which use the same coupling matrix; the orientation integral describes only a rotating target and is not used with them.

R-matrix on a Lagrange mesh

The same coupled equations are also solved by a completely different technique. The interval \([r_{\min}, R_{\max}]\) is divided into sectors of \(\approx 2\) fm; in each the channel wave functions are expanded in Lagrange–Legendre functions, with the kinetic energy and Bloch surface operators at both ends as matrix elements (as in the Fortran R-matrix of Liao and Hagino). In the first sector the incoming-wave condition \(u_n' = -ik_nu_n\) at \(r_{\min}\) enters as a complex surface term \(-i\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}k_n\,\varphi_i(r_{\min})\varphi_j(r_{\min})\). With the sector Green’s-function blocks \(\mathcal R_{XY} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\Phi_X^{\mathsf T}(T + V - E)^{-1}\Phi_Y\) (\(X, Y\) = left, right surface) the global R-matrix \(u = \mathcal R\,u'\) is propagated outward,

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathcal R_b = \mathcal R_{RR} - \mathcal R_{RL}\bigl(\mathcal R_a + \mathcal R_{LL}\bigr)^{-1}\mathcal R_{LR}, \\ S = \bigl(H^+ - \mathcal R H^{+\prime}\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(H^- - \mathcal R H^{-\prime}\bigr), \\ P_J = 1 - \sum_n\frac{k_1}{k_n}|S_{1n}|^2 . \end{gathered} \](57)

Propagation keeps every linear system small (sector size × channels) instead of one large matrix, and needs no stabilisation. The result is converged in the mesh (24 or 30 points per sector, 2 or 1.5 fm sectors give identical numbers). It reproduces the Fortran R-matrix for ¹⁶O + ¹⁴⁴Sm to \(6.6\times10^{-5}\) at all 21 energies (print precision), and agrees with CCFULL’s Numerov solution to \(10^{-4}\)–\(5\times10^{-4}\) (\(1.4\times10^{-3}\) at the deepest energy), the same level at which the Fortran solvers agree with each other. Refining CCFULL’s step does not remove this difference: it comes from the two different implementations of the boundaries (a Runge–Kutta start and two-point matching at \(R_{\max}\mp dr\) in CCFULL, exact Bloch conditions and derivative matching in the R-matrix), both of which are correct to this level.

Other propagators for the same equations

Four classical integrators solve the CCFULL equations with the same incoming-wave condition, \(Y(r_{\min}) = u'u^{-1} = -iK\), and the same matching through the log-derivative matrix at \(R_{\max}\),

\[ \begin{gathered} S = \bigl(YH^+ - H^{+\prime}\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(YH^- - H^{-\prime}\bigr), \\ P_J = -\frac{u^\dagger\,\tfrac{1}{2i}(Y - Y^\dagger)\,u}{k_1}, \quad u = H^-e_1 - H^+Se_1, \end{gathered} \](58)

i.e. the absorbed current. Runge–Kutta integrates \((u, u')\) with the classical fourth-order scheme and renormalises to \((I, Y)\) every 10 steps. Fox–Goodwin is the original three-term Numerov recurrence with a matrix inversion per step (CCFULL uses an inverse-free variant), renormalised every 20 steps. Johnson’s renormalised Numerov propagates only the ratio \(R_n = \hat W_{n+1}\hat W_n^{-1}\), \(\hat W = (I - \tfrac{h^2}{12}W)u\), via \(R_n = (I - T_n)^{-1}(2I + 10T_n) - R_{n-1}^{-1}\), so it never needs stabilisation. Johnson’s log-derivative method propagates \(Y\) itself,

\[ \begin{gathered} Y_j = (I + hY_{j-1})^{-1}Y_{j-1} + \frac{h}{3}w_jQ_j, \\ Q_j = \begin{cases}(I - \tfrac{h^2}{6}W_j)^{-1}W_j & j \text{ odd}\\ W_j & j \text{ even}\end{cases}, \quad w_j = 4, 2, \dots, 1, \end{gathered} \](59)

for \(u'' = Wu\), \(W = \frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}(V + C - E)\); the recurrence was checked against exact log-derivatives (growing exponential, oscillating wave, free \(l = 1\) wave) to \(10^{-10}\). For the Numerov-type methods the derivative at \(R_{\max}\) is taken from \(u_n' = [(I - 2T_{n+1})u_{n+1} - (I - 2T_{n-1})u_{n-1}]/2h\), \(T = h^2W/12\), which is \(O(h^4)\); the simpler \((I - T)\) form is only \(O(h^2)\) and caused errors of up to 11 % far below the barrier before it was corrected.

Orientation integration for deformed targets

When the rotational energies are negligible compared with the barrier curvature (\(E_{2^+} \ll \hbar\omega\), tens of keV against \(\approx 3.5\) MeV for ¹⁵⁴Sm), the target orientation is frozen during the passage of the barrier (sudden limit), and the coupled-channels result becomes an average over orientations:

\[ \begin{gathered} \sigma_{\rm fus}(E) = \int_0^1\sigma_\theta(E)\,d(\cos\theta), \\ V_\theta(r) = V_N\bigl(r - R_T[\beta_2Y_{20}(\theta) + \beta_4Y_{40}(\theta)]\bigr) + \frac{Z_pZ_te^2}{r} + \sum_\lambda V^{(C)}_\lambda(r)\,Y_{\lambda0}(\theta), \end{gathered} \](60)

each \(\sigma_\theta\) being an exact single-channel IWBC calculation. The deformation enters exactly in angle (no truncation of the rotational basis), there are no matrices to invert or stabilise, and the cost grows linearly with the number of Gauss–Legendre orientations; 8 orientations converge to 0.3 % far below the barrier and \(10^{-4}\) above it. With \(N\) rotational levels and \(E_{2^+} \to 0\), CCFULL is equivalent to an \(N\)-point orientation quadrature; for ¹⁶O + ¹⁵⁴Sm it converges to the orientation integral with increasing \(N\) (0.04–0.3 % with levels up to 14⁺; the remainder is CCFULL’s first-order Coulomb operator in the truncated basis). The finite rotational energy (82 keV) lowers the cross section by up to \(\approx 25\) % at 8 MeV below the barrier and by \(\le 2\) % near and above it, which measures the accuracy of the sudden limit; an adiabatic eigenchannel treatment overcorrects (\(\approx 50\) %) and is not used.

Which result to trust: CCFULL or the orientation integral?

Neither is “the true one” on its own. Converged CCFULL is the most complete result of the model, because it contains the real rotational energies; the orientation integral is exact only in the limit of infinitely many levels and \(E_{2^+} \to 0\). Why few levels fail at low energy. Far below the barrier, fusion is dominated by the orientations with the lowest barrier, the tips of the prolate nucleus, where the surface is shifted outward by \(R_T[\beta_2Y_{20}(0) + \beta_4Y_{40}(0)] = 1.39\) fm. What the remaining difference means. Converged CCFULL lies below the orientation integral by 25 % at 50–52 MeV, 6 % at 55 MeV, 2 % at 60 MeV and \(\approx 1\) % above the barrier. Practice. For final results use CCFULL and add levels until σ at the lowest energy of interest no longer changes (for ¹⁵⁴Sm: 10⁺–14⁺; the test deck’s 4⁺ is insufficient below \(\approx 58\) MeV). Use the orientation integral as a fast, truncation-free cross-check and for the smooth barrier distribution; for heavy rotors it is accurate to \(\approx 2\) % near and above the barrier and overestimates the deep sub-barrier region by up to \(\approx 30\) %. Both share the same model (one bare potential, isocentrifugal approximation, target excitation only), so “true” means “converged within this model”; comparison with data may still require projectile excitations or a different potential.

Pinpoints. (i) CCFULL results depend on the number of levels: the test deck’s 0⁺, 2⁺, 4⁺ truncation underestimates deep sub-barrier fusion by a factor \(\approx 3\) compared with the converged rotor; check convergence by adding levels. (ii) The barrier distribution of a rotor is a smooth hump for the orientation integral and a set of discrete eigenbarriers for a truncated basis. (iii) Only the target is excited here; projectile excitations, mutual excitations, transfer and anharmonicities of CCFULL are not included. (iv) Both methods use the isocentrifugal approximation and IWBC at the bare pocket.

Pinpoints. (i) This is a single-channel calculation. Couplings to collective excitations of the colliding nuclei split the barrier into a distribution and enhance sub-barrier fusion of heavy systems by orders of magnitude; the measured barrier distributions of ¹⁶O + ¹⁴⁴Sm or ²⁰⁸Pb show several peaks, a single-channel model one. (ii) The result depends on the inner part of the potential only through \(r_A\): shallow (Akyüz–Winther) and deep (folding) potentials give different pockets; for deep potentials the IWBC sits deep inside. (iii) The barrier distribution is a second derivative: experimental data need energy steps of ≈ 1–2 MeV and high precision; the model curve is noise-free. (iv) For light systems (¹²C + ¹²C) resonances and the identical-particle symmetry dominate the low-energy behaviour, and astrophysics uses the modified S-factor \(S^* = E\sigma\,e^{87.21/\sqrt E + 0.46E}\).

A particle bound to, or decaying from, a core that can be excited does not stay with the core in its ground state: the coupling mixes in excited core states. The core may be a rotor, with the rotational band \(I^\pi = 0^+, 2^+, 4^+, \dots\) (\(\epsilon_I = E_{2^+}I(I+1)/6\)), or a vibrator, with its ground state and one phonon \(\lambda^\pi\) at \(\hbar\omega\); excited core states that are themselves resonances get a complex energy \(\epsilon_I - i\Gamma_I/2\). For a projectile of spin \(s\) (0 for α, 1/2 for nucleons, 1 for deuterons) the channels are \((l, j, I)\): the orbital angular momentum couples with the spin to \(j\), and \(j\) with the core spin \(I\) to the total \(J\) (half-integer for nucleons), with parity \(\pi = (-1)^l\,\pi_I\) (\(\pi_I = +\) for a rotational band, \((-1)^\lambda\) for a phonon); for \(s = 0\), \(j = l\) and the channels are the pairs \((l, I)\) with \(|l - I| \le J \le l + I\). The radial functions obey

\[ \begin{aligned} &\Bigl[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{d^2}{dr^2} + \frac{\hbar^2l_i(l_i+1)}{2\mu r^2} + \epsilon_{I_i} - E\Bigr]u_i(r) + \sum_j V_{ij}(r)\,u_j(r) = 0,\\ &V_{ij}(r) = \sum_{\lambda = 0, 2, 4, \dots}V_\lambda(r)\,G^\lambda_{ij}, \end{aligned} \](61)

where \(V(r,\gamma) = -V f\bigl(r; R(\gamma), a\bigr) + V_C(r,\gamma) = \sum_\lambda V_\lambda(r)P_\lambda(\cos\gamma)\), with \(R(\gamma) = R_0[1 + \beta_2Y_{20}(\gamma) + \beta_4Y_{40}(\gamma)]\). Any analytic shape of the left panel can be deformed: the radius is deformed for Woods–Saxon, WS², Gaussian and the square well; for the radius-free Yukawa and exponential shapes the range \(a(\gamma) = a[1 + \beta_2Y_{20} + \beta_4Y_{40}]\) is deformed (the tabulated potential cannot be). The nuclear multipoles are projected by 48-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature (and continued to complex \(r\)); for the square well the projection is exact: \(R\) depends only on \(t = \cos^2\gamma\) and is quadratic in \(t\), so the surface crossings \(R(t) = r\) are roots of a quadratic and \(\int P_\lambda\,dx = [P_{\lambda+1} - P_{\lambda-1}]/(2\lambda+1)\) and the Coulomb multipoles \(\frac{3Z_1Z_2e^2}{2\lambda+1}\frac{R_C^\lambda}{r^{\lambda+1}}\beta_\lambda^{\rm eff}\sqrt{\tfrac{2\lambda+1}{4\pi}}\) (to second order in \(\beta_2\)). The geometric coefficients for a \(K = 0\) rotor follow from Edmonds’ recoupling formula,

\[ G^\lambda_{ij} = \bigl\langle(l_iI_i)J\big|P_\lambda(\cos\gamma)\big|(l_jI_j)J\bigr\rangle = \tfrac{4\pi}{2\lambda+1}(-1)^{l_j+I_i+J}\begin{Bmatrix}J&I_i&l_i\\\lambda&l_j&I_j\end{Bmatrix}\langle l_i\|Y_\lambda\|l_j\rangle\langle I_i\|Y_\lambda\|I_j\rangle, \](62)

which for \(J = 0\) reduces to \(\sqrt{(2l_i+1)(2l_j+1)}\begin{pmatrix}l_i&\lambda&l_j\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}^2\) (checked to \(2\times10^{-15}\); the matrix is symmetric for every \(J\)).

With nucleon spin. The deformed potential acts on the orbital motion and on the core, not on the spin, so the coefficient for channels \(((l_is)j_i, I_i)J\) follows from two recouplings (Edmonds 7.1.6 and 7.1.7),

\[ \begin{aligned} G^\lambda_{ij} &= \tfrac{4\pi}{2\lambda+1}(-1)^{j_j+I_i+J}\begin{Bmatrix}J&I_i&j_i\\\lambda&j_j&I_j\end{Bmatrix}\langle(l_is)j_i\|Y_\lambda\|(l_js)j_j\rangle\langle I_i\|Y_\lambda\|I_j\rangle,\\ \langle(l_is)j_i\|Y_\lambda\|(l_js)j_j\rangle &= (-1)^{l_i+s+j_j+\lambda}\sqrt{(2j_i+1)(2j_j+1)}\begin{Bmatrix}l_i&j_i&s\\j_j&l_j&\lambda\end{Bmatrix}\langle l_i\|Y_\lambda\|l_j\rangle, \end{aligned} \](63)

with 6j symbols of half-integer arguments (Racah formula in doubled units). For \(s = 0\) it reproduces the spinless coefficients to \(6\times10^{-16}\); for \(s = 1/2\) it agrees with SymPy’s exact Wigner symbols to \(2\times10^{-15}\) and is symmetric for every \(J^\pi\). The spin–orbit and surface terms of the left panel are added on the diagonal of each channel with its own \(\langle\boldsymbol l\cdot\boldsymbol s\rangle = [j(j+1) - l(l+1) - s(s+1)]/2\), using the page’s form factors (also at complex \(r\)); at zero deformation every channel is therefore exactly the single-channel problem with spin–orbit. The spin–orbit term itself is kept spherical (not deformed), a common simplification of particle–rotor models. For \(J^{\pi} = 1/2^{+}\) of a nucleon on a 0⁺, 2⁺, 4⁺ band the channels are s1/2 ⊗ 0⁺, d3/2 ⊗ 2⁺, d5/2 ⊗ 2⁺, g7/2 ⊗ 4⁺, g9/2 ⊗ 4⁺.

Solver

The pole \(E = E_r - i\Gamma/2\) is found by complex scaling. The \(N\) regular solutions are integrated outward from the origin on the real axis, the \(N\) outgoing (or decaying) solutions inward along the rotated ray \(r = R_m + se^{i\theta}\) and then along the real axis back into the well, both with a complex matrix Numerov recurrence that is re-orthogonalised every 10 steps (\(u \to uu_k^{-1}\)) so that channels with very different growth stay independent; the transformations are stored and applied to the coefficient vectors when the wave functions are assembled. With the log-derivative matrices at a matching radius inside the well,

\[ \mu_{\min}(E) = \text{eigenvalue of smallest modulus of } \bigl[Y_{\rm in}(E) - Y_{\rm out}(E)\bigr] = 0, \qquad Y = u'u^{-1}, \](64)

is solved by a complex secant. Three choices were essential and were found by testing: (i) the smallest eigenvalue instead of the determinant, because \(\det(Y_{\rm in} - Y_{\rm out})\) contains \(1/\det u(R)\) and is dense with poles in deep potentials; (ii) matching inside the well, because in the classically forbidden region \(Y_{\rm in}\) flips between \(\pm\kappa\) within an exponentially narrow energy window (for a deep α + ¹⁶O state it jumped from \(-2.1\) to \(+2.2\) fm⁻¹ within 1 keV); (iii) a contour long enough for every channel, closed ones included: the ¹¹Be halo (\(\kappa = 0.15\) fm⁻¹) needs \(S \approx 380\) fm, and too short a ray produced a spurious \(\mathrm{Im}\,E = -8\times10^{-6}\) MeV for a bound state. θ is raised automatically when a broad pole would lie outside the rotated region (never above 0.7 rad for a Gaussian, which is not dilation-analytic beyond π/4). The matching radius defaults to 0.8 times the outer turning point of the starting state, which keeps it inside the classically allowed region for every shape (a deep Yukawa state is allowed only inside \(\approx 0.6\) fm). For 1/r-singular potentials (Yukawa, point Coulomb) the regular solutions start as \(u_{ij} = r^{l_j+1}\delta_{ij} + c_{ij}r^{l_j+2}\), \(c_{ij} = S_{ij}/[(l_j+2)(l_j+1) - l_i(l_i+1)]\), \(S = \lim_{r\to0} rP(r)\), and the first Numerov step includes the finite limit of \(Fu\) at the origin; bound states (below every threshold) are located by a real secant, so \(\mathrm{Im}\,E = 0\) exactly.

The state is identified by continuation: at zero deformation the channels decouple and the pole is the single-channel pole of the chosen channel (shifted by \(\epsilon_I\)); the deformation is then switched on step by step. Each step is accepted only if (a) the pole stays close to a quadratic prediction from the path, (b) a full step moves the pole at most about 4.5 times as far as a half step (2 for a linear, 4 for a quadratic path; a jump between poles gave a ratio \(\approx 8\) in testing), and (c) two half steps land on the same pole as one full step; otherwise the step is halved. A depth fit (secant on \(V\)) follows the coupled pole itself, because at the fitted depth the uncoupled state may not exist at all (for ¹¹Be it becomes a virtual state).

\[ W_i = \frac{\int_C u_i^2\,dr}{\sum_j\int_C u_j^2\,dr}, \qquad \Gamma_i = \Gamma\,\frac{j_i}{\sum_j j_j}, \quad j_i = \mathrm{Im}\bigl(u_i^*u_i'\bigr)\big|_{r = R_m + 25\ {\rm fm}}, \](65)

Scattering at real energy: S-matrix, eigenphases and cross sections

The same channels, potential and couplings are also solved at real energies. The regular solutions are integrated outward on the real axis to \(R_f\) beyond the couplings and matched, through the log-derivative matrix, to unit-amplitude Coulomb waves in open channels and decaying waves in closed ones:

\[ \begin{gathered} u = H^-a - H^+S\,a, \qquad S = \bigl(YH^+ - H^{+\prime}\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(YH^- - H^{-\prime}\bigr), \qquad \tilde S_{ij} = S_{ij}\sqrt{k_i/k_j}, \\ \tilde S\,\boldsymbol v_k = e^{2i\delta_k}\boldsymbol v_k, \qquad \textstyle\sum_k\delta_k = \tfrac12\arg\det\tilde S, \qquad \sigma_J(a\to b) = \dfrac{\pi}{k_a^2}\,\dfrac{2J+1}{2s+1}\,\bigl|\delta_{ab} - \tilde S_{ba}\bigr|^2 . \end{gathered} \](66)

\(\tilde S\) is unitary and symmetric. At every pole the eigenphase sum rises by π; near an isolated resonance \(\sum_k\delta_k \approx \delta_{\rm bg} + \arctan\frac{\Gamma/2}{E_r - E}\), so the steepest slope gives \(E_r\) and \(\Gamma = 2/(d\sum\delta/dE)_{\max}\) — an independent check of the complex-scaling pole solver. The cross sections are the contributions of the chosen \(J^\pi\) to elastic scattering (entrance channels with the core in its ground state) and to inelastic excitation of each core state; core-state widths are not used at real energy.

Vibrational cores and core states that are resonances

One-phonon vibrator. Instead of a rotational band the core may be a vibrator with its ground state 0⁺ and one phonon of multipolarity λ (2⁺ for a quadrupole, 3⁻ for an octupole vibration) at \(\hbar\omega\). The core parity \((-1)^\lambda\) enters the channel parity, \((-1)^l\,\pi_I = \pi\), so a 3⁻ core pairs with odd \(l\) in a positive-parity state. The coupling is linear in the phonon amplitude (the standard vibrational model), acts only between the ground state and the one-phonon state, and is normalised so that the 0⁺ ↔ λ coupling equals that of a rotor with the same \(\beta_\lambda\) (same \(B(E\lambda)\!\uparrow\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} &V^{\rm vib}_{ij}(r) = \beta_\lambda\sqrt{\tfrac{2\lambda+1}{4\pi}}\Bigl[-R_0\frac{dV_N}{dr} + \frac{3Z_1Z_2e^2}{2\lambda+1}\frac{R_C^\lambda}{r^{\lambda+1}}\Bigr]G^\lambda_{ij}\quad (I_i = 0,\ I_j = \lambda \text{ or vice versa}),\\ &\epsilon_I \to \epsilon_I - \tfrac{i}{2}\Gamma_I . \end{aligned} \](68)

Unlike a rotor there is no reorientation term in the phonon state and no coupling beyond 0⁺ ↔ λ. Checks: at \(\beta = 0\) the single-channel state is reproduced to \(10^{-10}\) MeV; θ and the step change a coupled state by \(10^{-8}\) MeV; the form factor equals \(-\beta R_0\sqrt{7/4\pi}\,dV_N/dr\) to \(3\times10^{-8}\). Example (¹⁷O preset): the neutron d-wave resonance coupled to the ¹⁶O 3⁻ phonon (6.13 MeV, \(\beta_{3} = 0.733\), CCFULL deck values), with the depth fitted to 0.942 MeV, has \(\Gamma = 116\) keV, against 207 keV without the phonon and 96 keV measured; only its d ⊗ 0⁺ component (56 %) can emit the neutron to the ¹⁶O ground state.

Core states with a width. An excited core state that is itself a resonance (⁸Be(2⁺), \(\Gamma = 1.5\) MeV) is included through a complex excitation energy \(\epsilon_I - i\Gamma_I/2\) in its channels (second part of the equation above). The pole then contains the decay of the core: \(-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,E \approx \sum_c\Gamma_c + \sum_i\mathrm{Re}\,W_i\,\Gamma_{I_i}\), particle emission plus core decay weighted by the channel weights. Checks: without coupling the pole of a channel is exactly \(E_{sc} + \epsilon_I - i\Gamma_I/2\); θ-independence holds to \(4\times10^{-8}\) MeV; for the Hoyle model the core term accounts for 98 % of the width. Caution: a constant \(\Gamma_I\) is appropriate when the channel is open or nearly so. In the Hoyle model the α + ⁸Be(2⁺) channel is closed by 2.7 MeV, so the two α particles of the core have about 0.4 MeV available instead of 3.1 MeV and their \(l = 2\) barrier suppresses the decay enormously; a constant 1.5 MeV makes the Hoyle state 0.36 MeV wide instead of 8.5 eV. Energy-dependent core widths are needed for such cases (not included); the real-energy flux widths are not computed when core widths are present.

Wave functions and potentials: how to read the plots

The wave function. The full state is a sum over channels, each a radial function times a coupled angular–core function,

\[ \Psi_{JM} = \sum_i \frac{u_i(r)}{r}\,\bigl[Y_{l_i}(\hat r)\otimes\phi_{I_i}\bigr]_{JM}, \qquad \sum_i\int_C u_i^2\,dr = 1, \qquad W_i = \int_C u_i^2\,dr . \](70)

The channel functions \(u_i\) are what the tab plots; \(W_i\) is the probability that the core is in state \(I_i\) (the weights in the table), related to the spectroscopic factor of that core state. Near the origin \(u_i \propto r^{l_i+1}\), so channels with higher \(l\) start later; at large \(r\) every closed channel decays as \(e^{-\kappa_ir}\), \(\kappa_i = \sqrt{K(\epsilon_i - E)}\), and the most strongly closed channels die out first. On the contour plot (real axis, then the rotated ray, logarithmic scale) these decay rates appear as straight lines: for the fitted ¹¹Be state the 0⁺-core halo (\(\kappa = 0.15\) fm⁻¹) decays slowly, the 2⁺-core channel, closed by 3.87 MeV (\(\kappa = 0.41\) fm⁻¹), much faster. An open channel of a Gamow state grows on the real axis like \(e^{|\mathrm{Im}\,k|r}\) and decays on the ray, which is the purpose of complex scaling.

Re ψ and Im ψ. A bound state is real (Im \(\psi = 0\) exactly). For a Gamow state the c-product normalisation fixes ψ up to an overall sign, and the imaginary part measures how far the state is from being bound: for a narrow resonance Im ψ is small and grows with the width. For the Hoyle state (\(\Gamma = 6.1\) eV) \(\max|\mathrm{Im}\,\psi|/\max|\mathrm{Re}\,\psi| \approx 6\times10^{-5}\), which is why the single-channel plot draws Im ψ on its own right-hand axis. The channels themselves can also differ by orders of magnitude (for the Hoyle state with three levels the weights are 0.70, 0.27 and 0.03); the plot can therefore show them as computed, each scaled to a maximum of 1 (the factor is given in the legend), or in separate panels with their own scales.

The potentials. The equation of channel \(i\) contains the effective potential and the couplings,

\[ V^{\rm eff}_i(r) = V_{ii}(r) + \frac{\hbar^2l_i(l_i+1)}{2\mu r^2} + \epsilon_{I_i}, \qquad V_{ij}(r) = \sum_\lambda V_\lambda(r)\,G^\lambda_{ij}\ \ (i \ne j), \](71)

where the diagonal \(V_{ii} = V_0 + \sum_{\lambda>0}V_\lambda G^\lambda_{ii}\) contains, besides the monopole, the reorientation term of the excited core states (\(G^2_{ii} \ne 0\) for \(I_i \ge 2\); for \(J = 0\), \(G^2_{22} = 2/7\)). Each \(V^{\rm eff}_i\) rises to its own threshold \(\epsilon_{I_i}\) at large \(r\); a channel whose threshold lies above the state energy is closed. To first order in the deformation the multipoles are proportional to the slope of the nuclear potential,

\[ \begin{aligned} &V_N\bigl(r - R_0\textstyle\sum_\lambda\beta_\lambda Y_{\lambda0}\bigr) \approx V_N(r) - R_0\dfrac{dV_N}{dr}\sum_\lambda\beta_\lambda Y_{\lambda0}(\gamma)\\ &\Rightarrow\quad V_\lambda^{(1)}(r) = -\beta_\lambda R_0\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda+1}{4\pi}}\,\frac{dV_N}{dr}, \end{aligned} \](72)

so the couplings are concentrated at the nuclear surface, where \(dV_N/dr\) peaks: this is why core excitation is a surface effect and why the coupling plots peak near \(R_0\). The page does not use this approximation; it projects the deformed potential exactly. For the Hoyle example (\(\beta_{2} = 0.3\), \(R_0 = 4.48\) fm) the exact \(V_2\) peaks at 4.60 fm with −12.8 MeV and agrees with the first-order form to 4–10 % near the surface; the higher orders generate a hexadecapole of up to ±1.6 MeV although \(\beta_{4} = 0\), and shift the monopole by up to ±0.6 MeV. The directional plot shows the potential seen along a fixed direction,

\[ V(r,\gamma) = \sum_\lambda V_\lambda(r)\,P_\lambda(\cos\gamma), \qquad \gamma = 0^\circ\ \text{(tip)},\ 90^\circ\ \text{(equator)},\ 54.7^\circ\ (P_2 = 0), \](73)

for a prolate core (β₂ > 0) the surface lies \(R_0\beta_2[Y_{20}(0^\circ) - Y_{20}(90^\circ)] = \tfrac34\sqrt{5/\pi}\,R_0\beta_2\) further out along the tip than along the equator (\(\approx 1.3\) fm for the Hoyle example), and at the magic angle it nearly coincides with the spherical potential. All plotted potentials come from the solver’s own potential function: the multipoles summed with the \(G^\lambda_{ij}\) reproduce the coupling matrix to \(10^{-14}\).

17Electromagnetic transitions

For two spin-0 clusters the electric multipole operator of the relative motion is \(e_L\,r^LY_{LM}\) with \(e_L = Z_p(A_t/A)^L + Z_t(-A_p/A)^L\); for equal charge-to-mass ratios (α + α, α + ⁸Be, α + ¹⁶O) \(e_1 = 0\), so E1 vanishes at this order. With states \(J = l\),

\[ B(EL;\,l_i\to l_f) = \frac{2L+1}{4\pi}\,e_L^2\,\langle l_i\,0\,L\,0\,|\,l_f\,0\rangle^2\,I^2, \qquad I = \int_0^{R_m}u_f\,r^L\,u_i\,dr + e^{i\theta}\!\int_0^S u_f(z)\,z^L\,u_i(z)\,ds,\ \ z = R_m + s\,e^{i\theta}. \](74)

Both states are c-normalised on the same contour and multiplied without complex conjugation (Berggren). For bound states \(I\) is the ordinary real overlap (agreement 5 × 10⁻⁵ with a direct real-axis integral); with Gamow states \(B\) is complex and independent of θ (10⁻¹⁰ when θ changes by 0.2 rad); \(\mathrm{Re}\,B\) is the meaningful part when \(|\mathrm{Im}\,B| \ll |\mathrm{Re}\,B|\). The γ width and the Weisskopf unit are

\[ \Gamma_\gamma = \frac{8\pi(L+1)}{L\,[(2L+1)!!]^2}\Bigl(\frac{E_\gamma}{\hbar c}\Bigr)^{2L+1} e^2\,B(EL), \qquad B_W(EL) = \frac{1.2^{2L}}{4\pi}\Bigl(\frac{3}{L+3}\Bigr)^2A^{2L/3}\ e^2{\rm fm}^{2L}, \](75)

with \(E_\gamma = \mathrm{Re}(E_i - E_f)\), and \(B(EL;\,l_f\to l_i) = \frac{2l_i+1}{2l_f+1}B(EL;\,l_i\to l_f)\).

Pinpoints. The clusters are taken as spin 0 with \(J = l\); a spin-½ projectile with spin–orbit coupling would need \(j\)-coupled matrix elements (not included). The effective charge contains the centre-of-mass correction exactly; for \(Z_p/A_p = Z_t/A_t\) the E1 operator vanishes and isospin-forbidden E1 transitions require configurations beyond this model. The long-wavelength form (75) is used for Γγ. For transitions involving broad resonances \(|{\rm Im}\,B|\) can be as large as \({\rm Re}\,B\); then B characterises the pole but does not map directly onto a measured number.

Shared integrator. All auxiliary solutions of \(u'' = f(r)u\) (outward and inward continuations, Whittaker and outgoing Coulomb solutions, scattering wave functions, the under-barrier \(F\) and \(G\) of the effective-range module) use one routine, numerov(f, r0, H, n, u0, u1, visit, opt): equidistant grid \(r_j = r_0 + jH\) (\(H < 0\) inward), complex \(f\) and \(u\), an optional start term \((fu)(0)\) for singular origins, and automatic rescaling with a running logarithmic scale. Only the three innermost loops (the interior and exterior solutions of the complex-scaling model and the GAMOW port) keep hand-specialised copies, because they are called thousands of times per solve.

Background computation. At start-up the page builds a Web Worker from its own physics scripts. Every heavy job is sent there, so the page stays responsive (about 5 ms latency during a 40-second α-decay run). A job bar shows progress and a Cancel button, which terminates the worker immediately and starts a fresh one. Where workers are not allowed, the same jobs run on the page between screen updates; Cancel then stops at the next item.

Regression suite. A Node test suite (tests/run.js, distributed with the sources) recomputes 175 reference quantities in 53 cases: poles of all solvers, absorptive and tabulated potentials, ANC and Thomas widths, Coulomb functions, phase shifts, effective-range parameters, scattering wave functions, angular distributions, penetrabilities, rates, B(E2) between Gamow states, direct-capture and fusion cross sections, α-decay depths and widths, and α-decay fine structure. All must agree with the stored values to \(10^{-9}\) relative (\(10^{-8}\) for the extrapolated scattering length). It runs in about 3 s; node tests/run.js --update records new reference values after an intended change.

23Workflow

  1. System. Pick a preset or enter the nuclei (mass excesses in keV), the Coulomb radius and the potential boxes; switch on surface, spin–orbit, absorption or an external table as needed.
  2. State. Choose l and n (use \(2n + l = G\) for cluster bands) and a method; press Solve. Read the checks table: all ✓ means a true bound or quasi-bound state of this potential.
  3. Spectrum and scan. Compute the band or a grid of (n, l); use the Energy scan tab to see all poles in a window, including those not connected to a bound state.
  4. Adjust. Fit V (and a or r) to measured E and Γ; check the correlation.
  5. Observables. ANC and reduced widths, EM transitions, scattering (phase shifts, cross sections, angular distributions, excitation functions, low-energy parameters) and astrophysical rates are all built on the solved state and potential.
  6. Core excitation. If the core can be excited, open the Coupled channels tab: choose a rotational band (E(2⁺), β₂, β₄, number of levels) or a one-phonon vibration (λπ, ħω, βλ), optionally the widths of the excited core states, and the state (J, parity, start channel, nodes; set the projectile spin and spin–orbit term in the left panel); solve, and fit V to the measured energy. Compare the channel weights with spectroscopic factors, and the partial widths with branching ratios; for very narrow states (proton emitters) the half-life comes from the real-energy flux, with the occupation u² of the orbit. Check which state was reached (weights, fitted depth), and add levels until the results stop changing.
  7. Export. Every plot and table exports to PNG, SVG, CSV or DAT.

How it works

Masses come from A·u + Δ − Z·me with ħc = 197.3269804 MeV fm, e² = 1.43996448 MeV fm and u = 931.49410242 MeV. Radii follow the FRESCO convention R = r(Ap1/3 + At1/3).

Inside the matching radius Rm the regular solution is integrated outward with Numerov on the real axis at complex E. Outside, the equation is solved on the rotated line \(r = R_m + s\,e^{i\theta}\), inward from a point where the outgoing (or decaying) wave has fallen by e−20 relative to the other solution. The pole condition is continuity of u and u′ at Rm, solved by complex secant.

A Gamow state grows on the real axis, so it cannot be normalised with |u|². The integral ∫u² dr (no complex conjugate) is taken along the real axis to Rm and then along the rotated contour, where the integrand decays; the result does not depend on θ. For bound states this equals the ordinary ∫|u|² dr, which is checked separately.

The state (n, l) is found without guessing: if it is bound, Sturm node counting brackets it exactly; if not, V is deepened until it binds and the pole is followed back to the true depth. For very narrow states (Γ below about 10⁻⁹ MeV, e.g. α decay) Im E is at the level of rounding error, so the width is taken from the real-energy flux formula Γ = (ħ²/μ)k|u(R)|²/(|H⁺(R)|²∫₀R|u|²dr).
