START HERE — How to set up a reaction, parameters and quantum numbers

This practical walkthrough explains what to enter before running a calculation. Use a literature-backed preset as a starting point when available, then verify every nuclear property and potential parameter against your chosen reference. A preset is not proof that every field is experimentally evaluated.

Recommended first calculation: choose one reaction and one final bound-state configuration, solve that bound state, inspect its energy and wavefunction, then calculate the continuum phase shift and capture. Do not start by changing many parameters at once.

Step 1 — Define the reaction (Tab 1: Reaction Setup)

  1. Core a: enter the target/core nucleus, its mass number Aa, charge Za, and ground-state spin Ia.
  2. Captured particle b: enter the particle (often p or n), Ab, Zb, and intrinsic spin Ib=s. For a proton or neutron, s=1/2.
  3. Final nucleus c: enter the compound nucleus formed by capture. Check that its A and Z are consistent with a+b.
  4. Mass unit: this app approximates mx≈AxmN. Keep the default unless you have a specific mass convention to test; record any change because calculated energies can be sensitive to it.
  5. Separation energy |EB|: enter a positive value in MeV for the final bound state. Use a sourced experimental value when possible. This is the target binding energy for the fit buttons.
  6. Spectroscopic factor C²S: enter a value justified by your structure model or reference. It scales the single-particle capture contribution; it is not automatically inferred by this app.

Step 2 — Choose the quantum numbers for the bound state

The angular momenta are coupled in this order:

\(\boldsymbol{j}=\boldsymbol{l}+\boldsymbol{s},\qquad \boldsymbol{J}=\boldsymbol{j}+\boldsymbol{I}_a\)
  • l: orbital angular momentum of the captured particle relative to the core. l=0,1,2,3 correspond to s, p, d, f waves.
  • j: total angular momentum of the captured particle, combining l and its intrinsic spin s. For a spin-1/2 particle, j=l±1/2; for l=0 only j=1/2 is allowed.
  • J: total angular momentum of the final single-particle channel, coupling j to core spin Ia. It must satisfy |j−Ia|≤J≤j+Ia, in integer steps.
  • N (nodes): number of radial nodes of the reduced radial wavefunction u(r), excluding the origin. This identifies the radial solution, not the orbital angular momentum. If unsure, test plausible node counts and check the resulting wavefunction and energy against known level assignments.
  • Parity: for a core of parity πa, the configuration has π=πa(−1)l. Check this against the assigned final-state parity.

Example of angular-momentum bookkeeping: for a proton (s=1/2) in an l=1 partial wave, j can be 1/2 or 3/2. If the core has Ia=3/2, then j=3/2 permits J=0,1,2,3, while j=1/2 permits J=1,2. These are allowed couplings only; select the one matching the physical state being modelled.

Step 3 — Set the nuclear potential (Tab 2)

  • Central depth V₀: controls the attraction. A deeper well generally supports more bound states and shifts a given state downward.
  • Radius and diffuseness (R₀, a₀): control the size and surface fall-off. Use a radius convention and parameters consistent with your chosen reference; do not mix conventions without documenting it.
  • Spin–orbit strength and geometry: these affect channels with nonzero l and split j=l±1/2 partners. The expectation value is [j(j+1)−l(l+1)−s(s+1)]/2.
  • Coulomb term: active for charged core/particle combinations; it vanishes when ZaZb=0.
  • Potential shape: Woods–Saxon is the standard choice in this implementation. The Gaussian option is explicitly an extension. M3Y folding requires sensible densities and folding inputs; it is not parameter-free.

Start with a cited potential set or the loaded example. Change one parameter at a time and note the effect on the energy, wavefunction, phase shift and capture result. “Fit V₀ to |EB|” adjusts the depth to reproduce the target binding energy while holding other settings fixed; it does not validate the entire potential.

Step 4 — Solve and check the bound state (Tab 3)

  1. Set Rmax large enough that the tail is small at the boundary.
  2. Use a sufficiently fine radial mesh; increase mesh points and Rmax to test stability.
  3. Press Solve bound state. Check the calculated energy, node count and wavefunction shape.
  4. If matching a known separation energy, use Fit V₀ to |EB|, then solve again and run the convergence test. A fitted energy alone does not guarantee a reliable asymptotic tail.

Step 5 — Set continuum channels (Tab 4)

Choose continuum l and j for the incoming partial wave. These describe the scattering channel and need not equal the final bound-state l and j. Set a positive energy interval, enough energy points to resolve rapid phase-shift variation, a matching radius outside the nuclear interaction, and a small radial step h. If the nuclear potential is intended to be the same, use Copy tab-2 parameters; otherwise document why the continuum potential differs.

Step 6 — Calculate transitions and capture (Tabs 6–7)

  • In Tab 6, define the initial bound-state and final bound/continuum quantum numbers separately. Select E1, E2 or M1 according to the transition and check angular-momentum/parity selection rules.
  • In Tab 7, verify the final-state quantum numbers, C²S, entrance partial-wave channels and multipolarities. Include only channels justified by the model or reference.
  • For M1, provide the required magnetic moments; the app does not assume them. Use a sourced value and its sign/convention.
  • Choose the energy range and mesh to cover the region of interest. Run the calculation, inspect the plot and numerical messages, then export the table if you need independent checks.

Step 7 — Validate before interpreting results

Use Tab 10 diagnostics and benchmark tests. Repeat calculations with a finer mesh, larger radial extent and reasonable variations of potential geometry. Distinguish numerical convergence from model dependence: a stable result can still depend strongly on the assumed potential, spectroscopic factor or channel list. Record the reaction, quantum numbers, units, potential parameters, data source and convergence settings with any reported result.

Common setup mistakes
  • Entering a negative separation energy when the field asks for positive |EB|.
  • Choosing j or J values that violate angular-momentum coupling rules.
  • Confusing N (radial nodes) with l (orbital angular momentum).
  • Using a coarse energy grid that misses a narrow resonance, or a matching radius inside the nuclear potential.
  • Treating a fitted V₀, a preset, or C²S=1 as experimental evidence.
  • Comparing results that use different mass units, potential conventions, normalizations or channel definitions without accounting for the differences.
1. Reaction, angular momentum and two-body kinematics

The direct-capture reaction is \(a+b\rightarrow c+\gamma\), where \(a\) is an inert core and \(b\) is the captured particle. The final nucleus \(c\) is represented by a single-particle configuration \(b\otimes a\). With mass numbers \(A_a,A_b\), charge numbers \(Z_a,Z_b\), core spin \(I_a\), and captured-particle spin \(s=I_b\), the reduced mass is

$$\mu=\frac{m_a m_b}{m_a+m_b},\qquad m_x\simeq A_x m_N.$$

The relative-motion wave number and energy are related by

$$E=\frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2\mu},\qquad k=\frac{\sqrt{2\mu E}}{\hbar};\quad \hbar=\frac{\hbar c}{c}.$$

In the numerical convention where masses are entered in MeV/c² and \(c=1\), use \(\hbar^2/(2\mu)=(\hbar c)^2/(2\mu c^2)\) in MeV fm². For a photon of energy \(E_\gamma\), \(k_\gamma=E_\gamma/(\hbar c)\) in fm⁻¹. Neglecting recoil, \(E_\gamma\simeq E+S_b\), where \(S_b\) is the final-state separation energy; exact recoil corrections replace this by two-body energy-momentum conservation.

Angular momenta couple as \(\boldsymbol{j}=\boldsymbol{l}+\boldsymbol{s}\) and \(\boldsymbol{J}=\boldsymbol{j}+\boldsymbol{I}_a\). Thus \(|l-s|\le j\le l+s\) and \(|j-I_a|\le J\le j+I_a\). For a spin-1/2 captured particle, \(j=l\pm\tfrac12\) (with only \(j=1/2\) for \(l=0\)); parity is \(\pi=\pi_a(-1)^l\).

2. Radial Schrödinger equation and potentials

The reduced radial wave function \(u_{lj}(r)=rR_{lj}(r)\) satisfies the single-channel equation

$$\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{d^2}{dr^2}+\frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2\mu r^2}+V_{lj}(r)\right]u_{lj}(r)=E u_{lj}(r).$$

The total interaction may contain a central nuclear term, spin-orbit term, and Coulomb term:

$$V_{lj}(r)=V_c(r)+V_{\rm so}(r)\langle\boldsymbol l\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol s\rangle+V_C(r).$$ $$\langle\boldsymbol l\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol s\rangle=\frac{j(j+1)-l(l+1)-s(s+1)}{2}.$$

For a Woods–Saxon form, define \(f(r;R,a)=[1+\exp((r-R)/a)]^{-1}\), with \(R=r_0 A^{1/3}\) or the radius convention selected in the potential controls. Then \(V_c(r)=-V_0 f(r;R_0,a_0)\). A common derivative spin-orbit convention is

$$V_{\rm so}(r)= -V_{S0}\left(\frac{\hbar}{m_\pi c}\right)^2\frac1r\frac{df(r;R_S,a_S)}{dr},$$

with the overall sign and strength interpreted according to the controls' convention. The Coulomb interaction for a uniformly charged sphere of radius \(R_C\) is

$$V_C(r)=Z_aZ_b e^2\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2R_C}\left(3-\dfrac{r^2}{R_C^2}\right),&rFor neutral particles, \(Z_aZ_b=0\) and this term vanishes. The centrifugal barrier is the explicit \(\hbar^2l(l+1)/(2\mu r^2)\) term. A Gaussian central potential \(V(r)=V_0\exp[-(r/R_0)^2]\) is provided as an explicitly labelled extension, not as a standard RADCAP Woods–Saxon option.

M3Y folding extension. A microscopic-inspired central interaction can be obtained by folding an effective nucleon–nucleon interaction with projectile and target densities:

$$V_F(\boldsymbol R)=\int d^3r_a\,d^3r_b\;\rho_a(\boldsymbol r_a)\rho_b(\boldsymbol r_b)\,v_{NN}(\boldsymbol R+\boldsymbol r_b-\boldsymbol r_a).$$

The implementation provides the paper's momentum-space folding parameterization and density options. The resulting potential depends on the supplied densities and folding parameters; it is not a parameter-free prediction.

3. Boundary conditions, bound states and continuum scattering

At the origin the regular radial solution behaves as \(u_l(r)\propto r^{l+1}\). A bound state with \(E=-S<0\) must decay asymptotically. For a neutral channel, \(u(r)\propto e^{-\kappa r}\) up to the angular-momentum asymptotic polynomial, where \(\kappa=\sqrt{2\mu S}/\hbar\). For a charged bound state, the asymptotic solution is a Whittaker function, conventionally \(W_{-\eta_B,l+1/2}(2\kappa r)\), with \(\eta_B=Z_aZ_b e^2\mu/(\hbar^2\kappa)\).

Implemented bound-state method. The app propagates the radial equation with Numerov, brackets an eigenvalue using the node count and bisection, then constructs the displayed wavefunction by outward/inward Numerov matching near the outer classical turning point. The original code uses a WKB start; this app's method is independent. The finite mesh and outer boundary introduce numerical errors, so use the convergence test and vary mesh points and maximum radius.

Implemented continuum method. The app uses outward Numerov integration, estimates the logarithmic derivative at the matching radius with a five-point derivative, and matches to neutral Riccati–Bessel functions or charged-particle Coulomb reference functions. The charged-function implementation uses an asymptotic series, inward RK4 for the irregular function, and a continued-fraction/Wronskian construction for the regular function; this is a numerical approximation, not a general-purpose Coulomb-function library. In a neutral channel one common convention is

$$u_l(r)\underset{r\to\infty}{\longrightarrow} C\,[F_l(kr)\cos\delta_l+G_l(kr)\sin\delta_l],$$

where \(F_l,G_l\) are regular/irregular reference functions and \(\delta_l(E)\) is the nuclear phase shift (with Coulomb phase separated in charged channels). The logarithmic derivative \(u'(r)/u(r)\) is matched to the reference solution at a radius where the nuclear interaction is negligible. A rapid phase-shift rise through approximately \(\pi/2\) can indicate a resonance.

4. Resonance energies and widths

A narrow isolated resonance is characterized by a pole near \(E_p=E_R-i\Gamma/2\), with resonance energy \(E_R\) and width \(\Gamma>0\). Near an isolated resonance, the phase shift often has the approximate form

$$\delta_l(E)\simeq\delta_{\rm bg}(E)+\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\Gamma(E)/2}{E_R-E}\right).$$

For a sufficiently narrow resonance, a useful estimate is \(\Gamma\simeq 2/[d\delta/dE]_{E_R}\) when the phase-shift convention and background permit it. This is not universal for broad or overlapping resonances. The app's phase-shift diagnostics locate rapid variation and refine candidate energies; such single-particle widths depend on the potential and are not automatically experimental widths. Complex-energy pole methods, when used, require outgoing-wave boundary conditions and careful analytic continuation.

5. Electromagnetic operators and reduced transition strengths

In the long-wavelength approximation, the electric multipole operator for two clusters is written using an effective charge acting on the relative coordinate:

$$\mathcal M(E\lambda,\mu)=e_{\rm eff}^{(E\lambda)}r^\lambda Y_{\lambda\mu}(\hat r).$$

For mass-number-proportional cluster masses, the relative-coordinate effective charge is

$$e_{\rm eff}^{(E\lambda)}=e\left[Z_b\left(\frac{A_a}{A_a+A_b}\right)^\lambda+(-1)^\lambda Z_a\left(\frac{A_b}{A_a+A_b}\right)^\lambda\right].$$

In particular, \(E1\) is isoscalar-suppressed when the charge-to-mass ratios of the two clusters are equal; \(E2\) generally does not vanish. Effective-charge conventions can differ for composite clusters, so inspect the selected convention in the controls.

The reduced transition probability is defined by the Wigner–Eckart convention

$$B(E\lambda;J_i\to J_f)=\frac{1}{2J_i+1}\left|\langle J_f\Vert\mathcal M(E\lambda)\Vert J_i\rangle\right|^2.$$

The radial part of a capture matrix element is an overlap between the initial continuum and final bound radial functions:

$$I_{E\lambda}=\int_0^\infty u_f(r)\,r^\lambda\,u_i(r)\,dr,$$

multiplied by effective charge and angular-momentum recoupling factors (Clebsch–Gordan, 3j, and 6j coefficients). The continuum normalization matters: energy-normalized and momentum-normalized waves differ by a density-of-states factor. Magnetic transitions (M1) involve orbital, spin, and core magnetic operators rather than the electric effective-charge expression. Long-wavelength formulas require \(k_\gamma R\ll1\); retardation and higher-order corrections are omitted here.

For continuum strength distributions, \(dB/dE\) is the strength per unit energy. Since \(E=\hbar^2k^2/(2\mu)\), \(dE/dk=\hbar^2k/\mu\) and \(dB/dE=(\mu/\hbar^2k)\,dB/dk\). The app calculates radial overlaps on a finite mesh; increase the radial extent and mesh density to test convergence. M1 results additionally require user-supplied magnetic moments; no moments are silently assumed.

6. Direct-capture cross section and astrophysical S-factor

Radiative capture is calculated from the electromagnetic transition strength from continuum entrance channels to the selected bound final states. A commonly used multipole expression has the structure

$$\sigma_{E\lambda}(E)=\mathcal C_{E\lambda}\,\frac{k_\gamma^{2\lambda+1}}{k^2}\,\frac{dB(E\lambda)}{dE},$$

where \(\mathcal C_{E\lambda}\) contains the multipole-dependent electromagnetic and spin/statistical factors. The exact prefactors depend on the definition of \(B\), continuum normalization, and unit convention; the implementation uses its explicit channel-summed formulas rather than this schematic relation alone. Contributions from orthogonal, incoherently summed channels are added as cross sections; amplitudes interfere only when the channels and quantum numbers permit it and an interference model is specified.

Implemented observable. The capture modules evaluate explicit multipole- and channel-summed expressions from the computed \(dB/dE\), rather than the schematic expression alone. In the extended panel, contributions are grouped by final state, multipolarity or channel. Optional Breit–Wigner resonance contributions are a separate model term; interference is only the explicitly selected simplified option, not a general coherent multichannel calculation. For charged-particle reactions, the astrophysical S-factor removes the leading Coulomb penetrability and geometric energy dependence:

$$S(E)=E\,\sigma(E)\,e^{2\pi\eta},\qquad \eta=\frac{Z_aZ_b e^2}{\hbar v}=\frac{Z_aZ_b e^2\mu}{\hbar^2 k}.$$

Here \(v=\hbar k/\mu\). The exponential factor is the Gamow penetration factor's inverse for point-charge Coulomb repulsion. For neutral entrance channels this charged-particle definition is not useful; the tool reports the corresponding cross-section/rate quantities instead. Unit conversions must be tracked: \(1\,\mathrm{fm}^2=10\,\mathrm{mb}\), and \(1\,\mathrm{b}=100\,\mathrm{fm}^2\).

7. Photodissociation and detailed balance

The inverse reaction is related to capture by time-reversal invariance. Schematically, for \(a+b\leftrightarrow c+\gamma\),

$$\sigma_{\gamma c\to ab}(E_\gamma)=\mathcal G\left(\frac{k}{k_\gamma}\right)^2\sigma_{ab\to c\gamma}(E),$$

where \(\mathcal G\) is the ratio of initial/final spin statistical factors, including the photon polarization convention, and identical-particle factors must be treated consistently. This relation is often called detailed balance. It links cross sections at kinematically corresponding energies; the photon energy is above the breakup threshold by the final relative kinetic energy, with recoil corrections if retained. Always use the code's explicit spin-factor convention when comparing tabulated results.

8. Thermonuclear reaction rates

For a non-degenerate, non-relativistic Maxwell–Boltzmann plasma at temperature \(T\), the two-body thermal rate is the relative-velocity average

$$\langle\sigma v\rangle=\left(\frac{8}{\pi\mu}\right)^{1/2}(k_BT)^{-3/2}\int_0^\infty \sigma(E)\,E\,e^{-E/(k_BT)}\,dE.$$

Implemented integration limits and source. The rate module thermally averages the tabulated cross section from the extended-capture panel over its available energy grid. It reports the contribution/weight outside that grid; only when the below-minimum extrapolation option is checked does it extend below \(E_{\min}\), using constant \(S(E)\) for charged particles or \(\sigma\propto1/v\) for neutral particles. It does not extrapolate above \(E_{\max}\). The rate prefactor is evaluated in the app's documented astrophysical unit convention and converted to \(\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}}\); \(N_A\langle\sigma v\rangle\) is also reported. For identical reactants, the pair-counting factor \(1/(1+\delta_{ab})\) applies to the volumetric rate, while the code applies the corresponding factor in the resonance strength convention as documented in tab 9. Note that the tab 9 direct thermal rate uses the extended-capture result, not the separate direct-capture panel.

For a narrow, isolated resonance, the Maxwellian-averaged rate can be approximated by

$$N_A\langle\sigma v\rangle_r=N_A\left(\frac{2\pi}{\mu k_BT}\right)^{3/2}\hbar^2(\omega\gamma)e^{-E_R/(k_BT)},$$

where \(\omega\gamma\) is the resonance strength, typically \(\omega\gamma=\frac{2J+1}{(2I_a+1)(2I_b+1)}\frac{\Gamma_a\Gamma_\gamma}{\Gamma_{\rm tot}}\) for the specified entrance and radiative exit channels. The narrow-resonance approximation requires \(\Gamma\ll k_BT\) and an isolated resonance. Numerical integration is preferable for broad resonances or non-resonant capture.

The Gamow peak reflects the competition between the Maxwell factor \(e^{-E/k_BT}\) and Coulomb tunneling \(e^{-2\pi\eta}\). Its approximate energy for charged particles is

$$E_0=\left[\frac{(\pi Z_aZ_b e^2)^2\mu(k_BT)^2}{2\hbar^2}\right]^{1/3},\qquad \Delta\approx4\sqrt{\frac{E_0k_BT}{3}}.$$

These estimates are guides to the important energy window, not substitutes for evaluating the actual cross section.

9. Numerical methods, normalization and uncertainty checks
  • Radial discretization in this app: bound and continuum radial equations use Numerov propagation on a uniform radial grid. The bound-state solver uses node-count bisection and outward/inward matching; the continuum solver uses a five-point derivative at the match radius. These choices are not identical to every numerical detail of the original RADCAP code.
  • Matching: the continuum phase-shift calculation uses the selected matching radius and a numerical logarithmic derivative. Move the radius outward until the nuclear potential is negligible, but watch for numerical instability near zeros of the wavefunction. Compare results at multiple radii.
  • Convergence: use the built-in bound-state and transition-strength convergence tools where available; also vary maximum radius, matching radius and energy-grid density. The continuum radial step and energy scan have user controls. A smooth plot is not by itself a convergence test.
  • Wave normalization: normalize bound states to \(\int_0^\infty |u_f|^2dr=1\) for a single-particle state. Continuum waves use scattering normalization; using the wrong normalization changes capture cross sections.
  • Numerical error versus model uncertainty: coarse/fine differences and mesh convergence probe numerical sensitivity; they do not quantify the full physical uncertainty. Capture observables can depend strongly on bound-state asymptotic behavior, potential geometry, spectroscopic factor \(C^2S\), entrance-channel phase shifts, effective charges, magnetic moments and mass convention. Vary these inputs and report the spread.
  • Scope: this is a single-particle potential-model implementation, not a full many-body calculation. Core excitation, coupled channels, nonlocality, antisymmetrization beyond the adopted cluster model, and electromagnetic retardation are not automatically included.

Where this application departs from the printed equations
  • Eq. (24): as I read the PDF, its prefactor has an extra 1/ĵ. Explicit magnetic-substate sums give the same expression without it (the amplitude differs by exactly √(2j+1)). The corrected form is used. This may be a typographical or transcription issue.
  • Eq. (25), M1: the printed form failed the same test for every term. M1 is computed from standard 6j reductions of l, s and the core spin, and verified against explicit sums.
  • Eq. (31) and Eq. (29): Eq. (31) lacks the detailed-balance factor (kγ/k)²; Eq. (29) carries a factor m/ħ²k that belongs to dB/dk, not dB/dE. The standard forms are used and checked by the Thomas–Reiche–Kuhn and E2 sum rules.
Known disagreements with the paper (see tab 10 for current values)
  • The paper does not state which nucleon mass it uses. The default here is 938.919 MeV/c² per nucleon (editable in tab 1). With A × 931.494 the bound-state energies change by about 0.1 MeV and the resonance moves toward the paper's value; no single value reproduces everything.
  • At the time of writing: the 1⁺ resonance comes out near 548 or 570 keV instead of 631 keV; B(E2) for the Appendix C example is 5.4 e² fm⁴ instead of 3.76; S17 is 9–12 % below the values read by eye from the paper's Fig. 4. The causes are not established.
Conventions, units and approximations
  • Energies in MeV, lengths in fm, e² = 1.43996 MeV fm, ħc = 197.327 MeV fm. Cross sections in fm² internally (1 fm² = 10 mb); S-factors in eV b (charged) or ⟨σv⟩ in cm³ s⁻¹ (neutral); rates also as NA⟨σv⟩ in cm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹.
  • Masses in effective charges and eM are taken proportional to mass numbers. The electromagnetic operators are the long-wavelength limits; photon recoil is neglected (tab 10 checks the long-wavelength parameter).
  • Spectroscopic factors, magnetic moments, resonance parameters and continuum potentials are always your input. Nothing is assumed silently.
  • Single-particle model: one valence particle b outside an inert core a with spin Ia. The channel spin enters only through angular-momentum algebra and, where you choose, through channel-dependent potentials.
Running the file
  • No server, compiler or Python is needed. Open the HTML file in a current browser. All calculations run locally in the page.
  • The standard file loads Plotly and MathJax from public CDNs, so it needs an internet connection the first time it is opened. An offline build with both libraries embedded is a separate, larger file.
  • Large meshes (20000 points) and fine energy grids take longer; the status field in the top bar shows progress.
