Determining the optical potential from elastic-scattering data

Elastic angular distributions are the most precise data in nuclear reactions, but they constrain the complex nuclear potential $U(r)$ only indirectly, through the S-matrix. This workbench takes you from experimental data to a potential, and shows how far the data determine it. Everything runs live in your browser on EXFOR data.

The direct problem: from U(r) to σ(θ)

For two spin-zero nuclei the elastic channel is described by a one-body Schrödinger equation with a complex, energy-dependent optical potential. The imaginary part removes flux into all non-elastic channels.

Optical potential

$$U(r)=V_C(r)-V\,f(r;R_V,a_V)-iW\,f(r;R_W,a_W)-4iW_D\,\frac{e^{x_D}}{(1+e^{x_D})^{2}}$$
$$f(r;R,a)=\Big[1+e^{(r-R)/a}\Big]^{-1}\;\;(\text{WS})\quad\text{or}\quad f^{2}\;\;(\text{WS}^2),\qquad x_D=\frac{r-R_D}{a_D}$$

Radii: $R_i=r_i\,A_t^{1/3}$ for α projectiles and $R_i=r_i\,(A_p^{1/3}+A_t^{1/3})$ for heavy ions. $V_C$ is the potential of a uniformly charged sphere of radius $R_C$.

Radial equation

$$\left[\frac{d^2}{dr^2}+k^2-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}-\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}U(r)\right]u_\ell(r)=0$$
$$u_\ell(r)\xrightarrow{r>R_{\max}}\tfrac{i}{2}\big[H^-_\ell(\eta,kr)-S_\ell\,H^+_\ell(\eta,kr)\big],\quad H^\pm_\ell=G_\ell\pm iF_\ell$$

Integrated with Numerov's method and matched at two radii to Coulomb functions (Steed's continued fractions). Relativistic kinematics: $k=p_{cm}/\hbar c$, $\mu=E_1E_2/(E_1+E_2)$, Sommerfeld parameter $\eta=Z_pZ_te^2\mu/\hbar^2k$.

S-matrix and amplitude

$$S_\ell=\eta_\ell\,e^{2i\delta_\ell},\qquad 0\le\eta_\ell\le1$$
$$f(\theta)=f_C(\theta)+\frac{1}{2ik}\sum_\ell(2\ell+1)\,e^{2i\sigma_\ell}(S_\ell-1)P_\ell(\cos\theta)$$
$$f_C(\theta)=-\frac{\eta}{2k\sin^2\!\frac\theta2}\exp\!\Big[-i\eta\ln\sin^2\!\tfrac\theta2+2i\sigma_0\Big],\quad \sigma_\ell=\arg\Gamma(\ell+1+i\eta)$$

Observables

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta)|^2,\qquad \frac{\sigma}{\sigma_R}=\frac{|f(\theta)|^2}{|f_C(\theta)|^2},\qquad \sigma_{R}^{\text{reac}}=\frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_\ell(2\ell+1)\big(1-|S_\ell|^2\big)$$

For identical bosons (¹²C+¹²C) the amplitude is symmetrised, $d\sigma/d\Omega=|f(\theta)+f(\pi-\theta)|^2$. Only even $\ell$ contribute, and data are often given relative to the Mott cross section $|f_C(\theta)+f_C(\pi-\theta)|^2$.

Key takeaways
  • The data depend on $U(r)$ only through the set $\{S_\ell\}$. Any two potentials with the same $S_\ell$ are experimentally indistinguishable.
  • Strong absorption ($|S_\ell|\approx0$ for $\ell<\ell_g$) hides the nuclear interior. Only the tail near the strong-absorption radius is probed.
  • Weak absorption (light ions at high energy, α particles) lets refracted waves through the interior. This produces nuclear rainbows and Airy minima, which constrain the depth.

The inverse problem: from σ(θ) to U(r)

Find $U(r)$ given $\sigma(\theta_i)\pm\Delta\sigma_i$ at one or more energies (plus $\sigma_R$ and polarisation if measured). The problem is ill-posed: the angular range is finite, there are errors, and $U$ is complex and energy-dependent. All methods add constraints of some kind.

a · Phenomenological χ² fit

$$\chi^2/N=\frac1N\sum_i\left(\frac{\sigma_{th}(\theta_i;\mathbf p)-\sigma_i}{\Delta\sigma_i}\right)^2$$

A fixed form (WS, WS²) with about 6 parameters, minimised by Levenberg–Marquardt. Used on this page.

  • Robust, fast, gives errors on the parameters
  • Answer is biased by the chosen form; ambiguities

b · Model-independent forms

$$V(r)=V_0 f(r)+\sum_{n}b_n\, j_0\!\left(\frac{n\pi r}{R_c}\right)\Theta(R_c-r)$$

Fourier–Bessel series or sums of Gaussians. The error band of $V(r)$ shows which radii the data actually determine.

  • Gives realistic radial uncertainty bands
  • Needs regularisation; many parameters

c · Fixed-energy inversion

$$\{S_\ell\}_{E}\;\longrightarrow\;U(r)\quad\text{(Newton–Sabatier, Lipperheide–Fiedeldey)}$$

First a phase-shift analysis gives $S_\ell$. Then a rational S-matrix representation is inverted analytically (Lipperheide–Fiedeldey), or the Gel'fand–Levitan-type Newton–Sabatier equations are solved.

Two-step version implemented on Potential from one energy (with IP for the S → U step).

  • Direct construction, no form assumed
  • Needs an accurate S-matrix and an interpolation in ℓ

d · Fixed-ℓ inversion

$$K(r,t)+F(r,t)+\int_0^r K(r,s)F(s,t)\,ds=0$$

The Gel'fand–Levitan / Marchenko integral equations reconstruct $V(r)$ from $\delta_\ell(E)$ at all energies (plus bound states). Mostly used for nucleon–nucleon and light systems.

  • Exact and unique (for a real potential)
  • Energy-dependent complex $U$ breaks the assumptions

e · Semiclassical & IP inversion

$$U^{(n+1)}=U^{(n)}+\sum_j c_j\,\phi_j(r),\quad \frac{\partial S_\ell}{\partial c_j}\ \text{from}\ \int u_\ell^2\phi_j\,dr$$

The iterative-perturbative (IP) method linearises $S_\ell$ around a reference potential using basis corrections $\phi_j$. WKB/Abel-type inversion uses the deflection function $\Theta(\ell)$.

  • Works on data directly; handles complex $U$
  • Depends on the starting point

Ambiguities

$$\text{Igo:}\;\;V\,e^{R_V/a_V}\approx\text{const}\qquad\qquad J_V=\frac{4\pi}{A_pA_t}\!\int V(r)r^2dr$$

Continuous: with strong absorption only the tail $V(r)\approx Ve^{(R-r)/a}$ matters. Discrete: families with $\Delta J_V\approx$ one extra node of $u_\ell$ fit equally well. Rainbow data at high energy remove the discrete ambiguity.

Key takeaways
  • Expect the answer to be a family of potentials. Quote what is well determined: $J_V$, the rms radius, and $V(r)$ at the sensitive radius.
  • Use a notch test (Families & sensitivity) to find which radial region the data determine.
  • Combine several energies, $\sigma_R$, and refractive (rainbow) data to reduce the ambiguities.

How retrieval works

  1. Find entries. For α+¹²C, ¹²C+¹²C and ¹²C+²⁴Mg the complete entry lists are built in; they come from a scan of exfor_master. For other reactions, Find entries reads the file names of the exfortables_py tables. Those tables cover absolute cross sections only, so add ratio-data entries by hand if you know them.
  2. Read the master files from IAEA-NDS/exfor_master. The reader parses BIB, COMMON (subentry 001 applies to the whole entry) and DATA, including pointers, MeV/nucleon energies and ± errors. It keeps the reactions target(projectile,EL)target,,DA, plus ,,DA,,RTH and ,,DA,,MOT.
  3. Masses come from AME2020 (Java-NDS). They are used for lab → c.m. conversion and kinematics.
  4. Use in Workbench. Angles are converted to c.m. Cross sections given in the lab frame are converted with the Jacobian. A new reaction gets its own system with a generic starting potential.

Needs internet access. GitHub allows 60 Find entries requests per hour without login; reading master files has no such limit. Errors given as percent are converted to absolute values. Known EXFOR inconsistencies are corrected and flagged, as in the Python tool.

How the ranking works

For each model with k free parameters fitted to N points, with best χ²:

$$\mathrm{AIC_c}=\chi^2+2k+\frac{2k(k+1)}{N-k-1},\qquad \mathrm{BIC}=\chi^2+k\ln N,\qquad \mathrm{QAIC_c}=\frac{\chi^2}{\hat c}+2k+\frac{2k(k+1)}{N-k-1}$$

ĉ = smallest χ²/(N − k) among the candidates. When χ²/N ≫ 1 (model error larger than the quoted data errors, typical for optical-model fits) plain AIC and BIC always pick the largest model; QAICc first rescales χ² by ĉ, so only improvements beyond the misfit level count. Akaike weights wi ∝ exp(−Δi/2) give the relative support; models with Δ < 2 are about equally good. Rows marked at bound have a parameter stuck at its limit and are not recommended.

Choosing the form does not remove the depth (family) ambiguity: a good form can fit equally well with different JV. The table lists JV for each model; the Families and Family probabilities pages resolve that question.

What the S-matrix shows

These plots are what the inverse problem works with. $|S_\ell|$ shows where the absorption acts, and $\mathrm{Re}\,\delta_\ell$ shows the refraction. The Argand diagram traces $S_\ell$ in the complex plane as ℓ increases.

Key takeaways
  • A fixed-energy inversion (Lipperheide–Fiedeldey, Newton–Sabatier) starts from exactly this $\{S_\ell\}$.
  • The nuclear phase shift keeps growing below $\ell_g$ only when absorption is weak. That is the refractive regime, where interior information survives.

χ² landscape: which parameter combinations fit equally well?

This maps $\log_{10}(\chi^2/N)$ on a grid of two parameters, with all others held at their current values. A long curved valley in $(V, r_V)$ is the Igo ambiguity; the dashed line is $V e^{R_V/a_V}=$ const through the current point. Click a cell to load those parameters.

Key takeaways
  • A narrow minimum across the valley but a flat one along it means the data determine one combination (the tail) and not the depth.
  • For refractive data the valley splits into separate minima, which are the discrete families shown on Families & sensitivity.

Potential families and radial sensitivity

Discrete families: fix $V$ on a grid and refit all other parameters, continuing from one depth to the next. Notch test: cut the potential by 10% in a narrow Gaussian band at radius $r_n$ and record $\Delta\chi^2(r_n)$. The data are sensitive where $\Delta\chi^2$ is large.

Key takeaways
  • Family members cross near the same radius, the "sensitive radius". $V$ there is the best-determined number.
  • $\chi^2(J_V)$ often has several minima of similar depth. Data at another energy, or over a wider angular range, usually picks one.

Potential from one energy: data → Sℓ → U(r)

Here the potential is calculated from the data rather than assumed. Step 1 fits the S-matrix directly to σ(θ), with no potential involved. Step 2 rebuilds a local potential from that S-matrix, then refines it against the data. The error band shows which radii the data actually determine.

Step 1 · Phase-shift analysis (model-independent)

$$S_\ell=\eta_\ell\,e^{2i\delta_\ell},\qquad \eta_\ell=\frac{1}{1+e^{-a(\ell)}},\qquad \delta_\ell=d(\ell)$$
$$a(\ell)=\frac{\ell-\ell_g}{\Delta}+T(\ell)\,s_a(\ell),\qquad d(\ell)=\frac{\delta_0}{1+e^{(\ell-\ell_r)/\Delta_r}}+T(\ell)\,s_d(\ell)$$

First a smooth McIntyre S-matrix (5 parameters) is fitted, then free cubic-spline corrections $s_a, s_d$ on $K$ knots are added. The taper $T(\ell)\to0$ above grazing forces $|S_\ell|\to1$ and $\delta_\ell\to0$, as for any short-range potential. The penalty $\lambda_1\sum(\Delta^2 s)^2$ smooths the result in ℓ, because there are more Sℓ than the data can determine.

Step 2 · Iterative-perturbative (IP) inversion

$$U(r)=U_{\rm ref}(r)+\sum_{j}(\alpha_j+i\beta_j)\,e^{-(r-r_j)^2/w^2}$$
$$\frac{\partial S_\ell}{\partial\alpha_j}=-\frac{2i}{k}\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty\psi_\ell(r)^2\,\phi_j(r)\,dr,\qquad \psi_\ell\to\tfrac{i}{2}\big(H^-_\ell-S_\ell H^+_\ell\big)$$

2a: linearise Sℓ in the coefficients, solve the weighted, regularised least-squares problem $\min\sum_\ell|S^{\rm target}_\ell-S_\ell|^2/\sigma_\ell^2+\lambda_2\|c\|^2$, and iterate (Cooper & Mackintosh). 2b: refine the same coefficients directly against σ(θ), keeping Im U ≤ 0. The ±1σ band comes from the covariance of $c$.

Lipperheide–Fiedeldey

$$S(\lambda)=S_{\rm ref}(\lambda)\prod_{n}\frac{\lambda^2-\beta_n^2}{\lambda^2-\alpha_n^2}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;U(r)\ \text{in closed form}$$

Represent S as a rational function of λ = ℓ+½. The corresponding potential then follows analytically from a Gel'fand–Levitan-type kernel. Elegant, but sensitive to how the poles are placed and how S is extrapolated to complex λ. Not implemented here. IP needs only the forward solver and handles complex U directly.

Newton–Sabatier

$$K(r,r')=-\sum_\ell c_\ell\,u_\ell(r)\,\hat j_\ell(r'),\qquad V(r)=-\frac{2}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\frac{K(r,r)}{r}$$

Exact fixed-energy inversion for a real potential, built from the phase shifts through a linear system for the coefficients $c_\ell$. With absorption and only about 50 noisy Sℓ, that system is badly conditioned. Not implemented here, for that reason.

Key takeaways
  • The S-matrix fit (step 1) is the most faithful summary of the data. A χ²/N far below the Woods-Saxon value shows how much the assumed shape was costing.
  • If no local potential reproduces the step 1 S-matrix (χ² after step 2a ≫ step 1), the data need physics beyond a local, ℓ-independent U. One example is the α+¹²C backward rise, which calls for parity dependence or coupled channels.
  • The ±1σ band is computed from the data alone; the smoothing penalties are left out so they cannot make it look narrower. The potential is determined where the band is narrow, usually from about 1 fm inside the strong-absorption radius outward. Quote U(r) there, together with JV.
  • Step 2a weighs each Sℓ by its own uncertainty and ignores the correlations between them, so a small S-misfit can still give a poor χ². Step 2b, the fit against the data, decides the final potential.
  • Near-barrier data (¹²C+²⁴Mg at 16–24 MeV) leave the band wide almost everywhere: they fix only the potential tail. Use several energies or higher-energy data to go further in.

Potential from several energies (mixed-case inversion)

This page inverts several energies at once. The basis coefficients vary with energy, and the whole inversion is repeated from several quite different starting potentials. A single energy usually allows several potential families. Requiring one potential that changes smoothly with energy to fit all energies is the strongest test of which family the data support. Comparing different starting potentials shows which parts of U(r) come from the data and which from the starting guess.

Model

$$U(r,E)=U_{\rm ref}(r)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big[\alpha_j+\alpha'_j\,\varepsilon+i\big(\beta_j+\beta'_j\,\varepsilon\big)\Big]e^{-(r-r_j)^2/w^2},\qquad \varepsilon=E-E_0\ \ \text{or}\ \ \ln(E/E_0)$$
$$\chi^2=\sum_{E}\sum_i\left(\frac{\sigma_{th}(\theta_i,E)-\sigma_i}{\Delta\sigma_i}\right)^2+\lambda\,\|c\|^2+\lambda_s\|\Delta^2 c\|^2+\textstyle\sum_{r,E}\big[\max(0,\mathrm{Im}\,U)\big]^2$$

This is the "mixed case" of Cooper & Mackintosh, fitted directly to the data. The 4n coefficients are found by Levenberg–Marquardt with an analytic Jacobian: $\partial\sigma/\partial c_j=2\,\mathrm{Re}\big[F^*\sum_\ell A_\ell\,g_\ell(\theta)\,\partial S_\ell/\partial c_j\big]$, with $\partial S_\ell/\partial c_j$ from the IP response integral. That is one Numerov solve per energy per iteration. The smoothing strength λ is lowered in three stages (100λ → 10λ → λ).

Reference-independence test

The same inversion starts from up to four reference potentials that differ in depth or shape. Each run is accepted if its χ²/N is within 25% of the best run. Then:

  • where the accepted runs agree (spread smaller than about 2σ, or 10% of |U|), the potential is determined by the data;
  • where they differ, the data leave a family ambiguity that is still open;
  • a run that ends far above the best χ² has fallen into another family that the data reject.

Tested on synthetic α+¹²C data at four energies, generated from a potential whose depth changes with energy. The true J_V(E) was recovered within 2% from the standard and deep starts, and the shallow start was rejected (χ²/N 20 against 1.8). With a single energy, all three starts were accepted with J_V spread from 413 to 498: that is the ambiguity that multi-energy fitting removes.

Add fusion data: pin down the barrier

Elastic scattering near the Coulomb barrier fixes only the far tail of the potential. Fusion measures how much flux tunnels through the barrier, so it is sensitive to exactly the region the elastic data leave open: the barrier height, its radius and its curvature. Here both are calculated from the same optical potential, and the two data sets are fitted together.

Fusion from the optical potential

$$\sigma_F=\frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_\ell(2\ell+1)\,T^F_\ell,\qquad T^F_\ell=\frac{4}{k}\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\int_0^{R_B}W(r)\,|u_\ell(r)|^2\,dr$$

This is the flux absorbed inside the s-wave barrier radius $R_B$, with $u_\ell\to\tfrac i2(H^-_\ell-S_\ell H^+_\ell)$. Taking $R_B\to\infty$ recovers $\sigma_R$ exactly (checked: 342.509 against 342.505 mb). A short-range absorber $W_F\,[1+e^{(r-R_F)/a_F}]^{-1}$ inside the barrier plays the role of the ingoing-wave boundary condition, so σF becomes the transmission through the real barrier $V(r)=\mathrm{Re}\,U_N+V_C$.

Barrier, Wong formula and S-factor

$$\sigma_F^{\rm Wong}=\frac{\hbar\omega\,R_B^2}{2E}\ln\!\Big[1+e^{2\pi(E-V_B)/\hbar\omega}\Big],\qquad S(E)=E\,\sigma_F\,e^{2\pi\eta}$$

$V_B, R_B$ are the top of the real s-wave barrier, and $\hbar\omega=\hbar\sqrt{-V''(R_B)/\mu}$. The Wong curve is shown for reference only; the fit uses the full calculation. A maximum of the S-factor at low energy is the signature of fusion hindrance, which a standard Woods–Saxon potential cannot reproduce.

How probable is each family? Emulator and Bayesian sampling

An emulator replaces the Numerov solution by a small variational problem built from a few exact training solutions. Here it is used to sample the posterior of the potential depths with MCMC, and to turn the "which family?" question into probabilities.

Kohn-variational (eigenvector-continuation) emulator

$$U(r;\boldsymbol\theta)=\sum_a\theta_a U_a(r),\qquad \tilde\psi_\ell=\psi^{(0)}_\ell+\sum_{k=1}^{n_r}a_k\chi_k,\qquad \chi_k\in\mathrm{POD}\{\psi^{(i)}_\ell-\psi^{(0)}_\ell\}$$
$$S_\ell[\tilde\psi]=\tilde S-\frac{2i}{k}\int_0^\infty\tilde\psi\,(H_{\boldsymbol\theta}-E)\,\tilde\psi\,dr,\qquad \partial S_\ell/\partial a_k=0\;\Rightarrow\;\text{an } n_r\times n_r \text{ linear system}$$

Training solves the full problem at $n_s$ points (Latin hypercube in the prior box). All matrices are affine in θ, so they are precomputed once for each ℓ. Peripheral partial waves (|S−1| < 10⁻³) use the exact first-order response. Two emulators with different reference solutions are compared at every evaluation. Where they disagree by more than 0.2 data-σ (a Kohn anomaly), the exact solver is used instead.

Honest speed-up

Measured on a test machine (your own values appear in the tiles below): the full Numerov solution takes 0.7–7 ms per energy. The emulator takes 0.1–0.7 ms, a speed-up of about 2–15×, not the 10³ often quoted. Those figures are measured against much slower high-fidelity codes. The gain grows with the number of radial points and partial waves, so it is largest for ¹²C+¹²C at high energy. The table below is measured live on your machine.

$$p(\boldsymbol\theta\,|\,\text{data})\propto \exp\!\Big[-\frac{\chi^2(\boldsymbol\theta)}{2B}\Big]\,\Theta(\boldsymbol\theta\in\text{box}),\qquad B=\max\!\Big(1,\frac{\chi^2_{\min}}{N-n_\theta}\Big)$$

B (Birge factor) inflates the errors when the model cannot reach χ²/N ≈ 1. Parallel tempering (4 chains, T = 1, 2, 4, 8, with swaps) lets the sampler jump between families. A family's probability is the fraction of T = 1 samples in its JV mode.

Delayed acceptance (Christen & Fox 2005): a proposal for the T = 1 chain is first screened with the emulator. Only if it passes is the exact χ² computed, and a second test corrects for the emulator error. The T = 1 chain therefore samples the exact posterior whatever the emulator's accuracy; the emulator only saves the exact solves for proposals it already rejects.

Key takeaways
  • Emulators are accurate to a few hundredths of a data error bar here. They are only a few times faster than an efficient Numerov code, but that is enough to make full posterior sampling run in seconds.
  • The family question becomes a probability: the posterior of JV splits into modes, and their weights quantify how strongly the data prefer each family.
  • The probabilities hold only inside the prior box and for the chosen parametrisation. Widen the box or switch to 5 parameters to test how robust they are.

Data sets: what is fitted
