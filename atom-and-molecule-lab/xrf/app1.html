Method notes

Peak matching compares each detected peak to the nearest tabulated Kα1 / Kβ1 / Lα1 line within the tolerance window; it does not resolve overlapping lines from different elements. Reference energies are experimental values from NIST SRD-128 (Deslattes et al., Rev. Mod. Phys. 75, 35 (2003)).

FWHM finder — each peak's width is measured directly from its half-max crossing points rather than assumed; the integration window sizes itself to the peak actually observed. If a clean crossing isn't found (peak at the edge of the loaded range, or an unresolved neighbor), it falls back to a generic detector-resolution curve — see the meas / calc tag in the peak table's FWHM column.

Escape peaks — an unidentified peak sitting ~1.740 keV below a confirmed K line (the Si Kα energy) is flagged as a likely Si detector escape peak of that element, not a separate species, and is excluded from composition.

Kβ cross-check — every Kα1-identified element is checked for its expected Kβ1 line at the tabulated energy and roughly the expected intensity ratio; mismatches are flagged as a possible overlap or misidentification.

Composition is a semi-quantitative estimate from net peak area scaled by an approximate K/L-shell fluorescence yield — there is no fundamental-parameters or self-absorption / matrix correction, so treat fractions as relative order-of-magnitude, not a calibrated assay. Error bars are a √N counting-statistics estimate, not a full uncertainty budget. Fit per-element sensitivity factors from known reference standards in the Calibration tab to replace this estimate with a standard-referenced one.

Zeff follows the electron-fraction exponent model, defaulting to m = 2.94 (the commonly used value for photoelectric-dominated attenuation) — adjustable in the Zeff card if you need to match a different published exponent or energy regime.

Confidence-weighted normalization (optional, off by default) scales each identified element's contribution to the relative composition estimate by how tightly its line matched the reference energy — a peak near the edge of your tolerance window counts for less than one sitting right on the tabulated value. It never affects calib. rows, since those sensitivity factors were fit against the unweighted estimate.

Material match scores the identified composition against a library of known material profiles (the same synthetic demo materials, plus any Calibration-tab standards with a fitted result) by comparing normalized element fractions — a coarse fingerprint match, not a certified material ID. See the card below the composition chart.

01Why atoms emit characteristic X-rays

When an incident photon (from an X-ray tube or radioisotope source) or electron has enough energy to eject an inner-shell electron — typically K or L shell — the atom is left in an excited, ionized state. An electron from a higher shell falls into the vacancy, and the energy difference between the two shells is released either as a photon (fluorescence) or transferred to another electron which is ejected instead (the competing Auger effect).

Because inner-shell binding energies are set by the nuclear charge and are only weakly perturbed by chemical bonding, the emitted photon energy is essentially a fingerprint of the element — this is what makes XRF an elemental identification technique rather than a molecular one.

03From a spectrum to a peak's net area

A raw spectrum is a histogram of detected photon counts per energy (or channel) bin, sitting on top of a smoothly varying background — mostly bremsstrahlung continuum from the excitation source, plus scatter. Before a peak's intensity means anything, that background has to be estimated and subtracted.

This tool estimates a local baseline B(E) using a rolling-minimum filter (the lowest count value in a window around each point), then integrates the background-subtracted signal across a window of ±1.8× the peak's FWHM around its centroid:

The FWHM Γ itself is measured directly from each peak — walking outward from the centroid until the net signal crosses half its net height on both sides, and linearly interpolating the exact crossing energy. This adapts the integration window to the peak actually observed (broader for a detector-resolution-limited peak sitting on more noise, narrower for a clean high-statistics one) rather than assuming every peak matches a generic curve. When a clean crossing isn't found on both sides — the peak sits at the edge of the loaded range, or a neighboring peak's shoulder keeps the signal above half-max before the true edge — the tool falls back to a generic Si-drift-detector resolution model, ω(E) = 45 + 55·√(E/5.9) eV. The peak table's FWHM column marks each value meas or calc accordingly.

The shaded regions in the Analyzer's spectrum plot are exactly this integral, visualized.

04From net area to mass fraction

Net peak area is proportional to the number of characteristic photons a detector recorded — but that number depends on far more than how much of the element is present. It also depends on the fluorescence yield, the incident spectrum's intensity at the absorption edge, self-absorption of both the exciting radiation and the emitted line within the sample, detector efficiency at that energy, and geometry. A rigorous treatment (the Sherman equation, the basis of fundamental-parameters XRF software) accounts for all of these self-consistently, iterating over an assumed composition until predicted and measured intensities match.

This tool implements a deliberately simplified version: it corrects only for fluorescence yield, which is the single largest Z-dependent factor, and assumes everything else is roughly comparable across elements in the sample. For element i with tabulated fluorescence yield ωi and net line area Ai:

the un-normalized weight is then renormalized across all identified elements to give a mass-fraction percentage. Optionally, confidence-weighted normalization (a checkbox above the composition chart, off by default) first scales each element's un-normalized weight by a factor that decays with how far its matched peak sat from the tabulated line, relative to the tolerance window in use — a soft, monotonic penalty floored at 0.3× so a borderline-but-accepted match still contributes, not a rigorous likelihood treatment. It only touches the relative estimate; elements with a fitted Calibration-tab factor are never reweighted this way, since k was fit against the unweighted quantity.

What this ignores: absorption-edge jump ratios, matrix self-absorption (a Fe-rich matrix absorbs a Si Kα photon far more strongly than its own Fe Kα), the shape of the exciting spectrum, and detector efficiency. Expect correct ordering of major vs. trace elements, not calibrated percentages.

Error bars on the composition chart come from Poisson counting statistics: each net peak area's uncertainty is estimated as √N from the raw (gross, background-included) counts summed over the integration window, then carried through ω(Z) or the calibration factor k to a mass-fraction σ. This uses only the dominant, peak-specific term — it does not propagate baseline-fit uncertainty or the covariance introduced by normalizing across elements, so treat the bars as a lower bound on the true uncertainty, not a complete error budget.

07Where the model breaks down

  • Overlapping lines: if two elements' lines fall within the tolerance window of each other (e.g. Ba Lα and Ti Kα both sit near 4.5 keV), the matcher picks the closer one and silently ignores the possibility of an overlap.
  • No matrix correction: composition estimates from this tool should be read as "roughly this element is present, roughly this much more than that one" — not as calibrated assay values.
  • Detector resolution model is generic: the FWHM-vs-energy curve used for peak windowing is a reasonable Si-drift-detector approximation, not your specific instrument's measured resolution.
  • Fluorescence yield formulas are smooth empirical fits, not tabulated per-element values — accurate to maybe 10–20% for most elements, worse near shell-structure irregularities.
  • Reference line energies are experimental values from NIST SRD-128 where a direct or combined measurement exists; a handful of light-element Kα1/Kα2 pairs are NIST-reported only as an unresolved KL2,3 blend and are stored here as a single Kα1 entry.
  • Escape/Kβ heuristics are approximate: the Si-escape flag uses a fixed 1.740 keV shift and the Kβ cross-check uses a single representative Kβ/Kα ratio (~0.13) with a wide tolerance band — both are meant to catch gross misidentifications, not to be quantitatively precise across all Z.
  • Material match is a coarse fingerprint, not a certified ID: it compares normalized element fractions against a small built-in/user library using cosine similarity, with no consideration of trace-element specifications, heat-treatment, or alloy grade boundaries.

For research-grade quantification, the right next step is a fundamental-parameters package (e.g. the Sherman-equation-based tools bundled with most commercial XRF instruments) calibrated against certified reference materials.

01Loading a spectrum

1
Try the demo first.

Pick a sample from the Demo sample dropdown — a built-in database of synthetic material profiles (stainless steel, brass, bronze, gold-plated jewelry, solder, soil, and the original Si/S/Ar/Fe/Cu reference) — then click Load demo spectrum to see the full pipeline run against it. This confirms the tool is working before you bring in real data.

2
Upload your own file.

Click Upload spectrum file. Three formats are accepted automatically: a two-column CSV of energy (keV) and counts; a two-column CSV of channel number and counts (set the format dropdown to "Channel # + counts"); or a raw Amptek-style .mca text export, which is detected automatically by its <<DATA>> block.

3
.mca calibration.

If the file contains a <<CALIBRATION>> block, it's read and used automatically — you'll see "(calibration read from file)" in the spectrum tag. If not, the tool falls back to the gain/offset sliders described below.

02Calibrating raw channel data

Detectors record counts per channel, not per keV directly — a linear calibration converts channel index to energy: E = offset + gain × channel / 1000 (gain in eV/channel, offset in keV). If you know your instrument's calibration (check its acquisition software, e.g. Amptek ADMCA reports this under Calibration settings), set:

  • Gain slider — eV per channel. A typical Si-drift detector runs 10–20 eV/ch; 14 eV/ch is a common default.
  • Offset slider — the energy (keV) corresponding to channel 0. Usually close to zero, occasionally slightly negative.

If your calibration is off, every matched element will show the same systematic energy shift — check the Δ column in the peak table; if every identified peak is off by roughly the same number of eV in the same direction, adjust the offset first, then gain.

03Detection settings

  • Peak sensitivity — the minimum net-signal-to-max ratio a local maximum must clear to count as a peak. Lower it to catch small trace-element peaks; raise it if noise is being flagged as peaks.
  • Line match tolerance — how close (in eV) a detected peak's energy must be to a reference line to count as identified. Widen it for lower-resolution detectors or poorly calibrated data; narrow it to avoid false matches when many elements are present.

After changing either slider, click Run analysis to re-process the currently loaded spectrum.

04Reading the spectrum plot

  • E min / E max — set a custom energy window; leave blank and click Reset view to return to the full range.
  • Magnitude (Y axis) — switch to log scale to see small trace peaks alongside a dominant major-element peak on the same plot.
  • show baseline — overlays the estimated background used for net-area integration.
  • shade net peak areas — fills the region between the baseline and the signal for each detected peak, i.e. exactly what gets integrated into net area.
  • Clicking a row in the peak table zooms the plot to that peak.

05Reading the tables

Peak table: Δ shows how far the detected peak sits from the matched reference line, in eV — small is good. FWHM is the peak's measured width; a meas tag means it was read directly off that peak's half-max crossing points, a calc tag means no clean crossing was found and the generic detector-resolution curve was used instead — hover the tag for why. The confidence badge is just a visual read of how large Δ is relative to your tolerance setting (high/med/low), not a statistical confidence interval. Rows flagged esc are likely Si detector escape peaks of a nearby identified element, not separate species. A small marker next to a Kα1 row shows whether the expected Kβ1 line was also found at a plausible intensity (✓), was missing (?), or showed an anomalous ratio suggesting a possible overlap (⚠).

Composition: mass-fraction percentages sum to 100% across identified elements only — anything unmatched, or genuinely absent, isn't represented. Escape peaks are excluded automatically. The confidence-weighted normalization checkbox (off by default) downweights elements whose line match sat near the edge of your tolerance window before renormalizing; a ×0.xx tag on a row shows it was scaled. It never applies to calib. rows.

Effective atomic number: the exponent m in the Zeff formula defaults to 2.94 and is adjustable in the Zeff card (2.5–4.5) — use ↺ 2.94 to reset. Only change it if you have a specific published value or energy regime in mind; the tool doesn't tell you which value is right for your sample.

Calculation pipeline: shows the actual numbers at each step from net area through to each element's contribution to Zeff, so you can see exactly how a given mass-fraction number was produced rather than trusting it as a black box.

06Calibration mode

1
Set detection settings first.

Calibration fitting reuses the Analyzer tab's current peak sensitivity and line match tolerance, so tune those against your demo/real data before fitting.

2
Add reference standards.

In the Calibration tab, click + Add reference standard for each certified or well-characterized material you have (2–3 is enough to get a usable line). Load its spectrum the same way as in the Analyzer, and enter its known composition as element/mass % rows.

3
Fit.

Click Fit calibration. For each known element that's also detected as a peak in that standard's spectrum, the tool pools (measured weight, known %) pairs across all standards and fits a sensitivity factor k by least squares through the origin. Elements with only one standard still get a factor but no R².

4
Apply.

Check use calibration factors in Analyzer. Any element with a fitted factor now uses it directly for its mass %; the Analyzer's composition and pipeline tables flag those rows calib. — everything else still falls back to the relative yield-normalized estimate.

07Exporting results

Click Export results (.csv) to download the peak table and composition estimate as a single CSV file, suitable for pulling into a lab notebook, spreadsheet, or another one of your analysis tools. The export includes each net area's counting-statistics σ and, when calibration mode is active, which rows used a fitted factor versus the relative estimate.

08Batch mode

1
Set your detection settings first.

Batch mode reuses the Analyzer tab's current peak sensitivity, line match tolerance, data-format selection, and gain/offset — dial those in against one representative file (or a demo) before loading a batch.

2
Load files.

In the Batch tab, drag files onto the dropzone or click it to browse. Any number of energy/channel CSVs or .mca files can be queued; each appears as a row in Loaded Files.

3
Run.

Click Run batch analysis. Each file runs independently through the same pipeline as the Analyzer (baseline → peaks → matching → composition → Zeff → material match), and a summary row appears per file.

4
Inspect or export.

Click any result row to load that file into the Analyzer tab for the full peak table, plot, and pipeline view. Or click Export batch summary (.csv) for a single file listing every sample's top elements, mass %, Zeff, and closest material match.

09Material match

After running analysis (Analyzer or Batch), the Material Match card scores the identified composition against a small library of known profiles: the built-in demo materials (stainless steel, brass, bronze, gold-plated jewelry, solder, soil) plus any Calibration-tab reference standard that has a known composition entered. The score is a cosine similarity between normalized element-fraction vectors — 100% means the identified elements and their relative proportions line up closely with that profile; lower scores mean partial overlap or different proportions.

This is a coarse fingerprint match intended to suggest "this looks like it's in the ballpark of X," not to certify an alloy grade — trace-element specs and heat-treatment aren't represented in the underlying profiles. See Theory §7 for its limitations.

10Troubleshooting

  • No peaks found — lower the peak sensitivity slider, or check that your energy axis isn't wildly out of the detector's real range (e.g. channel data loaded without calibration).
  • Everything unidentified — widen the line match tolerance, or check for a systematic calibration offset (see §2).
  • Composition looks physically implausible — remember the fluorescence-yield-only model in the Theory tab; large Z gaps between major elements are where it's weakest.
  • File won't parse — the tool expects plain numeric columns separated by commas, semicolons, or tabs; strip extra metadata rows other than a single header line. The same parser is used in Batch mode and the Calibration tab.
  • A real trace element reads as "esc" — the escape-peak heuristic is a fixed-offset check, not a fit; if you believe a flagged peak is real, widen the line match tolerance slightly or check whether it also matches a tabulated line for another element near that energy.
  • A calibration factor looks off — check that the standard's known % was entered correctly and that the element's peak was actually detected in that standard's spectrum (add it as a standard, load its spectrum, and look for it in the Analyzer's peak table first if unsure).
  • Batch results look empty for a file — that file likely produced zero identified peaks under the current settings; open it via the batch results row to inspect it directly in the Analyzer.
  • Material match shows low scores everywhere — expected for anything outside the built-in library (e.g. an unusual alloy); add it as a Calibration-tab standard with its known composition to include it as a match target.
