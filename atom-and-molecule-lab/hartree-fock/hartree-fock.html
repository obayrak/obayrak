Why Hartree–Fock exists

The many-electron problem is exponentially hard

A single electron around a nucleus is exactly solvable — that's the hydrogen atom. The moment you add a second electron, the two particles start talking to each other through Coulomb repulsion, and the equation stops separating into simple pieces. Add more electrons, and the wavefunction's complexity explodes. Hartree–Fock is the first serious attempt to tame this explosion.

What each term means

Kinetic energy operator

\[-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tfrac12\nabla_i^2\]

Each electron carries its own kinetic energy operator (atomic units, so \(\hbar=m_e=1\)). This term is a sum of independent one-electron operators — it never couples different electrons to each other. If this were the only term, the electrons wouldn't interact at all.

Nuclear attraction operator

\[-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_A \frac{Z_A}{r_{iA}}\]

Every electron \(i\) is pulled toward every nucleus \(A\) with charge \(Z_A\), with the Coulomb attraction weakening as \(1/r_{iA}\). Like the kinetic term, this is a sum of one-electron operators — each electron only "sees" the fixed nuclei, not the other electrons directly.

Notice that only the electron–electron repulsion term links different particles together. Kinetic energy and nuclear attraction are separable, one-electron terms — if repulsion vanished, the exact many-electron problem would fall apart into N independent hydrogen-like problems.

Why it's hard — Because of \(1/r_{ij}\), \(\Psi\) cannot be factored into a simple product \(\psi_1(x_1)\psi_2(x_2)\cdots\). The exact wavefunction is an irreducibly correlated object living in a \(3N\)-dimensional configuration space.
The core approximation

Independent-particle (mean-field) approximation

Instead of tracking the instantaneous, correlated dance of every electron with every other, Hartree–Fock lets each electron move in the time-averaged field created by all the others. The many-body problem becomes N one-body problems, each in an effective potential.

What each electron "sees"

In the exact picture, electron 2's position jitters unpredictably relative to electron 1 — the potential electron 1 feels from electron 2 spikes and dips instant to instant (correlation). In the Hartree–Fock picture, electron 2 is replaced by its charge density \(|\phi_2(\mathbf r)|^2\) smeared over space, and electron 1 feels the smooth average field of that cloud.

\[V_{\text{mean}}^{(i)}(\mathbf r) \approx \sum_{j\neq i}\int \frac{|\phi_j(\mathbf r')|^2}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,d^3r'\]
Trade-off — This is exact in the limit of a fully smeared-out density, but it throws away instantaneous correlation — the tendency of electrons to actively dodge each other beyond what's captured by average repulsion and antisymmetry. That missing piece is called correlation energy.
Antisymmetry & the Pauli principle

The Slater determinant

Electrons are fermions: the total wavefunction must flip sign whenever you swap any two electrons. A simple orbital product doesn't do this — the Slater determinant does, automatically, and it's the actual trial wavefunction Hartree–Fock optimizes.

The determinant form

\[ \Psi(x_1,\dots,x_N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}} \begin{vmatrix} \phi_1(x_1) & \phi_2(x_1) & \cdots & \phi_N(x_1) \\ \phi_1(x_2) & \phi_2(x_2) & \cdots & \phi_N(x_2) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \phi_1(x_N) & \phi_2(x_N) & \cdots & \phi_N(x_N) \end{vmatrix} \]

Rows are electrons (coordinates \(x_i\)); columns are spin-orbitals \(\phi_k\). Swapping two rows (two electrons) swaps two rows of a determinant — which flips its sign. That's the Pauli exclusion principle for free: if two electrons occupy the same spin-orbital, two columns become identical and the determinant is exactly zero.

Physical meaning — No two electrons can share all the same quantum numbers, and electrons of the same spin naturally avoid each other in space — this spatial avoidance is called the exchange hole.

Putting it together

The Hartree–Fock equations & the Fock operator

Minimizing the energy of a Slater determinant with respect to the orbitals yields a set of effective one-electron eigenvalue equations — the Hartree–Fock equations — built from the Fock operator.

The Fock operator

\[\hat F(1) = \hat h(1) + \sum_{j=1}^{N/2}\Big[\,2\hat J_j(1) - \hat K_j(1)\,\Big]\]

\(\hat h\) — core Hamiltonian (kinetic + nuclear attraction, one electron).
\(\hat J_j\) — Coulomb operator: average electrostatic repulsion from the charge cloud of orbital \(\phi_j\).
\(\hat K_j\) — Exchange operator: a purely quantum, non-classical term from antisymmetry, only acting between same-spin electrons — with no classical analogue.

\[\hat J_j(1)\phi_i(1)=\left[\int \phi_j^*(2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}\phi_j(2)\,d\mathbf r_2\right]\phi_i(1)\] \[\hat K_j(1)\phi_i(1)=\left[\int \phi_j^*(2)\frac{1}{r_{12}}\phi_i(2)\,d\mathbf r_2\right]\phi_j(1)\]

The Hartree–Fock equation is the eigenvalue problem \(\hat F\phi_i=\varepsilon_i\phi_i\) — but since \(\hat F\) is built from the orbitals it's trying to solve for, it must be solved self-consistently (see the SCF cycle in later tabs).

Encoding the electron cloud

The one-particle density matrix

Every observable Hartree–Fock cares about — the energy, the Fock matrix, the electron count — can be built from a single object: the density matrix P. Edit the molecular-orbital coefficients below and watch P update live.

Definition

\[ P_{\mu\nu} = 2\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{occ}}} C_{\mu i}\,C_{\nu i} \]

\(\mu,\nu\) label basis functions (atomic-orbital-like), \(i\) runs over the occupied molecular orbitals only, and the factor of 2 counts both spin electrons in each doubly-occupied MO. \(C_{\mu i}\) is the coefficient of basis function \(\mu\) in occupied orbital \(i\) — exactly the eigenvectors the SCF cycle in Tab 6 solves for.

Diagonal \(P_{\mu\mu}\) is the electron population sitting on basis function \(\mu\). Off-diagonal \(P_{\mu\nu}\) measures how coherently electrons are shared between \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) — loosely, a bond-order-like term.

Choosing the building blocks

Basis functions: from Slater cusps to Gaussian speed

Every orbital \(\phi_i\) in a Slater determinant is itself built from a fixed set of simple functions — the basis set. Click a concept to learn what it means, then play with a real Gaussian primitive on the right to see exactly what an "exponent" controls.

Basis-set concepts

Gaussian basis Slater basis Minimal basis Double-zeta Triple-zeta Polarization Diffuse functions

Click any term above to see what it means and how it shows up in the picture above.

Gaussian basis (GTO) — functions of the form \(g(r)=N e^{-\alpha r^2}\). Smooth at the nucleus (zero slope, no cusp), which is not quite physical — but the product of two Gaussians on different centers is another Gaussian (the Gaussian Product Theorem). That single fact turns brutally hard multi-center integrals into fast closed-form ones, which is the entire reason Gaussians dominate quantum chemistry codes.

Slater basis (STO) — functions of the form \(\chi(r)=N e^{-\zeta r}\). These reproduce the true hydrogenic cusp at the nucleus and the correct exponential decay at large \(r\) — physically more faithful per function than a Gaussian. Their downside: multi-center integrals over STOs have no simple analytic form, making them impractical for polyatomic molecules. In practice, several Gaussians are fit together (contracted) to mimic one STO's shape, borrowing STO accuracy with GTO speed.

Minimal basis — exactly one basis function per atomic orbital that is occupied (or partially occupied) in the free atom: 1 function for H/He (1s), 5 for Li–Ne (1s, 2s, 2px, 2py, 2pz). Cheapest option, but every orbital is frozen at its free-atom size and shape — it can't stretch, shrink, or polarize when the atom forms bonds.

Double-zeta (DZ) — two basis functions (a "tight" and a "loose" one, i.e. two different exponents \(\zeta\)) per atomic orbital instead of one. The SCF procedure then chooses how much of each to mix in, letting every orbital shrink or expand to fit its actual molecular environment. This is usually the single biggest accuracy jump for the smallest extra cost.

Triple-zeta (TZ) — three basis functions per atomic orbital. More radial flexibility again, at proportionally higher computational cost (Fock-build cost grows roughly as the 4th power of basis size) — used when quantitative accuracy in energies or geometries actually matters.

Polarization functions — basis functions with higher angular momentum than anything occupied in the free atom: p-functions on hydrogen, d-functions on carbon/nitrogen/oxygen, and so on. A lone s-function can only ever be spherical; adding a p-function lets the electron density distort — "polarize" — away from spherical symmetry, which is exactly what happens when a real bond forms. Without polarization functions, bond angles and bond energies are systematically wrong.

Diffuse functions — extra basis functions with a very small exponent \(\alpha\), so they are spatially very wide (see the exponent slider on the right — small \(\alpha\) means a broad, low function). They're essential wherever electron density reaches far from the nuclei: anions, excited states, weakly-bound complexes, and Rydberg states. Leaving them out systematically underestimates how "loose" such electrons really are.

STO-3G — Minimal basis. Each Slater-type orbital is approximated by a fixed contraction of exactly 3 primitive Gaussians (an "STO-NG" scheme), giving one function per occupied atomic orbital with a single zeta and no polarization.

\[\chi^{STO\text{-}3G}_\mu(r) = \sum_{p=1}^{3} d_{p\mu}\,e^{-\alpha_{p\mu} r^2}\;\approx\; N e^{-\zeta_\mu r}\]

Cheap and qualitative only — the same rigid, one-size orbital limitation as any minimal basis (see Tab 8's "Minimal basis" concept).

3-21G — Split-valence. Core orbitals stay a single contraction of 3 primitives, but each valence orbital is split into an "inner" contraction of 2 primitives and an "outer" single uncontracted primitive, so valence orbitals can expand or contract to fit their bonding environment (the double-zeta idea from Tab 8, applied only to valence).

\[\chi_{core}=\sum_{p=1}^{3} d_p g_p,\qquad \chi_{val}^{inner}=\sum_{p=1}^{2} d_p g_p,\qquad \chi_{val}^{outer}=g_1\]

6-31G — The most widely used general-purpose split-valence basis. Core is a contraction of 6 primitives (a more accurate core than 3-21G's 3), and the valence shell is still split 3+1: an inner contraction of 3 primitives plus one outer uncontracted primitive.

\[\chi_{core}=\sum_{p=1}^{6} d_p g_p,\qquad \chi_{val}^{inner}=\sum_{p=1}^{3} d_p g_p,\qquad \chi_{val}^{outer}=g_1\]

6-31G* (also written 6-31G(d)) — 6-31G plus one set of d-type polarization functions (\(l=2\)) on every heavy (non-hydrogen) atom, letting the electron density distort away from spherical/axial symmetry as real bonds form — the "polarization functions" concept from the left panel, applied concretely.

\[\text{6-31G*} \;=\; \text{6-31G} \;+\; \chi_{d}(\mathbf r),\quad l=2\ \text{(heavy atoms only)}\]

Without this, bond angles and reaction energies from 6-31G alone are systematically off.

6-31G** (also written 6-31G(d,p)) — 6-31G* plus p-type polarization (\(l=1\)) on hydrogen as well, on top of the d-functions already on heavy atoms. Matters whenever hydrogen's position is chemically important — hydrogen bonding, proton transfer, N–H/O–H stretching modes.

\[\text{6-31G**} \;=\; \text{6-31G*} \;+\; \chi_{p}(\mathbf r),\quad l=1\ \text{(hydrogen)}\]

6-311G — Triple-split-valence. The valence shell isn't split into two functions but three (roughly a 3+1+1 primitive pattern), giving each valence orbital more independent radial freedom than a double-split basis like 6-31G — the "triple-zeta" concept from the left panel.

\[\chi_{core}=\sum_{p=1}^{6} d_p g_p,\quad \chi_{val}^{(1)}=\sum_{p=1}^{3} d_p g_p,\quad \chi_{val}^{(2)}=g_1,\quad \chi_{val}^{(3)}=g_1\]

cc-pVDZ — "Correlation-consistent polarized Valence Double-Zeta." Built with Dunning's correlation-consistent recipe: functions are grouped into shells so that every added shell contributes a comparable amount of recovered electron-correlation energy, giving smooth, systematic convergence toward the exact (complete-basis-set) limit as you move up the cc-pVXZ family. Double-zeta valence, one polarization shell per atom.

Designed specifically to pair with correlated post-Hartree–Fock methods (MP2, coupled cluster), not for HF alone.

cc-pVTZ — Same correlation-consistent family, one zeta level up from cc-pVDZ: triple-zeta valence plus two polarization shells per atom. Noticeably more accurate, at proportionally higher cost — the usual next rung when cc-pVDZ isn't accurate enough for quantitative energies or geometries.

aug-cc-pVTZ — cc-pVTZ augmented with one extra diffuse function of every angular momentum already present on each atom (s, p, d, …), each with a deliberately small exponent so it spreads far from the nucleus — the "diffuse functions" concept from the left panel, made concrete. Needed for anions, excited states, and weakly-bound (van der Waals) complexes where density reaches well beyond the valence region.

\[\text{aug-cc-pVTZ} \;=\; \text{cc-pVTZ} \;+\; \{\chi_l^{diffuse}\}_{l=0}^{l_{max}},\qquad \alpha_{diffuse}\ll\alpha_{valence}\]

From atoms to bonds

Molecular orbitals: bonding & antibonding LCAO

Bring two atoms together and their atomic orbitals combine — a linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO) — into new molecular orbitals delocalized over both nuclei. Depending on the relative sign of the mixing, that combination either piles density up between the nuclei (bonding) or tears a node right through the bond (antibonding).

LCAO construction

\[ \psi = c_A\phi_A + c_B\phi_B \]

\(\phi_A,\phi_B\) are atomic 1s orbitals centered on nuclei A and B; \(c_A,c_B\) are mixing coefficients chosen — by the variational principle — to minimize the energy of \(\psi\).

Bonding σ Antibonding σ*

Bonding orbital — \(c_A,c_B\) share the same sign (in-phase). The atomic orbitals interfere constructively between the nuclei, piling up electron density in the internuclear region and screening the nuclear repulsion. No node between the nuclei. Lower energy than either atomic orbital alone.

Antibonding orbital — \(c_A,c_B\) have opposite sign (out-of-phase). Destructive interference forces a node exactly between the nuclei, depleting density where it would otherwise hold the atoms together. Higher energy than either atomic orbital alone — occupying it weakens or breaks the bond.

Normalized forms — for a homonuclear pair (\(\phi_A,\phi_B\) identical, overlap \(S\)), the properly normalized combinations are \(\psi_\pm = (\phi_A\pm\phi_B)/\sqrt{2(1\pm S)}\).

Two flavors of electron–electron avoidance

Coulomb repulsion vs. the exchange hole

The Fock operator (Tab 5) has two pieces, \(J\) and \(K\), and they behave completely differently in real space. Coulomb repulsion \(1/r_{12}\) is a classical force that doesn't know or care about spin. The exchange hole is a purely quantum effect of antisymmetry — it only opens up for electrons that share the reference electron's spin. Hartree–Fock is exact within the single-determinant, mean-field approximation — but the true many-electron wavefunction is never a single determinant. SCF algorithm — Hartree–Fock has no closed form because the Fock operator depends on the orbitals it produces (Tab 6). Every real implementation is therefore an iterative fixed-point search: guess a density, build the Fock matrix from it, solve an eigenvalue problem for new orbitals, rebuild the density, and repeat until the density (and energy) stop changing.

Roothaan–Hall equations — expanding each molecular orbital in a finite basis \(\phi_i=\sum_\mu C_{\mu i}\chi_\mu\) turns the differential Hartree–Fock equation \(\hat F\phi_i=\varepsilon_i\phi_i\) into a matrix generalized eigenvalue problem:

\[ FC = SC\varepsilon \]

F and S are matrices in the basis \(\{\chi_\mu\}\), C holds the orbital coefficients, and \(\varepsilon\) is the diagonal matrix of orbital energies. This is the equation every SCF code actually solves, cycle after cycle.

Overlap matrix S — basis functions are not usually orthogonal, so \(S_{\mu\nu}=\int \chi_\mu^*\chi_\nu\,d^3r\) is not the identity. \(S\) measures how much basis functions spatially overlap; it appears on the right of \(FC=SC\varepsilon\) precisely because the basis isn't orthonormal. See the live 2-center example below.

Orthogonalization — standard eigenvalue solvers need an ordinary eigenvalue problem, not a generalized one. Löwdin's symmetric orthogonalization builds \(X=S^{-1/2}\) and transforms \(F'=X^{\dagger}FX\), \(C=XC'\), turning \(FC=SC\varepsilon\) into the ordinary problem \(F'C'=C'\varepsilon\) with \(X^{\dagger}SX=I\).

Matrix diagonalization — once orthogonalized, finding the orbitals is exactly finding the eigenvectors/eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix \(F'\). This is the single most expensive linear-algebra step repeated every SCF cycle.

Eigenvalue solver — modern codes use fast direct tridiagonalization + QL/QR methods, but the underlying idea is captured by the classical Jacobi eigenvalue algorithm: repeatedly rotate away the largest off-diagonal element until the matrix is (numerically) diagonal. Try it live below.

Where these fit — S and orthogonalization (X) are built once per geometry; the Fock build, diagonalization, and density update repeat every SCF cycle — exactly the loop shown in the flowchart on the right.

Matrix multiplication — the workhorse operation of every SCF cycle: building the Fock matrix, transforming to an orthonormal basis, and forming the density matrix are all matrix products. \((AB)_{ij}=\sum_k A_{ik}B_{kj}\) — see the step-by-step demo below.

        
Diagonalization — solving \(F'C'=C'\varepsilon\) (Tab 18) means finding the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix. No single "best" algorithm exists — Jacobi, QR, power iteration, and Davidson below are four different strategies, each suited to a different situation (small vs. large, dense vs. sparse, all eigenvalues vs. just the lowest few).

        
Jacobi method — repeatedly apply a 2×2 rotation that zeroes the currently-largest off-diagonal element. Simple, numerically robust, and easy to parallelize, but scales poorly to large matrices — mostly used today for small matrices or as a teaching example (see Tab 18's live version).

        
QR algorithm — factor \(A_k=Q_kR_k\), then form \(A_{k+1}=Q_k^{\mathsf T}A_kQ_k\) (an orthogonal similarity transform, so eigenvalues never change). Iterating drives \(A_k\) toward a diagonal matrix. This — with shifts and Hessenberg reduction for speed — is the workhorse behind general-purpose dense eigensolvers like LAPACK's.

        
Davidson method — for matrices too large to diagonalize directly (common in correlated post-HF methods), build a small "subspace" of trial vectors, diagonalize the tiny projected matrix exactly, then use the residual and a diagonal preconditioner to generate a new trial vector that expands the subspace toward the true lowest eigenvector. Converges to the smallest eigenvalue(s) without ever forming the full matrix explicitly.

        
Power iteration — the simplest eigensolver: repeatedly apply \(A\) to a vector and renormalize, \(v_{k+1}=Av_k/\|Av_k\|\). It converges to the eigenvector of largest |eigenvalue|, with convergence rate set by the ratio of the two largest eigenvalues. Davidson's subspace idea is, in spirit, a much smarter generalization of this.

        
LU decomposition — factor \(A=LU\) (lower-triangular × upper-triangular) via Gaussian elimination. Once factored, solving \(Ax=b\) for many different \(b\) is cheap (two triangular solves). Without pivoting it can fail or become wildly inaccurate when a pivot is small — see the live breakdown demo below.

        
Cholesky decomposition — for a symmetric positive-definite matrix, \(A=LL^{\mathsf T}\) with a single triangular factor \(L\). About twice as fast as general LU and always numerically stable (no pivoting needed) — exactly the case for the density matrix and overlap matrix, which is why Cholesky shows up throughout quantum chemistry codes.

        
Condition number — \(\kappa(A)=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}\), the ratio of largest to smallest singular value. A large \(\kappa\) means small errors in the input (or roundoff during computation) get amplified enormously in the solution — exactly what happens to an SCF calculation with a near-linearly-dependent basis set (Tab 18's overlap matrix S becomes ill-conditioned).

        
Eigenvectors — the directions a matrix only stretches (never rotates): \(Av=\lambda v\). For a symmetric matrix, eigenvectors are orthogonal and the matrix's action on any vector decomposes cleanly along them — the picture made concrete in the ellipse demo below.

Quantum chemistry — the field this whole app has been building toward: predicting molecular geometries, reaction energies, and spectra directly from the electronic Schrödinger equation. HF is the entry point; MP2, CI, and coupled cluster (Tab 14) refine it toward chemical accuracy (~1 kcal/mol).

            
Electronic structure theory — the umbrella discipline covering HF, density functional theory (DFT), and every post-HF method. DFT replaces the many-electron wavefunction with the electron density itself (Hohenberg–Kohn theorem) and is today's dominant workhorse — faster than HF-based correlation methods, though it still needs an approximate "exchange-correlation functional," playing a role similar to HF's exact-exchange term.

            
Band structure — take the mean-field idea and impose translational periodicity: each electron in a crystal feels the averaged, periodic potential of every nucleus and every other electron (Bloch's theorem). Solving the resulting periodic Hartree–Fock/DFT equations gives energy bands as a function of crystal momentum k — see the live 1D tight-binding demo below.

            
Molecular spectroscopy — HF/DFT-computed vibrational frequencies (from the curvature of the potential energy surface, Tab 15's dissociation curve generalized to many nuclear coordinates) and electronic excitation energies let chemists assign and predict IR, Raman, and UV-Vis spectra without first running the experiment — see the live vibrational-level demo below.

            
Materials science — predicting a solid's elastic constants, defect energies, and phase stability starts from the same electronic-structure equations, evaluated on a periodic crystal lattice instead of an isolated molecule. Used to screen candidate battery electrodes, semiconductors, and alloys computationally before any synthesis.

            
Nanotechnology — nanoparticles and nanotubes sit in an awkward size regime: too large for exact many-body treatment, too small for bulk (periodic) approximations to fully apply. Electronic structure methods here reveal how confinement itself changes electronic properties — e.g. the size-dependent band gap of quantum dots, a direct consequence of "particle in a box"-like confinement acting on the same orbitals from Tab 9.

            
Catalysis — a catalyst works by opening a lower-energy electronic pathway between reactants and products. Electronic structure calculations map the full reaction's potential energy surface, locating the transition state and its activation energy — see the live reaction-coordinate demo below.

            
Drug design — computing the binding energy of a candidate molecule inside a protein's active site is, at heart, the same electronic-structure problem as any other molecule — just embedded in a much larger environment (often treated with cheaper classical or semi-empirical methods around a quantum-mechanically treated active site, a "QM/MM" approach). Binding affinity estimates guide which candidates are worth synthesizing and testing.

            
Atomic physics — Hartree–Fock's oldest domain (Tab 16's helium example, Tab 17's periodic table): computing atomic energy levels, ionization energies, and transition rates for isolated atoms and ions, including highly-charged ions relevant to fusion plasmas and precision spectroscopy.

            
Nuclear physics — the same mean-field logic applies one level down: nucleons (protons and neutrons) in a nucleus can be treated as independent particles moving in a mean field generated by every other nucleon (the nuclear shell model), directly analogous to electrons in an atom. "Hartree–Fock" methods with realistic nucleon–nucleon interactions predict nuclear binding energies, shapes, and shell closures ("magic numbers").

            
Astrophysics — mean-field electronic structure sets the equation of state of matter in white dwarfs (electron degeneracy pressure supporting the star against gravity) and shapes atomic opacities that control how radiation moves through stellar atmospheres. At even higher densities, mean-field nuclear physics (previous tag) governs neutron-star structure.

Where the mean-field picture breaks down

    
Limitations of Hartree–Fock

    
Every earlier tab showed what Hartree–Fock gets right. This tab is about where the
    single-determinant, mean-field approximation from Tab 3 runs out of road — the physical effects it
    structurally cannot capture, no matter how large the basis set (Tab 8) gets. Click a limitation below to
    read what goes wrong and see it illustrated, then compare how later methods do against each one.

        Weak dispersion

Click a limitation above to read what it means, and see it illustrated on the right.


            
No dynamic correlation — Tab 14 defined \(E_{\text{corr}}=E_{\text{exact}}-E_{HF}\). Hartree–Fock's mean field lets each electron see only the time-averaged density of the others (Tab 3), so opposite-spin electrons are treated as statistically independent — there is no correlation hole opening up around a reference electron (revisit Tab 14's side-by-side view). This single missing piece is called dynamic correlation, and it is present, to some degree, in literally every real electronic system.


            
Weak dispersion (van der Waals) forces — the attraction between two well-separated closed-shell atoms or molecules (like two argon atoms, or two stacked benzene rings) comes entirely from instantaneous, correlated fluctuations of electron density on one fragment inducing a dipole on the other. Since HF's mean field is static and uncorrelated by construction, it produces essentially zero binding for a pure dispersion complex — not a small underestimate, but qualitatively the wrong physics. Recovering dispersion needs a correlated method (MP2, CCSD(T)) or an empirical dispersion correction bolted onto DFT.


            
Multi-reference failure — HF assumes one Slater determinant (Tab 4) is a qualitatively adequate description. That assumption collapses whenever two or more electron configurations become close in energy and comparably important — the textbook case is stretching a single bond (Tab 15's H₂ curve) past its equilibrium length. Restricted HF dissociates H₂ to an unphysical 50/50 mixture of neutral atoms and an ionic H⁺H⁻ pair, and the energy curve bends the wrong way at large R. Multi-configurational methods (CASSCF and its descendants) fix this by mixing several determinants from the start.


            
Strong (static) correlation — closely related to multi-reference failure but usually reserved for extended systems: transition-metal complexes with near-degenerate d-orbitals, and Mott-insulating solids where strong on-site Coulomb repulsion localizes electrons that band theory (Tab 20) predicts should be delocalized. A single mean field cannot represent a state where electrons are strongly correlated to avoid each other on a lattice — HF (and often even DFT) can get the qualitative physics backwards, predicting a metal where the real material is an insulator.


            
Excited states — the entire SCF machinery from Tab 6 variationally minimizes the energy of one Slater determinant, which by construction targets the ground state. Naively promoting an electron to a virtual orbital and re-running SCF (Δ-SCF) sometimes works but isn't rigorous and can converge to the wrong state, "root flip," or fail to converge at all. Properly, excited states need their own machinery — Configuration Interaction Singles (CIS), Time-Dependent HF/DFT (TDHF/TDDFT), or state-averaged CASSCF — layered on top of everything built in this app.

Accuracy — plain Hartree–Fock has no correlation energy at all (Tab 14), so its bond lengths, atomization energies, and reaction energies are systematically off, often by many kcal/mol. DFT bakes an approximate correlation (and exchange) functional directly into the mean field, so even a cheap functional usually beats bare HF for everyday thermochemistry and geometries — though DFT's accuracy is functional-dependent and less systematically improvable than the post-HF ladder from Tab 14.


            
Speed — for the same basis set, a single HF or DFT self-consistent cycle costs roughly the same order of operations (Tab 18's O(N⁴) integral wall dominates either way, though modern DFT codes exploit numerical integration grids and screening tricks that often make it faster in practice). The real speed gap opens up one rung higher: HF is a reasonable jumping-off point for expensive post-HF correlation methods (MP2, CCSD(T), Tab 14), while DFT usually delivers correlation "for free" in the mean-field step itself, at a fraction of post-HF's cost.


            
Scaling — conventional HF's cost is dominated by two-electron integrals, formally O(N⁴) in basis size N (Tab 18), though integral screening exploits sparsity to push this lower in practice for large systems. Standard Kohn–Sham DFT has the same O(N⁴) integral formal scaling, but its exchange-correlation term is evaluated on a numerical grid rather than analytically, which is often cheaper in the sub-quartic regime and scales more favorably with modern linear-scaling techniques. See the live N-scan below.


            
Applications — HF is mostly used today as a stepping stone: the reference determinant for post-HF correlation methods (MP2, CI, CCSD(T), Tab 14), or as a fast qualitative check before a more expensive calculation. DFT, especially with hybrid or range-separated functionals, is the default choice for most routine molecular geometries, thermochemistry, and — critically — periodic solid-state calculations (Tab 20's band structure) and biomolecular systems too large for correlated wavefunction methods.


            
Exchange — HF's exchange operator K (Tab 5) is computed exactly from the orbitals — no approximation, just an expensive integral. Pure DFT functionals (LDA, GGA) instead approximate exchange locally


            
Correlation — HF has zero correlation energy by construction (Tab 14); it must be added afterward via MP2, CI, or CC. DFT's exchange-correlation functional \(E_{xc}[\rho]\) folds an approximate correlation energy directly into the SCF cycle from iteration one — cheaply, but with accuracy entirely dependent on the functional's quality and no systematic way to know the error in advance, unlike the wavefunction-based post-HF hierarchy.

Hartree–Fock vs. post-HF methods

    
Tab 14 introduced the correlation-energy ladder; Tab 21 showed where HF structurally
    fails. This tab is the practical companion: seven post-HF methods compared side by side, an interactive
    decision tree for picking one, and a live accuracy-vs-cost plot showing exactly what you're trading for
    what.

Click a method above to read what it does, how it scales, and see it illustrated on the right.


            
MP2 (Møller–Plesset 2nd order) — the cheapest correlated method. Treats the difference between the true Hamiltonian and the HF mean-field Hamiltonian as a perturbation, evaluated to second order using double excitations out of the HF reference (Tab 14). Recovers roughly 80–90% of dynamic correlation at ~N⁵ cost, but is single-reference — it degrades badly wherever HF itself starts to fail (Tab 21's multi-reference cases).


            
CIS (Configuration Interaction Singles) — mixes the HF determinant with every singly-excited determinant and diagonalizes. Unlike the other methods here, CIS isn't primarily aimed at the ground state — it's the cheapest genuine method for vertical excited-state energies (Tab 21's "excited states" limitation), at roughly the same cost as HF itself. It misses double excitations entirely, so excited states with real double-excitation character come out badly wrong.


            
CI (truncated, e.g. CISD) — mixes the HF determinant with singly- and doubly-excited determinants and diagonalizes the Hamiltonian in that expanded space. More accurate than MP2 (~90%+ correlation recovered), but truncated CI is not size-extensive — unlike CCSD below, its accuracy quietly degrades as the system gets larger, which is why it's mostly been superseded by coupled cluster in practice.


            
MCSCF (e.g. CASSCF) — optimizes both the orbitals and the mixing coefficients of several determinants simultaneously over a chosen "active space" of near-degenerate orbitals. This is the direct fix for Tab 21's multi-reference failure: bond-breaking, diradicals, and transition-metal near-degeneracies. It captures static (multi-reference) correlation well, but essentially none of the dynamic correlation — real accuracy usually needs a follow-up correlated step (CASPT2, NEVPT2) on top.


            
CCSD (Coupled Cluster Singles and Doubles) — instead of adding excitations linearly like CI, coupled cluster exponentiates a cluster operator, \(\Psi=e^{T_1+T_2}\Phi_{HF}\). That exponential form automatically includes disconnected higher excitations and restores size-extensivity, so CCSD's accuracy doesn't degrade with system size the way truncated CI's does. Recovers a substantial majority of dynamic correlation at ~N⁶ cost.


            
CCSD(T) — CCSD plus a perturbative correction for triple excitations, added non-iteratively for efficiency. Often called the "gold standard" of single-reference quantum chemistry: typically recovers well over 99% of the correlation energy for well-behaved systems, at ~N⁷ cost — the steepest routinely-affordable rung on this ladder, and still fundamentally single-reference (Tab 21 still applies).


            
Full CI — every possible excitation in the given basis set, mixed exactly. This is the exact ground-state (and excited-state) energy within that basis (Tab 14) — the ceiling every method above is measured against. Its cost grows combinatorially, not polynomially, with system size (Tab 1's Hilbert-space explosion), so it's feasible only for the smallest molecules and is used mainly as a benchmark.
