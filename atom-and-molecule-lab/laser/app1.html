Section 01

Introduction to Lasers

A laser generates an intense, coherent beam via stimulated emission — a quantum process where photons of identical frequency, phase, and direction multiply inside a resonant cavity. Unlike any ordinary source, a laser emits highly directional, monochromatic radiation with extraordinary spatial and temporal coherence.

Ordinary Light — Incoherent
Laser Light — Coherent
Quantum Emission Processes

Spontaneous Emission

An excited atom spontaneously transitions to a lower energy level, releasing a photon with random direction and phase after a characteristic lifetime. This produces the incoherent light of LEDs, lamps, and stars — photons flying in all directions with no phase relationship.

Einstein A coefficient (spontaneous rate)
A21 = 8πhν³n³ / c³ · B21

Stimulated Emission

An incident photon triggers an excited atom to emit a second identical photon — same frequency, phase, direction, and polarization. Predicted by Einstein (1917), this coherent multiplication is the fundamental mechanism behind all laser operation.

Stimulated emission rate
Wstim = B21 · ρ(ν) · N2
Stimulated Emission
Population Inversion

Under thermal equilibrium, higher energy states are exponentially less populated (Boltzmann distribution). Lasing requires population inversion — N2 > N1 — so stimulated emission dominates absorption and net optical gain g(ν) becomes positive throughout the gain medium.

Boltzmann thermal population ratio
N2/N1 = exp(−ΔE / kBT)
Condition for positive net gain
ΔN = N2 − N1 > 0  →  g(ν) = σ·ΔN > 0

Historical Development
1917
Einstein's Prediction
Albert Einstein theorizes stimulated emission in his quantum theory of radiation — providing the theoretical foundation for laser amplification over 40 years before its realization.
1954
MASER Demonstrated
Townes, Basov, and Prokhorov independently build the MASER. Nobel Prize 1964. The direct conceptual predecessor to all optical laser systems.
1958
Optical Laser Proposed
Schawlow and Townes extend the maser to optical frequencies, proposing the Fabry-Pérot resonator design and calculating threshold conditions for the first time.
1960
First Laser — Ruby
Theodore Maiman fires the world's first working laser at Hughes Research Labs — a ruby crystal pumped by a xenon flashlamp producing 694.3 nm pulsed coherent red light.
1961
He-Ne CW Gas Laser
Javan, Bennett & Herriott at Bell Labs demonstrate the first CW gas laser at 1152 nm, later extended to the iconic 632.8 nm red wavelength used for alignment and interferometry.
1964
CO₂ and Nd:YAG
Patel develops the high-power CO₂ infrared laser at 10.6 μm; Geusic et al. demonstrate Nd:YAG at 1064 nm. Both remain dominant industrial and medical platforms to this day.
1985
Chirped Pulse Amplification
Strickland and Mourou invent CPA — stretching, amplifying, and recompressing ultrashort pulses to reach petawatt intensities. Nobel Prize in Physics awarded 2018.
Today
Fiber, Diode & Photonic Era
High-brightness fiber lasers dominate manufacturing. Diode arrays exceed 70% wall-plug efficiency. On-chip photonic integrated lasers enable LiDAR, optical computing and quantum photonics.
Section 02

Laser Formation Mechanism

Laser action emerges from the interplay of energy pumping, population inversion, stimulated emission gain, and optical resonator feedback. All conditions must be met simultaneously — and round-trip gain must exceed all round-trip losses — before coherent output builds up from spontaneous emission noise.

Each gain medium exploits distinct quantum transitions, cavity geometries, and pumping mechanisms.
