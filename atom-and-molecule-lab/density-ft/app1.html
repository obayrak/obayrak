1 · Introduction

What is Density Functional Theory?

Density Functional Theory (DFT) is a quantum-mechanical method for computing the electronic structure of atoms, molecules, and solids. Its central and remarkable claim is that all ground-state properties of a many-electron system are determined by the electron density $n(\mathbf{r})$ alone — a function of just three spatial coordinates — rather than by the full many-electron wavefunction $\Psi(\mathbf{r}_1,\mathbf{r}_2,\dots,\mathbf{r}_N)$, which depends on $3N$ coordinates.

KEY IDEA Instead of solving for an object living in a $3N$-dimensional space, DFT reformulates quantum mechanics in terms of an object living in ordinary 3D space — the electron density $n(\mathbf{r})$.

Why DFT is Important

  • Computational tractability: reduces an intractable $3N$-dimensional problem to a 3-dimensional one.
  • Predictive accuracy: with modern exchange-correlation functionals, DFT predicts bond lengths, reaction energies, and band structures often within a few percent of experiment.
  • Universality: applies to molecules, liquids, surfaces, defects, and crystalline solids using the same theoretical framework.
  • Scalability: practical DFT calculations scale far more favorably with system size than wavefunction-based methods, enabling simulations of hundreds to thousands of atoms.

History of DFT

Walter Kohn's Nobel Prize

1998 Walter Kohn was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry "for his development of the density-functional theory," sharing the prize with John Pople (recognized for developing computational methods in quantum chemistry). Kohn's 1964 paper with Pierre Hohenberg and his 1965 paper with Lu Jeu Sham established the theoretical foundation that transformed electronic-structure theory from a formally exact but impractical many-body theory into a computationally tractable, widely used tool.

Modern Importance

Today DFT is the workhorse method of computational condensed-matter physics, materials science, and quantum chemistry. It underlies:

  • Design of battery electrode and electrolyte materials
  • Discovery and screening of heterogeneous catalysts
  • Prediction of semiconductor and topological material band structures
  • Computational drug design and protein–ligand binding energetics
  • High-throughput materials databases (e.g. Materials Project, AFLOW, OQMD)

Relationship to Quantum Mechanics

DFT is not an approximation to quantum mechanics — it is, in principle, an exact reformulation of it. The Hohenberg–Kohn theorems (Section 3 onward) prove that the ground-state density determines the external potential (up to a constant), and hence the full many-body Hamiltonian and, in principle, every observable. The approximations enter only when we construct practical exchange-correlation functionals.

Relationship to Quantum Chemistry

Traditional quantum chemistry (Hartree–Fock, configuration interaction, coupled cluster) works directly with the many-electron wavefunction. DFT instead works with the density and an auxiliary set of non-interacting Kohn–Sham orbitals, often achieving comparable accuracy to correlated wavefunction methods at a fraction of the computational cost.

Relationship to Materials Science

Materials science relies on DFT to predict structural, electronic, mechanical, and thermodynamic properties of crystals and surfaces before — or instead of — synthesizing them in the lab. High-throughput DFT screening now underlies entire "materials genome" initiatives aimed at accelerating discovery of batteries, photovoltaics, and structural alloys.

2 · Motivation — Why the Many-Electron Problem is Hard

The full time-independent Schrödinger equation for $N$ interacting electrons is, in principle, exactly solvable. In practice it is not — for reasons that are worth making concrete and visual before we write down the Hamiltonian itself in Section 3.

Exponential Computational Scaling

The many-electron wavefunction $\Psi(\mathbf{r}_1,\dots,\mathbf{r}_N)$ is a function of $3N$ spatial coordinates (plus spin). If we tried to represent it on a real-space grid with $g$ points along each spatial direction, the number of grid points needed is:

$$ \text{grid points} \sim g^{3N} $$

This is exponential in the number of electrons $N$ — not polynomial. Doubling the system size does not double the cost; it squares it (or worse).

Electron–Electron Interaction

Electrons repel each other via the Coulomb interaction. This means the equation of motion for any one electron depends on the instantaneous positions of all other electrons — the problem does not separate into $N$ independent single-electron equations. The number of distinct pairwise interaction terms is:

$$ \binom{N}{2} = \frac{N(N-1)}{2} $$

The Many-Body Problem

Because of electron–electron repulsion, the motion of each electron is correlated with every other electron's motion. This is the same conceptual obstruction that makes the classical three-body gravitational problem far harder than the two-body Kepler problem — except here we have not three bodies, but potentially $N \sim 10^{23}$ in a macroscopic solid, and the objects are quantum mechanical, so we must track amplitudes and phases, not just trajectories.

The Curse of Dimensionality

The term "curse of dimensionality" (coined by Richard Bellman) refers to the fact that the volume of a space grows exponentially with its number of dimensions, so the resources needed to sample or represent a function over that space also grow exponentially. For the many-electron wavefunction, each additional electron adds 3 more dimensions to the domain of $\Psi$.

CONCRETE EXAMPLE A modest 30-electron system, represented on a coarse 10-point grid per spatial direction, would require storing $10^{90}$ numbers — vastly more than the number of atoms in the observable universe ($\sim 10^{80}$). Direct wavefunction diagonalization is hopeless beyond a handful of electrons, which is precisely the gap DFT is designed to close by working with the 3-dimensional density instead.

3 · The Many-Electron Schrödinger Equation

The Full Electronic Hamiltonian

Under the Born–Oppenheimer approximation (nuclei treated as fixed external sources — see Section 4), the non-relativistic electronic Hamiltonian for $N$ electrons and $M$ fixed nuclei of charge $Z_I$ at positions $\mathbf{R}_I$ is:

$$ \hat{H} = \underbrace{-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla_i^2}_{\hat{T}\ \text{(kinetic energy)}} \;\underbrace{-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{I=1}^{M}\frac{Z_I e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0|\mathbf{r}_i-\mathbf{R}_I|}}_{\hat{V}_{ext}\ \text{(electron–nucleus attraction)}} \;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{i

Kinetic Energy $\hat T$

Sums the single-particle kinetic energy operator $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla_i^2$ over all $N$ electrons. This term alone would separate into $N$ independent one-electron problems — it is not the source of the difficulty.

Electron–Nucleus Attraction $\hat V_{ext}$

Each electron feels the Coulomb attraction of every fixed nucleus. Because the nuclei are external (fixed) sources under Born–Oppenheimer, this term is a one-body external potential $v_{ext}(\mathbf{r}_i)$ acting independently on each electron — it also does not couple electrons to each other.

THE HARD TERM Electron–electron repulsion $\hat V_{ee}$ is the only term that couples different electrons' coordinates together. It is precisely this term that prevents the many-electron Schrödinger equation from separating into $N$ independent single-particle equations, and it is the entire reason the many-body problem is hard.

Why Exact Analytical Solutions Are (Generally) Impossible

Because $\hat V_{ee}$ depends on $|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|$ for every pair $(i,j)$, the Hamiltonian is not a sum of independent one-electron Hamiltonians, and the wavefunction $\Psi(\mathbf{r}_1,\dots,\mathbf{r}_N)$ cannot in general be written as a simple product (or single Slater determinant) of one-electron orbitals — the true wavefunction contains electron correlation. Exact analytic solutions exist only for one-electron systems (H atom, H$_2^+$); already for helium's two electrons, no closed-form solution exists and the ground state must be computed numerically or approximated.

4 · The Born–Oppenheimer Approximation

Separation of Nuclear and Electronic Motion

Nuclei are thousands of times more massive than electrons ($m_p/m_e \approx 1836$ for the proton). Consequently electrons move — and respond to changes in nuclear position — far faster than the nuclei themselves move. Max Born and J. Robert Oppenheimer (1927) showed that the full molecular wavefunction can be approximately factorized as:

$$ \Psi_{total}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R}) \approx \psi_{elec}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{R})\,\chi_{nucl}(\mathbf{R}) $$

where $\mathbf{r}$ collectively denotes electronic coordinates and $\mathbf{R}$ nuclear coordinates. The electronic wavefunction $\psi_{elec}$ depends on $\mathbf{R}$ only parametrically — the nuclear positions enter as fixed numbers, not as dynamical variables, when solving the electronic problem.

Fixed-Nuclei (Clamped-Nucleus) Approximation

For each fixed nuclear configuration $\mathbf{R}$, we solve the electronic Schrödinger equation:

$$ \hat H_{elec}(\mathbf{R})\,\psi_{elec}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{R}) = E_{elec}(\mathbf{R})\,\psi_{elec}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{R}) $$

This is exactly the Hamiltonian written in Section 3, with the nuclear positions $\mathbf{R}_I$ entered as fixed parameters rather than dynamical operators. Solving it at many different values of $\mathbf{R}$ traces out a function $E_{elec}(\mathbf{R})$.

The Potential Energy Surface (PES)

$E_{elec}(\mathbf{R})$, together with the (constant, at fixed $\mathbf{R}$) nuclear–nuclear repulsion, defines the potential energy surface on which the nuclei themselves subsequently move — governed by their own (now purely nuclear) Schrödinger equation, or, classically, by Newton's equations with force $-\nabla_\mathbf{R} E_{elec}(\mathbf{R})$. The PES is the central object linking electronic structure theory (including DFT) to molecular dynamics, vibrational spectroscopy, and reaction rate theory.

WHY IT MATTERS FOR DFT Every DFT calculation of a molecule or solid is, in practice, a single-point evaluation of $E_{elec}(\mathbf{R})$ for one fixed nuclear geometry. Structural relaxation, vibrational frequencies, and reaction pathways all come from mapping out this surface with repeated DFT calculations at different $\mathbf{R}$.

5 · The Electron Density

The Electron Density

The electron density $n(\mathbf{r})$ is defined as the expectation value of the electron-number density operator in the many-electron state $\Psi$. For $N$ electrons it is obtained by integrating $|\Psi|^2$ over all coordinates except one, and summing over spin:

$$ n(\mathbf{r}) = N\sum_{s_1}\int d^3r_2\cdots d^3r_N\, \big|\Psi(\mathbf{r},s_1;\mathbf{r}_2,s_2;\dots;\mathbf{r}_N,s_N)\big|^2 $$

Unlike the full wavefunction, $n(\mathbf{r})$ depends on only three spatial coordinates, no matter how many electrons the system contains — this is precisely why it can serve as the fundamental variable of an exact reformulation of quantum mechanics (the subject of Section 6).

Probability Interpretation

KEY IDEA $n(\mathbf{r})\,d^3r$ is the expected number of electrons found in an infinitesimal volume element $d^3r$ located at $\mathbf{r}$, at any instant, regardless of which electron it is. Because electrons are indistinguishable, $n(\mathbf{r})$ is a single, well-defined quantity summed over all $N$ electrons rather than a separate probability density per particle.

Density Normalization

Integrating the density over all space recovers the total number of electrons in the system:

$$ \int n(\mathbf{r})\, d^3r = N $$

This normalization condition is a strict constraint that any candidate density — and any approximate functional built from it — must satisfy exactly.

Physical Meaning

  • Measurable quantity: unlike the wavefunction's phase, $n(\mathbf{r})$ is (in principle) directly observable — X-ray and electron diffraction experiments measure quantities closely related to the electron density.
  • Chemical intuition: regions of high $n(\mathbf{r})$ correspond to bonding regions, lone pairs, and core electrons; density difference maps are routinely used to visualize charge transfer upon bond formation.
  • Foundational role: the Hohenberg–Kohn theorems (Section 6) prove that $n(\mathbf{r})$ alone determines the external potential and hence, in principle, every ground-state property of the system.

6 · The Hohenberg–Kohn Theorems

Section 5 introduced the electron density $n(\mathbf{r})$ as a 3-dimensional stand-in for the $3N$-dimensional wavefunction. Hohenberg and Kohn (1964) proved two theorems that turn this stand-in into the exact, complete foundation of a reformulated quantum mechanics — showing that $n(\mathbf{r})$ is not merely a useful summary of $\Psi$, but in fact carries all the information $\Psi$ does.

Theorem I — The Existence / Uniqueness Theorem

STATEMENT

The external potential $v_{ext}(\mathbf{r})$ is, up to an additive constant, a unique functional of the non-degenerate ground-state density $n(\mathbf{r})$. Equivalently: two different external potentials that differ by more than a constant cannot produce the same ground-state density.

MATHEMATICAL FORMULATION

Proof by contradiction (reductio ad absurdum). Suppose two potentials $v_{ext}(\mathbf r)$ and $v_{ext}'(\mathbf r)$, not differing by a constant, give rise to Hamiltonians $\hat H$ and $\hat H'$ with distinct non-degenerate ground states $\Psi$, $\Psi'$ and energies $E_0$, $E_0'$ — yet, by assumption, produce the same density $n(\mathbf r)$. Since $\Psi'\neq\Psi$, the Rayleigh–Ritz variational principle gives a strict inequality:

$$ E_0 < \langle\Psi'|\hat H|\Psi'\rangle = \langle\Psi'|\hat H'|\Psi'\rangle + \langle\Psi'|\hat H-\hat H'|\Psi'\rangle = E_0' + \int n(\mathbf r)\big[v_{ext}(\mathbf r)-v_{ext}'(\mathbf r)\big]\,d^3r $$

Exchanging the roles of the primed and unprimed systems gives the analogous inequality:

$$ E_0' < E_0 + \int n(\mathbf r)\big[v_{ext}'(\mathbf r)-v_{ext}(\mathbf r)\big]\,d^3r $$

Adding these two strict inequalities, the integral terms cancel exactly, leaving:

$$ E_0 + E_0' < E_0 + E_0' $$

a contradiction. Hence no two potentials differing by more than a constant can share the same ground-state density — the map $v_{ext}(\mathbf r)\to n(\mathbf r)$ is injective.

PHYSICAL MEANING

Because $n(\mathbf r)$ fixes $v_{ext}(\mathbf r)$ (up to the physically irrelevant zero of energy), and $N=\int n(\mathbf r)\,d^3r$ fixes the electron number, the density determines the entire Hamiltonian $\hat H = \hat T + \hat V_{ee} + \hat V_{ext}$ — since $\hat T$ and $\hat V_{ee}$ are universal operators, identical for every electronic system. A fully specified Hamiltonian means, in principle, a fully specified Schrödinger equation whose solution yields every state and every observable of the system.

CONSEQUENCES

Every ground-state observable — in particular the total energy — becomes a functional of $n(\mathbf r)$ alone:

$$ E[n] = \underbrace{T[n]+V_{ee}[n]}_{F_{HK}[n]\ \text{(universal)}} + \int v_{ext}(\mathbf r)\,n(\mathbf r)\,d^3r $$

The Hohenberg–Kohn functional $F_{HK}[n]$ has exactly the same mathematical form for any system — one hydrogen atom, one silicon crystal, one protein — with all system-specific information carried entirely by the explicit $v_{ext}$ term. This universality is what makes DFT, in principle, a single unified theory rather than a system-by-system prescription.

Theorem II — The Variational Theorem

STATEMENT

For any trial density $\tilde n(\mathbf r)\ge 0$ with $\int\tilde n(\mathbf r)\,d^3r = N$, the energy functional obeys $E[\tilde n]\ge E_0$, with equality if and only if $\tilde n(\mathbf r)$ is the true ground-state density $n_0(\mathbf r)$.

MATHEMATICAL FORMULATION

By Theorem I, every valid trial density $\tilde n$ corresponds to a unique antisymmetric wavefunction $\tilde\Psi=\Psi[\tilde n]$ (in principle). The ordinary Rayleigh–Ritz variational principle for wavefunctions then gives:

$$ E_0 \le \langle\tilde\Psi|\hat H|\tilde\Psi\rangle = \langle\tilde\Psi|\hat T+\hat V_{ee}|\tilde\Psi\rangle + \int v_{ext}(\mathbf r)\,\tilde n(\mathbf r)\,d^3r = F_{HK}[\tilde n] + \int v_{ext}(\mathbf r)\,\tilde n(\mathbf r)\,d^3r \equiv E[\tilde n] $$

with equality if and only if $\tilde\Psi = \Psi_0$, i.e. $\tilde n = n_0$.

PHYSICAL MEANING

Minimizing $E[n]$ over all valid trial densities — holding $N$ fixed — simultaneously yields both the true ground-state density and the true ground-state energy, without ever constructing the many-body wavefunction. The energy functional itself acts as a compass pointing toward the correct density.

CONSEQUENCES

The many-electron ground-state problem is converted from a diagonalization in an intractable $3N$-dimensional Hilbert space (Section 2's curse of dimensionality) into a constrained minimization over a well-behaved 3-dimensional function $n(\mathbf r)$. This is precisely what makes DFT computationally practical — and precisely what the Kohn–Sham construction (Section 7) exploits, by splitting the unknown universal $F_{HK}[n]$ into a large, exactly known non-interacting kinetic piece plus a smaller, unknown exchange-correlation remainder.

Why the Ground-State Density Uniquely Determines the System

Putting the two theorems together: Theorem I shows the map $n_0(\mathbf r)\to v_{ext}(\mathbf r)$ is well defined and one-to-one (up to a trivial constant), while $N=\int n_0\,d^3r$ is read off directly from the density. Together, $v_{ext}$ and $N$ are exactly the two ingredients needed to write down the full electronic Hamiltonian $\hat H=\hat T+\hat V_{ee}+\hat V_{ext}$ from Section 3 — since $\hat T$ and $\hat V_{ee}$ never change from system to system. A fully specified $\hat H$ has a fully specified spectrum: one ground state, all excited states, and every observable that quantum mechanics can predict. That is the precise sense in which the humble 3-dimensional ground-state density $n_0(\mathbf r)$ contains, in principle, the same complete information as the full $3N$-dimensional wavefunction $\Psi(\mathbf r_1,\dots,\mathbf r_N)$ — the theoretical justification for replacing $\Psi$ with $n(\mathbf r)$ as the basic variable of electronic structure theory.

7 · Kohn–Sham Theory

Theorem II (Section 6) tells us that minimizing $E[n]=F_{HK}[n]+\int v_{ext}(\mathbf r)n(\mathbf r)\,d^3r$ yields the exact ground state — but $F_{HK}[n]=T[n]+V_{ee}[n]$ is a genuinely unknown universal functional. In particular, the exact kinetic-energy functional $T[n]$ of an interacting electron gas has no known explicit form in terms of $n(\mathbf r)$ alone. Kohn and Sham's 1965 insight sidesteps this by introducing a fictitious, mathematically convenient reference system.

KEY IDEA Replace the intractable interacting problem with an auxiliary system of non-interacting electrons moving in an effective potential $v_{eff}(\mathbf r)$, chosen so that this fictitious system reproduces the exact same ground-state density $n(\mathbf r)$ as the real, interacting system.

Animated Derivation

Step 1 / 7

The Kohn–Sham Equations

$$ \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla^2 + v_{eff}(\mathbf r)\right]\varphi_i(\mathbf r) = \varepsilon_i\,\varphi_i(\mathbf r) $$

These are formally single-particle Schrödinger equations for a set of auxiliary orbitals $\varphi_i(\mathbf r)$ — but the electron correlation of the real interacting system is not lost; it is folded entirely into $v_{eff}(\mathbf r)$.

The Effective Potential

$$ v_{eff}(\mathbf r) = \underbrace{v_{ext}(\mathbf r)}_{\text{nuclei}} \;+\; \underbrace{e^2\!\int\! \frac{n(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,d^3r'}_{v_H(\mathbf r)\ \text{Hartree}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{\delta E_{xc}[n]}{\delta n(\mathbf r)}}_{v_{xc}(\mathbf r)\ \text{exchange-correlation}} $$

Non-Interacting Reference System

The density is built from the lowest $N$ occupied auxiliary orbitals exactly as for independent particles: $n(\mathbf r)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\varphi_i(\mathbf r)|^2$. Because these orbitals are non-interacting, the kinetic energy $T_s[n]=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\sum_i\int\varphi_i^*\nabla^2\varphi_i\,d^3r$ is known exactly, unlike $T[n]$ for the real interacting system.

Exchange-Correlation Functional

All error from the two substitutions — approximating $T[n]$ by $T_s[n]$, and $V_{ee}[n]$ by the classical Hartree term — is swept into one unknown piece, $E_{xc}[n] = \big(T[n]-T_s[n]\big) + \big(V_{ee}[n]-E_H[n]\big)$. This is typically a small fraction of the total energy, which is why even simple approximations to $E_{xc}$ (LDA, GGA) give useful accuracy.

8 · Exchange-Correlation Functionals

Section 7 isolated a single unknown quantity, $E_{xc}[n]$, into which all the physics missed by the non-interacting reference system and the classical Hartree term is folded. Everything else in Kohn–Sham theory is exact. The entire practical enterprise of modern DFT is therefore the search for better approximations to $E_{xc}[n]$.

Exchange Energy

Exchange energy has no classical analogue — it is a purely quantum consequence of the Pauli exclusion principle acting on identical fermions. For a single Slater determinant of KS orbitals, the exact exchange energy is:

$$ E_x = -\frac{e^2}{2}\sum_{i,j}^{occ}\int\!\!\int \frac{\varphi_i^*(\mathbf r)\varphi_j^*(\mathbf r')\varphi_i(\mathbf r')\varphi_j(\mathbf r)}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,d^3r\,d^3r' $$

For a uniform electron gas of density $n$, this reduces to the analytically known Dirac/Slater exchange functional:

$$ E_x^{LDA}[n] = -C_x\!\int n(\mathbf r)^{4/3}\,d^3r, \qquad C_x = \frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{3}{\pi}\right)^{1/3} $$
KEY IDEA Exchange is always negative — it lowers the energy — and reflects the fact that same-spin electrons avoid each other automatically because of antisymmetry, independent of the Coulomb interaction strength.

Correlation Energy

Correlation energy captures everything beyond exchange: the additional lowering of energy from same- and opposite-spin electrons dynamically avoiding each other due to Coulomb repulsion, plus the small kinetic-energy correction $T[n]-T_s[n]$ from Section 7. It is defined simply as what remains once exchange is subtracted from the full $E_{xc}$:

$$ E_c[n] \equiv E_{xc}[n] - E_x[n] $$

Unlike $E_x$, no closed analytic form for $E_c[n]$ exists even for the uniform electron gas; modern LDA correlation functionals (e.g. Perdew–Wang, Perdew–Zunger) are parametrized fits to essentially-exact quantum Monte Carlo data.

The Exchange-Correlation Hole

Physically, $E_{xc}$ can be understood through the exchange-correlation hole $n_{xc}(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')$: the reduction, relative to the uncorrelated density, in the probability of finding a second electron at $\mathbf r'$ given that one electron is known to be at $\mathbf r$. Every electron "digs a hole" of missing charge around itself.

$$ \int n_{xc}(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')\,d^3r' = -1 \qquad\text{(exact sum rule — the hole contains exactly one missing electron)} $$
WHY LDA WORKS BETTER THAN EXPECTED LDA's exchange-correlation hole is not shaped correctly at short range, but it is spherically averaged and satisfies the exact sum rule by construction — and $E_{xc}$ itself only depends on the spherical average of the hole. This is the classic explanation for why LDA performs far better than the crudeness of the local-density assumption would suggest.

Exchange-Correlation Potential

The exchange-correlation potential entering the Kohn–Sham effective potential (Section 7) is the functional derivative of $E_{xc}[n]$ with respect to the density:

$$ v_{xc}(\mathbf r) = \frac{\delta E_{xc}[n]}{\delta n(\mathbf r)} $$
ADVANCED NOTE The exact $v_{xc}$ jumps discontinuously as the electron number $N$ crosses an integer (the "derivative discontinuity"). Most approximate functionals lack this feature, which is the root cause of DFT's well-known systematic underestimation of band gaps in semiconductors and insulators.

9 · The Self-Consistent Field (SCF) Cycle

Sections 7–8 established that the Kohn–Sham effective potential $v_{eff}(\mathbf r)$ depends on the density $n(\mathbf r)$, while $n(\mathbf r)$ itself is built from orbitals that solve the Schrödinger-like equation containing $v_{eff}$. This circular dependency — the potential needs the density, the density needs the potential — is resolved not analytically but iteratively: the self-consistent field procedure.

KEY IDEA "Self-consistent" means the loop is only finished when the density you put in to build $v_{eff}$ equals (to some tolerance) the density that comes out of solving the Kohn–Sham equations with that potential.

The SCF Loop

  • 1. Initial guess — start from a physically reasonable trial density $n^{(0)}(\mathbf r)$, e.g. a superposition of atomic densities.
  • 2. Construct the effective potential — build $v_{eff}^{(k)}(\mathbf r)=v_{ext}+v_H[n^{(k)}]+v_{xc}[n^{(k)}]$ from the current density.
  • 3. Solve the Kohn–Sham equations — diagonalize $\big[-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla^2+v_{eff}^{(k)}\big]\varphi_i=\varepsilon_i\varphi_i$ for the occupied orbitals.
  • 4. Calculate the new (output) density — $n_{out}^{(k)}(\mathbf r)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\varphi_i(\mathbf r)|^2$.
  • 5. Mix densities — blend input and output to form the next guess, $n^{(k+1)} = (1-\beta)\,n^{(k)} + \beta\,n_{out}^{(k)}$, damping oscillations that pure output-feedback would cause.
  • 6. Convergence test — check whether $|n^{(k+1)}-n^{(k)}|$ (and/or $|E^{(k+1)}-E^{(k)}|$) is below a chosen tolerance.
  • 7. Repeat — if not converged, return to step 2 with the mixed density; if converged, the loop terminates and $E[n]$, $n(\mathbf r)$, and the orbitals are the final self-consistent result.

10 · Basis Sets

The Kohn–Sham equations (Section 7) are differential equations for continuous functions $\varphi_i(\mathbf r)$. To solve them on a computer, each orbital is expanded in a finite set of known basis functions $\chi_\mu(\mathbf r)$:

$$ \varphi_i(\mathbf r) = \sum_{\mu=1}^{M} c_{i\mu}\,\chi_\mu(\mathbf r) $$

This turns a differential eigenvalue problem into a finite matrix eigenvalue problem for the coefficients $c_{i\mu}$ — but the choice of $\{\chi_\mu\}$ determines accuracy, cost, and which systems the method is well suited to. This section walks through the five major basis-set families.

Plane Waves

Plane waves $\chi_{\mathbf G}(\mathbf r) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\Omega}}e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r}$ are the natural basis for periodic solids, where $\mathbf G$ runs over reciprocal-lattice vectors and $\Omega$ is the unit-cell volume. The expansion is truncated by a kinetic-energy cutoff:

$$ \frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}|\mathbf G|^2 \le E_{cut} $$

A sharply peaked target function (schematic core-region feature) built from a truncated Fourier series. Notice how many plane waves are needed to resolve a sharp feature — this is the core weakness of plane waves near nuclei.

Gaussian-Type Orbitals (GTOs)

Gaussian basis functions $\chi(\mathbf r)\propto e^{-\alpha r^2}$ are the workhorse of molecular quantum chemistry because all required integrals (overlap, kinetic, Coulomb) have closed analytic forms — the product of two Gaussians is another Gaussian. A single Gaussian has the wrong shape at the nucleus, so several are contracted (fixed linear combination) to approximate a true atomic orbital.

RMS deviation from exact 1s orbital near r=0: –

THE CUSP PROBLEM The true hydrogenic 1s orbital has a cusp — a discontinuous derivative — at the nucleus, required exactly by the Kato cusp condition. A Gaussian is smooth ($d\chi/dr=0$ at $r=0$) and can never reproduce this cusp exactly, no matter how many are contracted together; it only gets closer in an integrated, root-mean-square sense.

Numerical Atomic Orbitals (NAOs)

Rather than fitting to an analytic form, NAOs tabulate the radial part $R(r)$ directly on a numerical grid — typically as the solution of the free-atom (or confined-atom) Kohn–Sham problem itself — multiplied by spherical harmonics for the angular part. This gives essentially unlimited shape flexibility, including the exact cusp, at the cost of needing many grid points concentrated near the nucleus.

Slater-Type Orbitals (STOs)

Slater-type orbitals $\chi(\mathbf r)\propto e^{-\zeta r}$ have exactly the correct exponential decay at large $r$ and the correct cusp at $r=0$ — physically the right shape for an atomic orbital. Their drawback is computational: unlike Gaussians, products and integrals of STOs on different centers have no simple closed form, making multi-center molecular integrals expensive. This is precisely why Gaussians (with the wrong shape but easy integrals) displaced STOs for routine molecular calculations.

Augmented Plane Waves (APW)

APW-family methods (APW, LAPW) get the best of both worlds for solids by partitioning space into non-overlapping muffin-tin spheres around each nucleus, plus the interstitial region between them. Inside each sphere, atomic-like numerical radial functions handle the rapidly-varying, cusped wavefunction near the nucleus; in the interstitial region, smooth plane waves are used, with the two pieces matched in value (and, in LAPW, derivative) at the sphere boundary.

Left/top: schematic real-space partition — atomic (numerical) regions inside the spheres, plane-wave region outside. Bottom: the matching of the two representations along a line through the sphere boundary at $r=R_{MT}$.

Computational Tradeoffs

CHOOSING A BASIS Molecular quantum chemistry overwhelmingly favors Gaussians for their integral speed; periodic solid-state DFT overwhelmingly favors plane waves for their systematic, uniform-accuracy simplicity; high-precision all-electron solid-state work favors (L)APW or NAO methods that handle the near-nucleus region exactly. There is no universally best basis — only the best basis for a given system and property.

11 · Numerical Methods Behind a DFT Calculation

Every visual and equation in Sections 7–10 ultimately becomes a linear-algebra or optimization problem that has to run on real hardware in finite time. This section surveys the numerical machinery — some exact, some iterative — that makes a practical DFT code possible.

Matrix Diagonalization

Expanding the Kohn–Sham orbitals in a finite basis (Section 10) turns $\big[-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla^2+v_{eff}\big]\varphi_i=\varepsilon_i\varphi_i$ into a generalized matrix eigenvalue problem $H\mathbf c_i = \varepsilon_i S\mathbf c_i$, where $H_{\mu\nu}=\langle\chi_\mu|\hat H_{KS}|\chi_\nu\rangle$ and $S_{\mu\nu}=\langle\chi_\mu|\chi_\nu\rangle$ is the (generally non-orthogonal) overlap matrix.

$$ H\mathbf c_i = \varepsilon_i S\mathbf c_i \qquad \text{(size } M\times M\text{, } M=\text{number of basis functions)} $$

Direct (dense) diagonalization — e.g. Householder reduction to tridiagonal form followed by the QR algorithm — is exact up to floating-point precision and scales as $\mathcal O(M^3)$, which dominates the cost of small-to-medium calculations.

Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)

Plane-wave codes (Section 10) constantly convert quantities between real space $n(\mathbf r)$ and reciprocal space $n(\mathbf G)$ — e.g. to apply the kinetic energy operator (diagonal in $\mathbf G$-space) and the local potential (diagonal in real space) within the same iteration. Direct discrete Fourier transformation costs $\mathcal O(M^2)$; the FFT computes the identical result by recursive divide-and-conquer in $\mathcal O(M\log M)$.

$$ n(\mathbf G) = \sum_{\mathbf r} n(\mathbf r)\,e^{-i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r} \quad\xrightarrow{\text{FFT}}\quad \mathcal O(M\log M)\ \text{instead of}\ \mathcal O(M^2) $$

Poisson Solvers

The Hartree potential $v_H(\mathbf r)$ (Section 7) satisfies Poisson's equation $\nabla^2 v_H(\mathbf r) = -4\pi e^2 n(\mathbf r)$. In a plane-wave basis this is solved algebraically in one shot — Poisson's equation becomes diagonal in reciprocal space:

$$ v_H(\mathbf G) = \frac{4\pi e^2}{|\mathbf G|^2}\,n(\mathbf G) \qquad (\mathbf G\neq 0) $$

so $v_H(\mathbf r)$ is obtained by FFT to $\mathbf G$-space, dividing by $|\mathbf G|^2$, and FFT-ing back — no iteration required. Real-space codes instead solve the Poisson equation iteratively (e.g. multigrid or successive over-relaxation, SOR) directly on a spatial grid.

Density Mixing

Covered in depth in Section 9 — linear mixing $n^{(k+1)}=(1-\beta)n^{(k)}+\beta\,n_{out}^{(k)}$ damps the oscillations that direct output-feedback would otherwise cause. It is simple but converges slowly and needs a carefully tuned $\beta$.

Pulay Mixing (DIIS)

Direct Inversion in the Iterative Subspace (Pulay, 1980) improves on linear mixing by using a short history of past densities $\{n^{(i)}\}$ and their residuals $\{R^{(i)}=n_{out}^{(i)}-n^{(i)}\}$, and solving a small linear system for mixing coefficients $\{c_i\}$ that minimize the norm of the extrapolated residual subject to $\sum_i c_i=1$:

$$ n^{(k+1)} = \sum_{i} c_i\, n_{out}^{(i)}, \qquad \text{chosen so that}\ \left\|\sum_i c_i R^{(i)}\right\|\ \text{is minimized} $$

This typically converges in far fewer cycles than fixed-$\beta$ linear mixing, especially for systems prone to charge "sloshing."

Davidson Algorithm

For large basis sets, forming and fully diagonalizing $H$ directly ($\mathcal O(M^3)$) is prohibitive when only the lowest $N\ll M$ eigenpairs are needed. The Davidson algorithm builds a small trial subspace, diagonalizes the tiny projected matrix exactly, and iteratively enlarges the subspace using preconditioned residuals — converging the wanted eigenpairs without ever forming the full $H$ explicitly.

Conjugate Gradient (CG)

Conjugate gradient minimizes a quadratic (or quadratic-like) function far more efficiently than plain steepest descent, by choosing successive search directions that are conjugate with respect to the underlying curvature — avoiding the characteristic "zig-zagging" of steepest descent in narrow, elongated valleys. It is used both to solve linear systems (e.g. within iterative Poisson/eigensolvers) and to directly minimize the KS energy functional with respect to orbital coefficients.

BFGS Optimization

The Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) algorithm is a quasi-Newton method: rather than computing the true (expensive) Hessian, it builds up an approximate inverse Hessian from successive gradient evaluations, giving Newton-like convergence at gradient-only cost. In DFT it is the standard workhorse for geometry (structural) relaxation — minimizing the total energy $E(\mathbf R)$ over nuclear coordinates on the potential energy surface (Section 4).

12 · The Total Energy — Every Contribution

Sections 7–9 built up the Kohn–Sham machinery term by term. This section assembles the complete total-energy expression for a real (not auxiliary) system of interacting electrons and fixed nuclei, and lets you explore how each physical contribution adds up to the final number a DFT calculation reports.

$$ E_{total} = T_s[n] \;+\; E_{ext}[n] \;+\; E_H[n] \;+\; E_x[n] \;+\; E_c[n] \;+\; E_{II} $$
KEY IDEA Every term here is a well-defined piece of physics with a specific sign and physical origin. Some lower the energy (kinetic, exchange, correlation are stabilizing in different senses), some raise it (nuclear repulsion), and some can go either way depending on the system (external potential, Hartree). The DFT total energy is their sum — never any one term in isolation.

1 · Kinetic Energy $T_s[n]$

The kinetic energy of the non-interacting Kohn–Sham reference system (Section 7) — computed exactly from the occupied orbitals:

$$ T_s[n] = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int \varphi_i^*(\mathbf r)\,\nabla^2\varphi_i(\mathbf r)\,d^3r $$

Sign/character: always positive. Physical origin: confinement of electrons in bound orbitals (larger for tightly bound core electrons, smaller for diffuse valence states). The small correction $T[n]-T_s[n]$ needed to reach the true interacting kinetic energy is absorbed into $E_c[n]$.

2 · External Potential Energy $E_{ext}[n]$

The electrostatic attraction between the electron density and the fixed nuclei (Section 3's $\hat V_{ext}$, evaluated on the density):

$$ E_{ext}[n] = \int v_{ext}(\mathbf r)\,n(\mathbf r)\,d^3r, \qquad v_{ext}(\mathbf r) = -\sum_I \frac{Z_I e^2}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf R_I|} $$

Sign/character: always negative (attractive). Physical origin: Coulomb attraction of negatively charged electrons to positively charged nuclei — typically the single largest-magnitude term in the total energy.

3 · Hartree Energy $E_H[n]$

The classical electrostatic self-repulsion of the electron charge cloud, treating $n(\mathbf r)$ as if it were a classical charge distribution:

$$ E_H[n] = \frac{e^2}{2}\int\!\!\int \frac{n(\mathbf r)\,n(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,d^3r\,d^3r' $$

Sign/character: always positive (repulsive). Physical origin: pure classical Coulomb repulsion between electron density at $\mathbf r$ and $\mathbf r'$. Note this overcounts repulsion — it includes a spurious "self-interaction" of each electron with itself, which is corrected (imperfectly, in approximate functionals) by $E_{xc}$.

4 · Exchange Energy $E_x[n]$

The Pauli-principle correction that keeps same-spin electrons apart, lowering the energy below the naive Hartree estimate (Section 8):

$$ E_x[n] \le 0 $$

Sign/character: always negative (stabilizing). Physical origin: antisymmetry of the many-electron wavefunction under exchange of identical fermions — a purely quantum-statistical effect, present even for non-interacting particles.

5 · Correlation Energy $E_c[n]$

Everything beyond exchange: dynamical avoidance of opposite-spin (and residual same-spin) electrons due to instantaneous Coulomb repulsion, plus the kinetic-energy correction $T[n]-T_s[n]$ (Section 8):

$$ E_c[n] \le 0 \quad\text{(typically)} $$

Sign/character: usually negative, and usually much smaller in magnitude than $E_x[n]$. Physical origin: electron correlation beyond the mean-field/Hartree-Fock-like picture — the hardest part of $E_{xc}$ to approximate accurately, and the primary target of GGA/meta-GGA/hybrid functional development (Section 8).

6 · Nuclear Repulsion $E_{II}$

The classical Coulomb repulsion between the fixed nuclei themselves — a constant for any given nuclear geometry $\mathbf R$ (Section 4), entirely independent of the electron density:

$$ E_{II} = \frac{e^2}{2}\sum_{I\neq J} \frac{Z_I Z_J}{|\mathbf R_I - \mathbf R_J|} $$

Sign/character: always positive (repulsive). Physical origin: pure classical electrostatics between point charges — no quantum mechanics involved. This term is what makes molecules and solids favor spreading nuclei apart, balanced against $E_{ext}[n]$ pulling electron density (and hence indirectly the nuclei) together.

13 · Geometry Optimization

From the Potential Energy Surface to Equilibrium Structures

Section 4 introduced the potential energy surface $E_{elec}(\mathbf R)$ traced out by solving the electronic problem at many fixed nuclear geometries. Geometry optimization is the numerical search for the point(s) on this surface where the nuclei experience zero net force — the equilibrium structure(s) of the molecule or solid.

Atomic Forces

The force on nucleus $I$ is minus the gradient of the total energy with respect to that nucleus's position:

$$ \mathbf F_I = -\nabla_{\mathbf R_I} E(\mathbf R) $$

A structure sits at a stationary point of the PES — an equilibrium geometry, a transition state, or a higher-order saddle — precisely when $\mathbf F_I=0$ for every nucleus $I$ simultaneously. A local minimum additionally requires the Hessian $\partial^2E/\partial R_I\partial R_J$ to be positive semi-definite (no imaginary vibrational frequencies).

The Hellmann–Feynman Theorem

STATEMENT

For a Hamiltonian $\hat H(\lambda)$ depending on a parameter $\lambda$ (here, a nuclear coordinate $\mathbf R_I$), with normalized eigenstate $\Psi(\lambda)$ of eigenvalue $E(\lambda)$:

$$ \frac{\partial E}{\partial \lambda} = \left\langle \Psi \right| \frac{\partial \hat H}{\partial \lambda} \left| \Psi \right\rangle $$
DERIVATION

Differentiating $E(\lambda)=\langle\Psi|\hat H|\Psi\rangle$ with respect to $\lambda$ gives three terms:

$$ \frac{\partial E}{\partial \lambda} = \left\langle\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\lambda}\right|\hat H\left|\Psi\right\rangle + \left\langle\Psi\right|\hat H\left|\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\lambda}\right\rangle + \left\langle\Psi\right|\frac{\partial\hat H}{\partial\lambda}\left|\Psi\right\rangle $$

Since $\hat H|\Psi\rangle=E|\Psi\rangle$, the first two terms combine to $E\,\partial_\lambda\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle$, which vanishes because normalization $\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle=1$ holds for every $\lambda$. Only the explicit-dependence term survives.

APPLICATION TO NUCLEAR FORCES

Taking $\lambda=\mathbf R_I$: only the electron–nucleus attraction and nuclear–nuclear repulsion terms of $\hat H$ (Section 3) depend explicitly on nuclear position — $\hat T$ and $\hat V_{ee}$ do not. So:

$$ \mathbf F_I = -\left\langle\Psi\right|\frac{\partial \hat V_{ext}}{\partial \mathbf R_I}\left|\Psi\right\rangle \;-\; \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf R_I}\sum_{J\neq I}\frac{Z_IZ_Je^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0|\mathbf R_I-\mathbf R_J|} $$

Because $\hat V_{ext}$ is one-body and built from $n(\mathbf r)=\langle\Psi|\hat n(\mathbf r)|\Psi\rangle$ (Section 5), this is an entirely classical electrostatic force: the nucleus feels the Coulomb pull of the electron density treated as a continuous charge cloud, plus ordinary point-charge repulsion from the other nuclei.

WHY THIS MATTERS The force is an expectation value evaluated at the current $\Psi$ — it does not require differentiating $\Psi$ itself, even though $\Psi$ depends on $\mathbf R_I$ too; the $\partial\Psi/\partial\lambda$ contributions cancel exactly. This is what makes analytic-gradient optimization affordable: one converged electronic-structure calculation yields the energy and every atomic force at once, instead of a separate finite-difference calculation for each of the $3M$ nuclear coordinates.
SUBTLETY Hellmann–Feynman is exact only for an exact eigenstate, or — for variational methods like Hartree–Fock or Kohn–Sham DFT in a fixed basis — when the basis functions don't move with the nuclei. Real codes use atom-centered basis sets, so an extra term (the Pulay force) must be added to account for the basis set's own $\mathbf R_I$-dependence.

Energy Minimization Algorithms

Steepest Descent

Move every atom directly along its force, $\mathbf R_I \leftarrow \mathbf R_I + \gamma\,\mathbf F_I$. Simple and guaranteed to lower the energy for small enough $\gamma$, but converges slowly and zig-zags in narrow, curved valleys of the PES.

Conjugate Gradient

Chooses each step direction conjugate to previous search directions rather than the raw force, avoiding steepest descent's zig-zag. No explicit Hessian needed; converges considerably faster on typical PES shapes.

Quasi-Newton (BFGS)

Builds an approximate Hessian from successive energy/force evaluations and takes Newton-like steps $\Delta\mathbf R=-\mathbf H^{-1}\mathbf F$. The default optimizer in most quantum chemistry and materials codes for well-behaved minima.

FIRE

"Fast Inertial Relaxation Engine" — damped molecular dynamics with adaptive time step and velocity mixing. Popular in large-scale materials and force-field codes for robustness and low memory cost.

Structural Relaxation in Practice

A geometry optimization loop repeats: (1) solve the electronic problem at the current $\mathbf R$ — e.g. one Kohn–Sham SCF cycle — to get $E(\mathbf R)$ and, via Hellmann–Feynman, every $\mathbf F_I$; (2) hand $E$ and $\{\mathbf F_I\}$ to an optimizer, which proposes a new $\mathbf R$; (3) repeat until $\max_I|\mathbf F_I|$ drops below a threshold (commonly $10^{-2}$–$10^{-3}$ eV/Å in production DFT codes). 14 · Band Structure

Sections 1–13 treated a single molecule with a discrete, finite set of orbitals. In a crystal, translational periodicity replaces "discrete orbitals" with "continuous bands" — the subject of this section. Everything here is a single-electron picture (Bloch/nearly-free-electron theory); DFT enters only insofar as the Kohn–Sham potential is itself periodic, so Kohn–Sham eigenvalues $\varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}$ organize into bands in exactly the way described below.

Reciprocal Space

For a 1D crystal with lattice constant $a$, the real-space lattice is the set of points $x = ma$, $m\in\mathbb Z$. The reciprocal lattice is the set of wavevectors $G_n$ for which a plane wave $e^{iG_nx}$ has the same periodicity as the lattice:

$$ G_n = \frac{2\pi n}{a}, \qquad n\in\mathbb Z $$

Bloch's theorem states that any eigenstate of a Hamiltonian with a periodic potential $V(x+a)=V(x)$ can be written as a plane wave modulated by a function with the lattice's periodicity:

$$ \psi_k(x) = e^{ikx}\,u_k(x), \qquad u_k(x+a) = u_k(x) $$

The label $k$ — the crystal momentum — is only defined modulo a reciprocal lattice vector: $\psi_{k+G_n}(x)$ describes the same physical state as $\psi_k(x)$, just with $G_n$'s oscillation absorbed into $u_k(x)$. This redundancy is what makes it possible to restrict attention to a single finite window of $k$.

The Brillouin Zone

KEY IDEA The first Brillouin zone is the set of $k$ values closest to $k=0$ — the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice. In 1D this is simply $-\pi/a < k \le \pi/a$. Because of the modulo-$G_n$ redundancy above, every physically distinct state is captured by a $k$ inside this single interval; this is the reduced zone scheme used throughout this section (as opposed to the "extended zone scheme," which instead plots the unfolded free-electron parabola across all of $k$-space).

The center of the zone, $k=0$, is conventionally labeled $\Gamma$ (Gamma); the zone boundary $k=\pm\pi/a$ is the point where Bragg reflection off the periodic lattice is strongest, and — as shown below — where the periodic potential's effect on the energy spectrum is largest.

Electronic Bands

For each allowed $k$ in the Brillouin zone, the periodic Schrödinger equation $\hat H_k u_k = E_n(k)\,u_k$ (with $\hat H_k = \frac{1}{2m}(-i\hbar\partial_x+\hbar k)^2 + V(x)$) has a discrete, ordered set of eigenvalues $E_1(k) < E_2(k) < E_3(k) < \cdots$, indexed by the band index $n$. Plotting $E_n(k)$ as a continuous function of $k$ for each $n$, across the Brillouin zone, produces the band structure — the single most information-dense diagram in solid-state electronic structure theory. Where the periodic potential is weak, bands resemble the free-electron parabola $E=\hbar^2k^2/2m$ folded into the zone; where the potential is strong, gaps open wherever Bragg reflection couples $k$ and $k-G_n$ states of equal energy — generically at the zone boundary and zone center.

Direct and Indirect Gaps

Direct Gap

If the valence band maximum (VBM) and conduction band minimum (CBM) occur at the same $k$, the gap is direct. An electron can absorb a single photon (which carries negligible crystal momentum on the scale of $\pi/a$) and jump straight from VBM to CBM — the basis of efficient LEDs and laser diodes (e.g. GaAs).

Indirect Gap

If the VBM and CBM occur at different $k$, the gap is indirect. Optical absorption/emission across the gap requires a phonon (lattice vibration) to supply the missing crystal momentum, making the process far less probable — why silicon, an indirect-gap semiconductor, is a poor light emitter despite dominating electronics.

Metals, Semiconductors, and Insulators

Metal

A band is only partially filled, or two bands overlap in energy with no gap at the Fermi level. Electrons arbitrarily close to the highest occupied state can be excited into unoccupied states with an infinitesimal energy input — giving high electrical conductivity even at $T=0$.

Semiconductor

Bands are completely filled up to a gap, but the gap is small enough (conventionally $\lesssim 4$ eV, e.g. Si: 1.1 eV, GaAs: 1.4 eV) that thermal excitation or modest photon energies promote a non-negligible number of carriers across it at room temperature.

Insulator

Topologically identical to a semiconductor — filled bands separated by a gap — but the gap is large (e.g. diamond: 5.5 eV, SiO$_2$: ~9 eV), so thermal excitation across it is negligible and the material does not conduct under ordinary conditions.

CAVEAT The metal/semiconductor/insulator threshold at "~4 eV" is a convention, not a sharp physical boundary, and real 3D materials can have multiple overlapping bands with different dispersions along different crystallographic directions — a full classification requires knowing the actual electron count and 3D Fermi surface, not just a single 1D gap value. 15 · Density of States

Section 14 organized single-electron eigenvalues $E_n(k)$ into bands across the Brillouin zone. The density of states (DOS) repackages that same information into a single function of energy alone — collapsing away the $k$-dependence entirely — and is the direct link between band structure and thermodynamic/spectroscopic quantities: electronic specific heat, optical absorption onset, X-ray photoemission spectra, and the Fermi level itself all follow from $g(E)$.

Definition of the DOS

$$ g(E) = \frac{2}{N_k}\sum_{n}\sum_{k}\delta\big(E-E_n(k)\big) $$

Here the sum runs over all bands $n$ and all $k$-points sampled in the Brillouin zone, $N_k$ is the number of $k$-points, and the factor of 2 accounts for spin degeneracy. $g(E)\,dE$ is the number of electronic states per unit cell (per spin-summed) lying in the energy window $[E, E+dE]$.

KEY IDEA The DOS is exactly what you'd get by taking the band structure plot from Section 14, rotating it 90°, and counting how many bands cross each horizontal energy slice — dense band crossings produce a high $g(E)$; flat, widely separated bands produce a low one. A true $\delta$-function DOS is a distribution, not a plotted curve, so in practice every DOS plot — including the one below — replaces each $\delta(E-E_n(k))$ with a finite-width broadening function.

Gaussian Broadening

The most common numerical approximation replaces each delta function with a normalized Gaussian of width $\sigma$:

$$ \delta(E-E_i) \;\approx\; \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(E-E_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right] $$

Small $\sigma$ preserves fine structure (van Hove singularities — the sharp peaks/kinks that occur wherever $dE_n/dk=0$, i.e. at band extrema) but is noisy unless the $k$-point sampling is very dense; large $\sigma$ smooths the curve into something visually cleaner but can wash out real spectral features, including narrow gaps. Choosing $\sigma$ is a genuine numerical trade-off in every real DFT calculation, not just a cosmetic one — the slider below lets you feel that trade-off directly.

Projected Density of States (PDOS)

The total DOS $g(E)$ answers "how many states exist at energy $E$?" but not "what are those states made of?" The projected (or partial) DOS answers the second question by decomposing $g(E)$ into contributions from a chosen subset of the Hilbert space — most commonly atomic orbitals on specific atoms:

$$ g_\alpha(E) = \frac{2}{N_k}\sum_{n,k}\big|\langle\phi_\alpha|\psi_{nk}\rangle\big|^2\,\delta\big(E-E_n(k)\big), \qquad g(E)=\sum_\alpha g_\alpha(E) $$

where $\phi_\alpha$ is an atomic-orbital-like basis function (via a Mulliken or Löwdin population analysis) and $|\langle\phi_\alpha|\psi_{nk}\rangle|^2$ is the weight of orbital $\alpha$ in Bloch state $\psi_{nk}$. PDOS is the standard tool for reading off chemical character directly from a band-structure calculation — e.g. showing that a metal oxide's valence band is dominated by oxygen $2p$ character while its conduction band is dominated by the metal's $d$ states.

SIMPLIFICATION IN THIS DEMO The 1D Kronig–Penney model used here has no atomic orbital basis to project onto, so the "PDOS" panel below decomposes $g(E)$ by band index instead of atomic character — $g(E)=\sum_n g_n(E)$. This preserves the essential idea (the total DOS is a sum of identifiable, separately-plottable contributions) without pretending to a chemical resolution the model doesn't have.

The Fermi Level

At zero temperature, electrons fill available single-particle states from the lowest energy upward, one pair (spin-up/spin-down) per state, until all $N_e$ electrons in the cell are placed. The Fermi level (or Fermi energy) $E_F$ is the energy of the highest occupied state — equivalently, the value satisfying:

$$ N_e = \int_{-\infty}^{E_F} g(E)\,dE $$
  • Metal: $E_F$ falls inside a band, where $g(E_F)>0$ — there are states available arbitrarily close to $E_F$ on both sides, enabling conduction at any temperature $T>0$.
  • Semiconductor / insulator: $E_F$ falls inside a gap, where $g(E_F)=0$ — no states exist exactly at $E_F$; conduction requires thermally or optically promoting electrons across the finite gap above it, exactly as introduced in Section 14.

16 · Charge Density

Sections 1–15 built up the theoretical machinery — density as fundamental variable (Section 5), the energy functional (Section 6), forces on nuclei (Section 13), and periodic band structure (Sections 14–15). This section returns to $n(\mathbf r)$ itself as a 3D object and asks how chemists and materials scientists actually read it: not as a single spherically-averaged plot, but as a full 3D field visualized via slices and isosurfaces, and decomposed into total, difference, and localization pictures.

Total Density

The total density $n(\mathbf r)$ (Section 5) is a single non-negative scalar field over all space. For a molecule it typically looks like two or more roughly spherical, exponentially-decaying atomic cores connected by lower-amplitude "bridges" of density in bonding regions. Plotted as an isosurface — the set of points where $n(\mathbf r)$ equals some fixed value — a high isovalue traces out compact blobs around each nucleus, while progressively lower isovalues reveal the diffuse valence and bonding regions merging the separate atoms into one connected surface.

Difference Density

The total density alone is dominated visually by core electrons and reveals bonding effects only subtly. The standard remedy is the charge-density difference:

$$ \Delta n(\mathbf r) = n_{mol}(\mathbf r) - n_{promolecule}(\mathbf r) $$

where $n_{promolecule}(\mathbf r)$ is the promolecule density — a reference built by simply superposing the densities of neutral, isolated, spherical atoms placed at the same nuclear positions, with no bonding or charge transfer allowed. Because both $n_{mol}$ and $n_{promolecule}$ integrate to the same total electron count, $\Delta n(\mathbf r)$ integrates to exactly zero overall — it only ever redistributes charge, never creates or destroys it.

KEY IDEA $\Delta n(\mathbf r) > 0$ marks regions that gained electron density upon bond formation (typically the bonding region between nuclei); $\Delta n(\mathbf r) < 0$ marks regions that lost density (typically just outside the atomic cores, along the bond axis, as valence density is pulled inward toward the bond). This accumulation/depletion pattern is the standard visual signature of covalent bond formation.

Charge Transfer

For a heteronuclear bond between atoms of different electronegativity, $\Delta n(\mathbf r)$ is asymmetric: the more electronegative atom shows net accumulation in its own basin, the less electronegative atom shows net depletion. Integrating $\Delta n(\mathbf r)$ separately over the region "belonging" to each atom gives an estimate of the net charge transferred:

$$ \Delta Q_\alpha = \int_{\Omega_\alpha} \Delta n(\mathbf r)\, d^3r $$
SIMPLIFICATION Rigorous approaches (Bader's Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules) define each atomic basin $\Omega_\alpha$ by the true zero-flux surfaces of $\nabla n(\mathbf r)$. The simulator below instead uses a simple planar bisector between the two nuclei as a stand-in boundary — adequate for a qualitative charge-transfer estimate in this toy diatomic, but not a substitute for a real Bader analysis.

Electron Localization

Total and difference density show where charge is, but not whether it behaves like a shared covalent pair, a lone pair, or a delocalized electron gas. The Becke–Edgecombe electron localization function (ELF) answers this using the excess local kinetic energy relative to a uniform electron gas of the same density — computed from the actual Kohn–Sham orbitals, not from $n(\mathbf r)$ alone. ELF is bounded in $[0,1]$: values near 1 indicate strongly localized pairs (bond pairs, lone pairs, core shells); values near 0.5 resemble a uniform electron gas (typical of delocalized metallic bonding).

SIMPLIFICATION IN THIS DEMO True ELF requires orbital-level kinetic energy densities that this section's simple analytic density model does not have. The "ELF" panel below instead uses an illustrative reduced-density-gradient proxy, $\text{ELF}_{toy}=1/(1+\chi^2)$ with $\chi \propto |\nabla n|/n$ — high wherever the density is smooth and slowly varying relative to its own magnitude (e.g. right at a symmetric bond midpoint, where two atoms' density gradients cancel), low where the density changes rapidly. It reproduces the qualitative shape of real ELF bonding basins but is not a substitute for the rigorous orbital-based quantity.

17 · Spin Density Functional Theory

Every section so far has used a single, spin-summed density $n(\mathbf r)$. That is sufficient for closed-shell, non-magnetic systems, but it cannot describe unpaired electrons, magnetism, or any system where spin-up and spin-down electrons occupy space differently. Spin-DFT (von Barth & Hedin, 1972) generalizes the Hohenberg–Kohn/Kohn–Sham framework (Sections 6–7) to two coupled densities.

Spin Polarization

Instead of one density, spin-DFT works with two: $n_\uparrow(\mathbf r)$ and $n_\downarrow(\mathbf r)$, the densities of spin-up and spin-down electrons separately. The total (charge) density and the spin density are:

$$ n(\mathbf r) = n_\uparrow(\mathbf r) + n_\downarrow(\mathbf r), \qquad m(\mathbf r) = n_\uparrow(\mathbf r) - n_\downarrow(\mathbf r) $$

The Kohn–Sham equations split into two coupled channels, each with its own effective potential — because the exchange-correlation potential depends on both densities, not just the total:

$$ \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla^2 + v_{eff}^{\sigma}(\mathbf r)\right]\varphi_{i\sigma}(\mathbf r) = \varepsilon_{i\sigma}\,\varphi_{i\sigma}(\mathbf r), \qquad \sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow $$

with $v_{eff}^{\sigma}(\mathbf r) = v_{ext}(\mathbf r) + v_H[n](\mathbf r) + v_{xc}^{\sigma}[n_\uparrow,n_\downarrow](\mathbf r)$. Only the exchange-correlation piece distinguishes the two spin channels; the external and Hartree potentials depend only on the total density $n(\mathbf r)$.

FRACTIONAL SPIN POLARIZATION A convenient dimensionless measure is $\zeta(\mathbf r) = m(\mathbf r)/n(\mathbf r)$, ranging from $\zeta=0$ (perfectly unpolarized, $n_\uparrow=n_\downarrow$, the ordinary spin-restricted case) to $\zeta=\pm1$ (fully polarized, only one spin channel occupied at that point).

Magnetic Moments

Integrating the spin density gives the total magnetic moment of the system, in units of the Bohr magneton $\mu_B$:

$$ M = \mu_B \int m(\mathbf r)\,d^3r = \mu_B\,(N_\uparrow - N_\downarrow) $$

For an isolated atom, Hund's first rule — maximize total spin $S$ before pairing electrons in the same orbital — is a direct, if approximate, consequence of exchange energy (Section 8): parallel-spin electrons avoid each other via the Pauli principle, lowering $E_x$, which spin-DFT captures naturally once $v_{xc}^\uparrow \ne v_{xc}^\downarrow$.

Ferromagnetism

In a ferromagnet (e.g. bcc iron, fcc nickel), the exchange interaction favors parallel alignment of neighboring magnetic moments throughout the crystal, producing a net macroscopic magnetization even with no applied field, below the material's Curie temperature. In spin-DFT terms: the exchange-correlation energy is lowered more by having $n_\uparrow(\mathbf r) \neq n_\downarrow(\mathbf r)$ consistently in the same sense across all atomic sites than by remaining unpolarized.

Antiferromagnetism

In an antiferromagnet (e.g. NiO, Cr metal), neighboring magnetic moments align anti-parallel, alternating sign from site to site. The net magnetization is zero, but each site still carries a nonzero local moment — spin-DFT still requires two spin channels and a spatially varying $m(\mathbf r)$, even though the volume-integrated $\int m(\mathbf r)\,d^3r$ vanishes.

WHY THIS MATTERS COMPUTATIONALLY A spin-unpolarized (single-density) DFT calculation cannot distinguish a ferromagnet, an antiferromagnet, or a non-magnetic metal — all three would be forced to have $n_\uparrow=n_\downarrow$ everywhere. Only spin-DFT, allowing $n_\uparrow(\mathbf r)$ and $n_\downarrow(\mathbf r)$ to differ locally and by site, can capture any of this physics at all.

18 · Time-Dependent Density Functional Theory

Every preceding section worked with a single, static ground-state density $n(\mathbf r)$. But absorption spectra, photochemistry, and any process driven by a time-varying field — a laser pulse, an oscillating dipole — require following the density as it evolves in time, $n(\mathbf r,t)$. Time-Dependent DFT (TDDFT) extends the Hohenberg–Kohn/Kohn–Sham framework (Sections 6–7) to exactly this setting.

The Runge–Gross Theorem

STATEMENT

For a fixed initial state $\Psi(t_0)$, two time-dependent external potentials $v_{ext}(\mathbf r,t)$ and $v_{ext}'(\mathbf r,t)$ that differ by more than a purely time-dependent function $c(t)$ produce, after $t_0$, two different time-dependent densities $n(\mathbf r,t)\neq n'(\mathbf r,t)$ (Runge & Gross, 1984).

PHYSICAL MEANING

This is the time-domain analogue of Hohenberg–Kohn Theorem I (Section 6): $n(\mathbf r,t)$ determines $v_{ext}(\mathbf r,t)$ uniquely (up to $c(t)$), and hence — given the fixed initial state — every observable of the evolving system. $n(\mathbf r,t)$ is therefore, in principle, as complete a description of the dynamics as the full time-dependent wavefunction $\Psi(\mathbf r_1,\dots,\mathbf r_N,t)$.

CAVEAT Unlike Hohenberg–Kohn Theorem II, there is no simple energy-minimization principle in the time domain — action-functional formulations exist but are more subtle than the static variational principle of Section 6.

The Time-Dependent Kohn–Sham Equations

As in Section 7, the interacting time-dependent problem is mapped onto a fictitious non-interacting system evolved under an effective potential chosen to reproduce the exact $n(\mathbf r,t)$ at every instant:

$$ i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varphi_i(\mathbf r,t) = \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\nabla^2 + v_{eff}(\mathbf r,t)\right]\varphi_i(\mathbf r,t), \qquad n(\mathbf r,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\varphi_i(\mathbf r,t)|^2 $$
$$ v_{eff}(\mathbf r,t) = v_{ext}(\mathbf r,t) + \underbrace{e^2\!\int\!\frac{n(\mathbf r',t)}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}d^3r'}_{v_H(\mathbf r,t)} + v_{xc}[n](\mathbf r,t) $$
ADIABATIC APPROXIMATION The exact $v_{xc}[n](\mathbf r,t)$ depends on the density's entire history — a "memory" effect. Nearly all practical TDDFT plugs the instantaneous density into an ordinary ground-state $v_{xc}$ functional (Section 8), $v_{xc}^{adia}[n](\mathbf r,t)\equiv v_{xc}^{static}[n(t)](\mathbf r)$. This discards memory but works well for a wide range of excitations.

Linear Response TDDFT

Excitation energies and optical spectra are usually extracted from the response of the ground-state density to an infinitesimally weak perturbation $\delta v_{ext}(\mathbf r,t)$:

$$ \delta n(\mathbf r,\omega) = \int \chi(\mathbf r,\mathbf r',\omega)\,\delta v_{ext}(\mathbf r',\omega)\,d^3r' $$

The density-density response function $\chi(\mathbf r,\mathbf r',\omega)$ has poles exactly at the true many-body excitation energies $\Omega_I$. TDDFT constructs $\chi$ from Kohn–Sham quantities via a Dyson-like equation:

$$ \chi = \chi_s + \chi_s\Big[\frac{e^2}{|\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2|}+f_{xc}\Big]\chi $$

where $\chi_s$ is the exactly known non-interacting KS response function (Section 7) and $f_{xc}=\delta v_{xc}/\delta n$ is the exchange-correlation kernel — the time-dependent generalization of $E_{xc}$'s functional derivative (Section 8).

The Casida Equations

Casida (1995) recast linear response as a matrix eigenvalue problem over KS orbital transitions $a\to b$:

$$ \sum_{jb}\Big[\delta_{ia,jb}\,\omega_{ia}^2 + 4\sqrt{\omega_{ia}}\,K_{ia,jb}\,\sqrt{\omega_{jb}}\Big]\,F_{jb}^{I} = \Omega_I^2\,F_{ia}^{I} $$

Here $\omega_{ia}=\varepsilon_a-\varepsilon_i$ are bare KS orbital-energy differences (Section 7), and $K_{ia,jb}$ is built from the Hartree kernel and $f_{xc}$. The eigenvalues $\Omega_I^2$ are the squared true excitation energies; the eigenvectors give oscillator strengths.

WHY THIS MATTERS Bare KS energy differences $\omega_{ia}$ are usually poor approximations to true excitations — the coupling matrix is what corrects them. The toy model below builds exactly this kind of matrix and diagonalizes it live.

Real-Time Propagation TDDFT

An alternative to Casida: apply a brief impulsive "kick" to the ground-state density, propagate the TDKS equations forward, and record the induced dipole $\mu(t)$. A delta-like kick excites all frequencies at once, so a single Fourier transform of $\mu(t)$ recovers the whole spectrum in one propagation instead of one diagonalization.

SAME PHYSICS, TWO ROUTES Casida and real-time propagation solve the same linearized TDKS equations but scale differently: Casida's cost grows with the number of KS transitions (good for a few low-lying excitations); real-time propagation's cost grows with simulation time (better for large systems or broadband spectra).

A full 1D real-space Kohn–Sham SCF solver (Section 7) for a soft-Coulomb "1D atom": finite-difference kinetic operator, self-consistent Hartree potential, Dirac/Slater LDA exchange, linear density mixing.

3 · Finite Difference DFT

.py finite_difference_dft.py

The real-space numerical machinery underlying Section 11: building a finite-difference Laplacian (3-point and 5-point stencils) and solving the discretized Poisson equation for the Hartree potential via a tridiagonal solve.

4 · Plane-Wave Implementation

.py plane_wave_dft.py

A 1D periodic plane-wave Hamiltonian (Section 10/14): kinetic energy diagonal in reciprocal space, a periodic potential coupling different plane waves, diagonalized at each k-point to build a band structure.

5 · Density Mixing

.py density_mixing.py

Reusable LinearMixer and PulayMixer (DIIS) classes (Sections 9 & 11), benchmarked against each other on the same toy nonlinear fixed-point map.

6 · Geometry Optimization

.py geometry_optimization.py

Relaxing a Morse-potential diatomic (Section 13) via steepest descent and via BFGS (scipy.optimize.minimize), using an analytic Hellmann–Feynman-style force, with a convergence comparison.

7 · Band Structure Calculation

.py band_structure.py

Kronig–Penney band structure (Section 14) via root-finding on the transcendental dispersion relation, swept across the first Brillouin zone and plotted with matplotlib.

8 · Density of States

.py density_of_states.py

Gaussian-broadened DOS $g(E)$ built from band eigenvalues (Section 15) — imports band_structure.py directly, locates the Fermi level by filling states, and classifies metal vs. semiconductor vs. insulator.

9 · Charge Density Visualization

.py charge_density_visualization.py

Hydrogen-like radial densities and a 2D cross-sectional density heatmap (Section 5/16), plotted with matplotlib for 1s, 2s, and 2p orbitals side by side.
