§1The Hydrogenic Hamiltonian

A hydrogenic (hydrogen-like) atom is a single electron bound to a nucleus of charge $+Ze$: neutral hydrogen ($Z=1$), He$^+$ ($Z=2$), Li$^{2+}$ ($Z=3$), etc. Treating the nucleus as fixed (or using the reduced mass $\mu$), the non-relativistic time-independent Schrödinger equation is

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2\psi(\mathbf r) \;-\;\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\,\psi(\mathbf r) \;=\; E\,\psi(\mathbf r)$$

The potential $V(r)=-\dfrac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$ is spherically symmetric, so angular momentum $\hat L^2$ and $\hat L_z$ both commute with $\hat H$ — the problem separates in spherical coordinates.

§2Separation of Variables

Writing $\psi(r,\theta,\varphi)=R(r)\,Y(\theta,\varphi)$ and substituting into $\nabla^2$ in spherical coordinates splits the PDE into an angular equation (solved by spherical harmonics $Y_l^m$) and a radial equation:

$$\hat L^2\,Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)=\hbar^2 l(l+1)\,Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi),\qquad l=0,1,2,\dots,\quad m=-l,\dots,l$$
$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) + \left[\frac{\hbar^2 l(l+1)}{2\mu r^2} - \frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\right]R(r) = E\,R(r)$$

§3Effective Potential & the Radial Equation

Substituting $u(r)=rR(r)$ turns the radial equation into an effective 1-D Schrödinger equation:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} + V_{\text{eff}}(r)\,u(r) = E\,u(r),\qquad V_{\text{eff}}(r) = \underbrace{-\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}}_{\text{Coulomb}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{\hbar^2 l(l+1)}{2\mu r^2}}_{\text{centrifugal barrier}}$$

For $l>0$ the centrifugal term repels the electron from the origin, creating a potential well with a minimum at finite $r$ — this well shape and its bound-state ladder are plotted numerically in the Simulations tab.

Energy Quantization

Demanding $u(r)\to0$ as $r\to\infty$ and regularity at the origin quantizes the energy purely through the principal quantum number $n=n_r+l+1$ ($n_r=0,1,2,\dots$ = number of radial nodes):

$$E_n = -\frac{\mu Z^2 e^4}{2(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2\hbar^2}\frac{1}{n^2} = -\,\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\,\text{Ry} = -\,13.606\,\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\ \text{eV}$$

Remarkably $E_n$ depends only on $n$, not on $l$ or $m$ — an "accidental" (Coulomb) degeneracy special to the $1/r$ potential, with degeneracy $n^2$ (or $2n^2$ including spin).

Length & Energy Scales

$$a_Z=\frac{a_0}{Z},\qquad a_0=\frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{\mu e^2}\approx 0.529\ \text{\AA}$$

$a_0$ is the Bohr radius; the hydrogenic orbit/orbital shrinks as $1/Z$ while binding energy grows as $Z^2$ — heavier hydrogenic ions are smaller and far more tightly bound.

$$1\ \text{Ry} = 13.6057\ \text{eV},\qquad E_1(\text{H}) = -13.6\ \text{eV}$$

§1Origins of Fine Structure

The non-relativistic Schrödinger equation predicts that $E_n$ depends only on the principal quantum number $n$ — states with the same $n$ but different $l$ are exactly degenerate. This is an artifact of treating the electron non-relativistically. Expanding the exact relativistic (Dirac) description of the electron to order $\alpha^2$ relative to the Bohr energies reveals three corrections of the same order, collectively called fine structure:

CorrectionPhysical originDepends on
Relativistic kinetic energy$T=\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}-mc^2 \neq \dfrac{p^2}{2m}$ — the electron's speed is a non-negligible fraction of $c$ in the inner shells$n,l$
Spin–orbit couplingIn the electron's rest frame the nucleus orbits it, creating a magnetic field that couples to the electron's spin magnetic moment: $\hat H'_{SO}\propto \mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$$n,l,j$
Darwin termA relativistic "jitter" (Zitterbewegung) smears the electron's position over roughly a Compton wavelength, softening the singular Coulomb potential it feels at the origin$n$, only $l=0$

Each correction scales as $(Z\alpha)^2$ relative to $E_n$, where $\alpha\approx1/137.036$ is the fine-structure constant — hence the shifts are $\sim10^{-5}$ of $E_n$ for hydrogen, growing as $Z^4$ for hydrogenic ions.

1 · Relativistic Kinetic-Energy Correction

Expanding $T=\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}-mc^2 \approx \dfrac{p^2}{2m}-\dfrac{p^4}{8m^3c^2}+\dots$ treats the second term as a perturbation:

$$\hat H'_{\text{rel}} = -\frac{\hat p^4}{8m^3c^2}$$

Using $\hat p^2/2m = \hat H_0 - V(r)$ together with the hydrogenic expectation values $\langle 1/r\rangle_{nl}$ and $\langle 1/r^2\rangle_{nl}$:

$$\Delta E_{\text{rel}} = -\frac{(Z\alpha)^4 mc^2}{2n^4}\left[\frac{n}{l+\tfrac12}-\frac34\right]$$

2 · Spin–Orbit Coupling

The moving electron sees the nuclear Coulomb field partly as a magnetic field; this couples to its spin moment via $\hat H'_{SO}=\dfrac{1}{2m^2c^2}\dfrac1r\dfrac{dV}{dr}\,\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$. With $\langle \mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S\rangle=\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2}\big[j(j{+}1){-}l(l{+}1){-}\tfrac34\big]$ and $\langle 1/r^3\rangle_{nl}$:

$$\Delta E_{SO} = \frac{(Z\alpha)^4mc^2}{4n^3\,l(l+\tfrac12)(l+1)}\Big[j(j{+}1)-l(l{+}1)-\tfrac34\Big]$$

Vanishes identically for $l=0$ since $\mathbf L=0$; positive for $j=l+\tfrac12$, negative for $j=l-\tfrac12$ — the higher-$j$ member of a doublet sits at higher energy.

§3Darwin Term

The Dirac electron is not a sharp point but is smeared over roughly its reduced Compton wavelength $\hbar/mc$ (Zitterbewegung). Averaging the Coulomb potential over this smearing gives a contact interaction $\propto\nabla^2V\propto\delta^3(\mathbf r)$, which only a wavefunction with $\psi(0)\neq0$ — i.e. an $l=0$ state — can feel:

$$\hat H'_{D} = \frac{\pi\hbar^2}{2m^2c^2}\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\delta^3(\mathbf r)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta E_{D} = \frac{(Z\alpha)^4mc^2}{2n^3}\,\delta_{l,0}$$

§4Combined Result & Term Symbols

Summing the three corrections, the $l$-dependence cancels exactly — the total fine-structure shift depends only on $n$ and $j$:

$$E_{n,j} = -13.6057\,\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\left[\,1+\frac{(Z\alpha)^2}{n^2}\left(\frac{n}{j+\tfrac12}-\frac34\right)\right]\ \text{eV}$$

States of the same $n$ and $j$ but different $l$ (e.g. $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$) remain exactly degenerate at this order — that residual degeneracy is lifted only by the QED Lamb shift (not modeled here). Each level carries a term symbol $^{2S+1}L_J$ (here $S=\tfrac12$ always):

§1Origin: Coupling to the Nucleus

Fine structure (previous tab) treats the nucleus as a fixed point charge with no internal structure. In reality the nucleus carries an intrinsic spin $\mathbf I$ and, unless $I=0$, a magnetic dipole moment $\boldsymbol\mu_I=g_I\mu_N\mathbf I/\hbar$, where $\mu_N$ (the nuclear magneton) is $\sim1836\times$ smaller than the Bohr magneton $\mu_B$ because of the proton-to-electron mass ratio. The electron's magnetic moment interacts weakly with this tiny nuclear moment, splitting each fine-structure level into a set of hyperfine sublevels — typically $\sim2000\times$ smaller than the fine-structure splitting itself, hence "hyperfine".

Total angular momentum including the nucleus is $\mathbf F=\mathbf I+\mathbf J$, with $F=|I-J|,\dots,I+J$. Radiative transitions between hyperfine levels of the ground state underlie atomic clocks and radio astronomy's famous 21 cm line.

Landé Interval Rule

Regardless of the microscopic mechanism, a coupling of the form $\hat H_{hf}=A\,\hat{\mathbf I}\cdot\hat{\mathbf J}/\hbar^2$ gives exactly solvable energies:

$$E_F = \frac{A}{2}\Big[F(F{+}1)-I(I{+}1)-J(J{+}1)\Big]$$

so adjacent levels are spaced by $E_F-E_{F-1}=A\,F$ — the Landé interval rule, an experimental fingerprint of magnetic-dipole hyperfine structure.

Two Mechanisms for A

$l=0$ (s states): the electron has non-zero probability density at the nucleus, giving a contact (Fermi) interaction — exactly tractable in closed form. $l>0$: $\psi(0)=0$, so instead the electron's orbital motion and the classical dipole field of its spin couple to $\boldsymbol\mu_I$ from a distance. That combined orbital + dipolar calculation is real physics but needs angular-momentum (Racah) algebra beyond a single closed formula, so — consistent with never presenting an unverified number as fact — the calculator below is restricted to the exactly-solvable $ns$ contact-term case.

§3The Fermi Contact Term (s states)

For $l=0$ the dominant hyperfine interaction is the Fermi contact term, a $\delta$-function coupling between the electron and nuclear magnetic moments evaluated at the origin:

$$\hat H_{hf} = \frac{2\mu_0}{3}\,g_e\mu_B\,g_I\mu_N\,\frac{\hat{\mathbf S}\cdot\hat{\mathbf I}}{\hbar^2}\,\delta^3(\mathbf r)$$

Using $|\psi_{ns}(0)|^2=Z^3/(\pi n^3a_0^3)$, the hyperfine constant for an $ns$ state (nuclear charge $Z$, single electron) is:

$$A_{ns} = \frac{2\mu_0}{3}\,g_e\mu_B\,g_I\mu_N\,\frac{Z^3}{\pi n^3a_0^3}\ \ \propto\ \frac{g_I\,Z^3}{n^3}$$

For hydrogen's $1s$ ground state ($Z=1,n=1$, proton $g_p=5.5857$) this leading-order formula gives $A/h\approx1422.8$ MHz — within $0.17\%$ of the measured $F{=}1\to F{=}0$ splitting of 1420.405751 MHz, the famous 21 cm line. The small residual comes from higher-order QED, relativistic, and finite-nuclear-size corrections not included in this leading-order treatment.

§1Why the Kinetic Energy Needs Correcting

The Schrödinger equation uses the non-relativistic kinetic energy $T=p^2/2m$. But in the inner shells of a hydrogenic ion the electron's speed is a non-negligible fraction of $c$ — roughly $v/c\sim Z\alpha$, which is already a few percent for $Z\sim10$. The exact relativistic kinetic energy is

$$T=\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}-mc^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m}-\frac{p^4}{8m^3c^2}+\frac{p^6}{16m^5c^4}-\dots$$

The leading correction beyond $p^2/2m$ is $-p^4/8m^3c^2$; treated as a first-order perturbation on top of the exact Coulomb eigenstates, this is the relativistic kinetic-energy correction.

§2Perturbation Theory Derivation

The perturbing Hamiltonian is $\hat H'_{\text{rel}}=-\hat p^4/8m^3c^2 = -\dfrac{1}{2mc^2}\big(\hat p^2/2m\big)^2$. Since $\hat p^2/2m=\hat H_0-V(r)$ acting on an eigenstate of $\hat H_0$, this becomes $-\dfrac{1}{2mc^2}(E_n-V)^2$, so first-order perturbation theory only needs the radial expectation values $\langle 1/r\rangle_{nl}$ and $\langle 1/r^2\rangle_{nl}$ (the Kramers relations for hydrogenic states):

$$\left\langle\frac1r\right\rangle_{nl}=\frac{Z}{n^2a_0},\qquad\qquad \left\langle\frac1{r^2}\right\rangle_{nl}=\frac{Z^2}{n^3\left(l+\tfrac12\right)a_0^2}$$

Substituting and simplifying gives the closed-form first-order shift:

$$\Delta E_{\text{rel}} = \langle\hat H'_{\text{rel}}\rangle_{nl} = -\frac{(Z\alpha)^4mc^2}{2n^4}\left[\frac{n}{l+\tfrac12}-\frac34\right]$$

Two structural features follow directly from this formula: (1) $\Delta E_{\text{rel}}<0$ always — the true relativistic energy is lower than the naive $p^2/2m$ estimate, because the exact $T(p)$ curves below its parabolic approximation at large $p$; (2) for fixed $n$, the shift is largest in magnitude for $l=0$ (the term in brackets is largest when $l$ is smallest) — s-electrons penetrate closest to the nucleus and reach the highest momenta.

§1A Magnetic Field From the Electron's Point of View

In the lab frame the electron orbits a stationary nucleus. Boosting to the electron's instantaneous rest frame, the nucleus instead appears to orbit it, and a moving charge encircling a point produces a magnetic field there, $\mathbf B\propto \mathbf E\times\mathbf v \propto \mathbf L$. This field couples to the electron's own spin magnetic moment $\boldsymbol\mu_s=-g_e\mu_B\mathbf S/\hbar$, giving an interaction energy $-\boldsymbol\mu_s\cdot\mathbf B\propto\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$. A careful relativistic treatment (Thomas precession) contributes an extra factor of $\tfrac12$ from the non-inertial rotation of the rest frame, giving the accepted spin–orbit Hamiltonian:

$$\hat H'_{SO}=\frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr}\,\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}, \qquad V(r)=-\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\ \Rightarrow\ \frac1r\frac{dV}{dr}=\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{1}{r^3}$$

Evaluating ⟨L·S⟩

$\hat{\mathbf L}$ and $\hat{\mathbf S}$ don't commute individually with $\hat H'_{SO}$, but $\hat{\mathbf J}=\hat{\mathbf L}+\hat{\mathbf S}$ does, so $|n,l,j,m_j\rangle$ is the right basis. Squaring $\mathbf J=\mathbf L+\mathbf S$:

$$\hat J^2=\hat L^2+\hat S^2+2\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}\ \Rightarrow\ \langle\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}\rangle=\frac{\hbar^2}{2}\Big[j(j{+}1)-l(l{+}1)-\tfrac34\Big]$$

with $j=l\pm\tfrac12$ for a spin-$\tfrac12$ electron ($l>0$; for $l=0$ there is no orbital motion to couple to, so $\hat H'_{SO}\equiv0$ identically).

Radial Part & Closed Form

The remaining radial integral is the Kramers relation $\langle 1/r^3\rangle_{nl}=Z^3/\big[n^3l(l+\tfrac12)(l+1)a_0^3\big]$ (only defined for $l>0$). Combining both pieces:

$$\Delta E_{SO}=\frac{(Z\alpha)^4mc^2}{4n^3\,l(l+\tfrac12)(l+1)}\Big[j(j{+}1)-l(l{+}1)-\tfrac34\Big]$$

§2The Doublet Splitting Collapses to a Clean Formula

For $j=l+\tfrac12$: $\,j(j{+}1)-l(l{+}1)-\tfrac34=l$. For $j=l-\tfrac12$: $\,j(j{+}1)-l(l{+}1)-\tfrac34=-(l{+}1)$. So the two sublevels sit at $\Delta E_{SO}=+C\,l$ and $\Delta E_{SO}=-C\,(l{+}1)$ around the unsplit energy, where $C=(Z\alpha)^4mc^2/[4n^3l(l+\tfrac12)(l+1)]$. The higher-$j$ level always lies above the lower-$j$ level. Their separation simplifies remarkably (the factor of $2l{+}1$ cancels the $l+\tfrac12$ in the denominator):

$$\Delta E_{SO}(j{=}l{+}\tfrac12)-\Delta E_{SO}(j{=}l{-}\tfrac12) = \frac{(Z\alpha)^4mc^2}{2\,n^3\,l(l+1)}$$

This doublet splitting is exactly what produces fine-structure "doublets" in real spectra (the hydrogenic analogue of the sodium D-line splitting) — it grows as $Z^4$, so it is easily resolved even at modest resolution in heavier hydrogenic ions.

§1The Electron Isn't Quite a Point

The Dirac equation, expanded non-relativistically via the Foldy–Wouthuysen transformation, reveals that a Dirac electron's position is not perfectly sharp: rapid interference between positive- and negative-energy components (Zitterbewegung, "trembling motion") smears its effective position over roughly a reduced Compton wavelength, $\lambdabar_C=\hbar/mc\approx3.86\times10^{-13}\,\text{m}$. The electron therefore samples the potential $V(\mathbf r)$ not at a point but averaged over this tiny neighborhood, which to leading order replaces $V(\mathbf r)$ with $V(\mathbf r)+\tfrac16\lambdabar_C^2\nabla^2V(\mathbf r)$.

§2A Contact Interaction — and Why It Only Touches s-States

For the Coulomb potential, $\nabla^2V\propto\nabla^2(1/r)=-4\pi\,\delta^3(\mathbf r)$ (the familiar Poisson-equation identity for a point charge). The Foldy–Wouthuysen procedure fixes the numerical prefactor, giving:

$$\hat H'_D = \frac{\pi\hbar^2}{2m^2c^2}\,\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\delta^3(\mathbf r)$$

A $\delta^3(\mathbf r)$ operator only has a nonzero expectation value against wavefunctions with $\psi(0)\neq0$. Every hydrogenic orbital with $l>0$ vanishes at the origin (the centrifugal barrier $l(l+1)/r^2$ excludes them), so only s-states ($l=0$) feel the Darwin term at all — it is exactly zero for every $p,d,f,\dots$ orbital. Using $|\psi_{n00}(0)|^2=Z^3/(\pi n^3a_0^3)$:

$$\Delta E_D = \langle\hat H'_D\rangle_{n0} = \frac{(Z\alpha)^4mc^2}{2n^3}\qquad(l=0\ \text{only})$$

§3A Curious Coincidence

Spin–orbit coupling vanishes identically for $l=0$ (there's no orbital angular momentum to couple to spin), so naively an $ns_{1/2}$ state would be missing the "spin–orbit-like" piece that every other $j=\tfrac12$ state gets. The Darwin term exactly fills that gap: for $l=0$, $\Delta E_D$ takes precisely the value that the spin–orbit formula would give if you (invalidly) substituted $l=0,j=\tfrac12$ into it. The net effect — already summarized on the Fine Structure tab — is that the total shift $\Delta E_{\text{rel}}+\Delta E_{SO}+\Delta E_D$ depends only on $n$ and $j$, never on $l$ directly, restoring an effective degeneracy between e.g. $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$.

§1The Degeneracy That Isn't

The Fine Structure tab showed that relativistic corrections leave the energy depending only on $n$ and $j$ — so $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$, despite having different $l$, were predicted to be exactly degenerate. In 1947 Willis Lamb and Robert Retherford used microwave spectroscopy to show this is wrong: $2S_{1/2}$ sits 1057.8 MHz above $2P_{1/2}$. Neither the Schrödinger equation nor the Dirac equation predicts this — it is a pure quantum electrodynamic (QED) effect, arising because the electron continuously emits and reabsorbs virtual photons and because the vacuum itself is polarizable. The Lamb shift was the experimental result that essentially launched modern QED (Bethe's rapid theoretical explanation, within weeks, is one of the most celebrated calculations in physics history).

1 · Self-Energy (dominant)

The electron perpetually emits and reabsorbs virtual photons. This "dresses" the electron, slightly smearing its effective charge distribution and softening the Coulomb singularity it feels — conceptually similar to the Darwin term's Zitterbewegung smearing, but arising from radiative (photon-loop) rather than purely kinematic physics. This effect is largest for $l=0$, where the electron actually reaches the nucleus, but survives at greatly reduced strength for $l>0$ through a "magnetic moment"-type term.

2 · Vacuum Polarization (small, opposite sign)

The vacuum is not empty: virtual electron–positron pairs briefly pop in and out of existence around the nucleus, partially screening its charge at short range (the Uehling potential). This slightly weakens the effective attraction felt very close to the nucleus, shifting s-state energies in the opposite direction to the self-energy effect and partially canceling it — at leading order it is about $12\times$ smaller.

§2The Bethe Logarithm & Leading-Order Formula

A full QED treatment (Bethe, 1947; refined by many others since) gives the self-energy and vacuum-polarization shifts as power series in $Z\alpha$. Keeping only the leading logarithm and leading constant — the terms that dominate for light hydrogenic ions — the self-energy contribution is:

$$\Delta E_{SE} = \frac{\alpha}{\pi}\frac{(Z\alpha)^4}{n^3}mc^2\left[\frac43\ln\frac{1}{(Z\alpha)^2} - \frac43\ln k_0(n,l) + \frac{10}{9}\right]\qquad(l=0)$$

The large logarithm $\ln(1/(Z\alpha)^2)\approx9.7$ comes from integrating the virtual-photon energy over the huge range between atomic binding energies and $mc^2$; the state-dependent piece, $\ln k_0(n,l)$, is the Bethe logarithm — a weighted average of $\ln|E-E_n|$ over the full spectrum of virtual intermediate states. It cannot be obtained from a simple closed formula and must be computed numerically state-by-state (Drake & Swainson, 1990); this app uses the published values below rather than approximating it. For $l>0$ the large logarithm vanishes ($\delta_{l0}=0$ in the formula) and only a much smaller "magnetic moment" term survives:

$$\Delta E_{SE} = \frac{\alpha}{\pi}\frac{(Z\alpha)^4}{n^3}mc^2\left[-\frac43\ln k_0(n,l) - \frac{1}{2\kappa(2l+1)}\right]\qquad(l>0),\qquad \kappa=\begin{cases}-(l+1) & j=l+\tfrac12\\[2pt] l & j=l-\tfrac12\end{cases}$$

The leading vacuum-polarization piece is a much smaller contact term, nonzero only for $l=0$:

$$\Delta E_{VP} = \frac{\alpha}{\pi}\frac{(Z\alpha)^4}{n^3}mc^2\left(-\frac{4}{15}\right)\delta_{l0}, \qquad \Delta E_{\text{Lamb}} = \Delta E_{SE}+\Delta E_{VP}$$

This is a genuinely leading-order treatment: higher powers of $Z\alpha$, two-loop diagrams, and nuclear recoil/size effects (all documented in CODATA's much longer expansion) are omitted, so results are accurate to a few tenths of a percent for hydrogen — this is essentially Bethe's original 1947 calculation, which itself predicted $\sim$1040 MHz against Lamb & Retherford's measured $\sim$1000 MHz, already vindicating the QED picture.

§1Physical Origin & the Perturbing Hamiltonian

Placing a hydrogenic atom in a uniform external electric field $\mathbf F = F\hat z$ adds a perturbation to the Hamiltonian equal to the electron's potential energy in that field:

$$\hat H = \hat H_0 + \hat H',\qquad \hat H' = eFz$$

This is the Stark effect — the electric-field analogue of the Zeeman effect. Unlike the magnetic case, $\hat H'$ has odd parity ($z\to-z$ under inversion), so it connects states of opposite parity. Since all $n^2$ states of a given shell $n$ are degenerate in the unperturbed hydrogenic spectrum (the same Coulomb "accidental" degeneracy discussed in the Theory tab) and span both parities (every $l=0,\dots,n-1$ is present), ordinary non-degenerate perturbation theory does not apply — the field mixes states within the shell at first order, however weak.

§2Degenerate Perturbation Theory & Parabolic Coordinates

The right approach is degenerate perturbation theory: diagonalize $\hat H'$ within the $n^2$-fold degenerate subspace of shell $n$. In the spherical basis $|n\,l\,m\rangle$, the matrix elements $\langle n\,l\,m|z|n\,l'\,m\rangle$ are nonzero only for $l'=l\pm1,\ m'=m$ (the dipole selection rule), so $\hat H'$ is tridiagonal in $l$ at fixed $n,m$ — not yet diagonal, but sparse.

Remarkably, this tridiagonal matrix is diagonalized exactly by switching to parabolic coordinates:

$$\xi = r+z,\qquad \eta = r-z,\qquad \varphi=\tan^{-1}(y/x),\qquad (\xi,\eta\in[0,\infty))$$

The reason this works is a special integrability property of the Coulomb-plus-uniform-field problem: writing $z=\tfrac12(\xi-\eta)$ and $r=\tfrac12(\xi+\eta)$, both $-Ze^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$ and $eFz$ become additively separable when the Schrödinger equation is multiplied through by $\xi+\eta$. Seeking $\psi=f(\xi)\,g(\eta)\,e^{im\varphi}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ splits the exact (field-included) equation into two ordinary differential equations coupled only through a separation constant $\beta$ and the quantum number $m$ — solvable in closed form at $F=0$ (confluent hypergeometric/Whittaker functions building the usual $R_{nl}$), and perturbatively in $F$ for small fields. This exact separability is why parabolic quantum numbers, not $(n,l)$, are the natural labels once a field is present.

§3Linear Stark Effect: Closed-Form Energies

Solving the degenerate perturbation problem (equivalently, first-order perturbation theory directly in the parabolic basis) shows that the states $|n\,n_1\,n_2\,m\rangle$ — labeled by the parabolic quantum numbers $n_1,n_2=0,1,2,\dots$ and $m=0,\pm1,\dots,\pm(n-1)$ subject to

$$n_1+n_2+|m|+1 = n$$

are already exact eigenstates of $\hat H'$ within the shell, with a first-order energy shift that is linear in the field:

$$\Delta E^{(1)}_{n\,n_1n_2m} = \frac{3}{2}\,n\,(n_1-n_2)\,\frac{e\,a_0}{Z}\,F$$

using $a_Z=a_0/Z$ for a hydrogenic ion of nuclear charge $Z$ (Bethe & Salpeter, Quantum Mechanics of One- and Two-Electron Atoms, 1957; Griffiths, Introduction to Quantum Mechanics, Ch.7). Physically, $(n_1-n_2)$ measures the built-in electric dipole moment $\langle z\rangle=\tfrac32 n(n_1-n_2)a_Z$ that the degenerate Coulomb states already carry — the field simply couples linearly to this pre-existing dipole rather than inducing one. Each shell fans out into $2n-1$ equally spaced energy levels (in the quantity $n_1-n_2\in\{-(n-1),\dots,n-1\}$), symmetric about the unperturbed $E_n$, with the $m$ quantum number contributing only to degeneracy, not to the energy.

Because $\hat H'$ is odd under parity while $E_n$ depends only on $n$ at zeroth order, this first-order shift vanishes identically for $n_1=n_2$ (in particular for the ground state, where $n=1$ forces $n_1=n_2=m=0$) — those states have no permanent dipole and show no linear Stark effect.

§4Quadratic Stark Effect (1s) & Field Ionization

Ground-State Polarizability

For the $1s$ state the linear term vanishes, so the leading response is second order (quadratic) in $F$ — an induced dipole $\propto F$ giving energy $\propto -\tfrac12\alpha F^2$. For hydrogen this sum-over-states calculation has an exact closed form (solved via the Dalgarno–Lewis method):

$$\Delta E^{(2)}_{1s} = -\frac{9}{4}\,\frac{a_Z^3}{1}\,F^2\ \Longrightarrow\ \alpha_{1s}=\frac{9}{2}a_Z^3=\frac{9}{2}\frac{a_0^3}{Z^4}$$

The polarizability scales as $Z^{-4}$ (length$^3$/energy $\sim (1/Z)^3/Z^2$), so heavier hydrogenic ions are far stiffer against an external field. For $n>1$ states with $n_1=n_2$ (no linear shift) the analogous second-order polarizability requires a full sum over intermediate states without a simple closed form, so — consistent with never presenting an unverified number as fact — this app models the quadratic term only for the exactly-solvable $1s$ case.

Field Ionization: the Tilted Potential

Adding $eFz$ to the Coulomb well tilts it into a potential with no true bound states: on the "downhill" side the combined potential $V(z)=-Ze^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0|z|)+eFz$ (atomic units: $V(z)=-Z/|z|+Fz$) rises to a finite barrier and then falls to $-\infty$, so a bound electron can tunnel — or, at high enough field, simply flow over the top. Setting $dV/dz=0$ locates the saddle at $z_s=-\sqrt{Z/F}$, with barrier height:

$$V_{\text{saddle}} = -2\sqrt{ZF}\qquad\text{(atomic units)}$$

Over-barrier (classical) ionization sets in once $E_n\ge V_{\text{saddle}}$, i.e. $-Z^2/2n^2\ge-2\sqrt{ZF}$, giving the classical critical field:

$$F_{\text{crit}} = \frac{Z^3}{16\,n^4}\quad\text{(atomic units, }1\text{ a.u.}=5.142\times10^{9}\text{ V/cm)}$$

Real (quantum) ionization rates rise smoothly via tunneling well below $F_{\text{crit}}$ and depend on $(n_1,n_2,m)$, not just $n$; $F_{\text{crit}}$ is only the classical over-the-barrier threshold, shown here as an orientation scale, not a sharp quantum cutoff.

§1Physical Origin

An external magnetic field $\mathbf B=B\hat z$ couples to the electron's total magnetic moment, which has an orbital part and a spin part:

$$\boldsymbol\mu = \boldsymbol\mu_l+\boldsymbol\mu_s = -\frac{\mu_B}{\hbar}\big(\hat{\mathbf L}+g_e\hat{\mathbf S}\big), \qquad \hat H_Z = -\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B = \frac{\mu_B B}{\hbar}\big(\hat L_z + g_e\hat S_z\big)$$

$\mu_B=e\hbar/2m\approx5.7884\times10^{-5}\,\text{eV/T}$ is the Bohr magneton and $g_e\approx2.00232$ is the electron spin g-factor (the same value already used on the Hyperfine tab). The factor of $g_e\approx2$ rather than $1$ on the spin term — spin is "twice as magnetic" as orbital motion for the same angular momentum — is exactly why the orbital and spin contributions don't simply add: $\mathbf L+g_e\mathbf S$ is not parallel to $\mathbf J=\mathbf L+\mathbf S$, which is the root cause of the Zeeman effect's complexity.

The full problem is $\hat H=\hat H_0+\hat H'_{SO}+\hat H_Z$, where $\hat H'_{SO}=\zeta_{nl}\,\hat{\mathbf L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf S}/\hbar^2$ is the same spin–orbit coupling from the L·S Coupling tab, with coupling constant

$$\zeta_{nl} = \frac{(Z\alpha)^4mc^2}{2n^3\,l(l+\tfrac12)(l+1)}\qquad(l>0),\qquad \zeta_{n0}\equiv0$$

chosen so that $\Delta E_{SO}(j)=\tfrac{\zeta_{nl}}{2}\big[j(j{+}1)-l(l{+}1)-\tfrac34\big]$ reproduces exactly the fine-structure formula from the L·S Coupling tab. Which term dominates — $\hat H'_{SO}$ (favoring the $|n,l,j,m_j\rangle$ basis) or $\hat H_Z$ (favoring the $|n,l,m_l,m_s\rangle$ basis) — sets the physical regime, controlled entirely by the dimensionless ratio $x=\mu_B B/\zeta_{nl}$.

2 · Weak Field: Anomalous Zeeman Effect ($x\ll1$)

When $\mu_B B\ll\zeta_{nl}$, spin–orbit coupling dominates and $\mathbf J=\mathbf L+\mathbf S$ is the good quantum number; $\mathbf L$ and $\mathbf S$ precess rapidly about the (nearly fixed) $\mathbf J$, which itself precesses slowly about $\mathbf B$. First-order perturbation theory in the $|n,l,j,m_j\rangle$ basis projects $\mathbf L+g_e\mathbf S$ onto $\mathbf J$, giving the Landé g-factor:

$$g_J = 1+(g_e-1)\,\frac{j(j{+}1)+s(s{+}1)-l(l{+}1)}{2j(j{+}1)},\qquad \Delta E_Z = g_J\,\mu_B B\,m_j$$

Each fine-structure level $j$ splits into $2j{+}1$ equally spaced sublevels $m_j=-j,\dots,+j$, but the spacing $g_J\mu_B B$ differs between levels of different $j$ (hence "anomalous" — historically mysterious before electron spin was known). For $l=0$ ($j=\tfrac12$), $g_J=g_e\approx2$ exactly: a pure spin doublet.

3 · Strong Field: Paschen–Back Effect ($x\gg1$)

When $\mu_B B\gg\zeta_{nl}$, the field dominates and decouples $\mathbf L$ and $\mathbf S$: each precesses independently and rapidly about $\mathbf B$, so $m_l$ and $m_s$ (not $j,m_j$) become good quantum numbers. Treating $\hat H'_{SO}$ as the (now smaller) perturbation on top of $\hat H_Z$:

$$\Delta E_{PB} = \mu_B B\,(m_l+g_e m_s) + \zeta_{nl}\,m_l m_s$$

The spacing between adjacent $m_l$ levels becomes uniform ($\mu_B B$) and independent of $l$; the residual spin–orbit term $\zeta_{nl}m_lm_s$ survives as a small, $B$-independent correction. Selection rules simplify dramatically in this basis, which is why Paschen & Back originally discovered the effect at fields large enough to wash out the fine-structure multiplet pattern.

§4Intermediate Field: Exact Diagonalization

Neither limit is exact at general $B$, but the full problem is exactly solvable at every field strength because $\hat H'_{SO}+\hat H_Z$ conserves $M\equiv m_l+m_s$ (both terms commute with $\hat L_z+\hat S_z$). For fixed $l>0$ and $M$, only two basis states share that $M$: $|m_l{=}M{-}\tfrac12,m_s{=}{+}\tfrac12\rangle$ and $|m_l{=}M{+}\tfrac12,m_s{=}{-}\tfrac12\rangle$ (except at the stretched extremes $M=\pm(l+\tfrac12)$, where only one state exists and no mixing is possible). The $2\times2$ Hamiltonian matrix in this subspace,

$$H = \begin{pmatrix} \mu_B B(M{-}\tfrac12{+}\tfrac{g_e}2)+\tfrac{\zeta}{2}(M{-}\tfrac12) & \tfrac{\zeta}{2}\sqrt{l(l{+}1)-M^2+\tfrac14} \\[4pt] \tfrac{\zeta}{2}\sqrt{l(l{+}1)-M^2+\tfrac14} & \mu_B B(M{+}\tfrac12{-}\tfrac{g_e}2)-\tfrac{\zeta}{2}(M{+}\tfrac12) \end{pmatrix}$$

is diagonalized in closed form (a $2\times2$ eigenvalue problem), giving the two exact energies $E_\pm=\tfrac12(H_{11}{+}H_{22})\pm\sqrt{\tfrac14(H_{11}{-}H_{22})^2+H_{12}^2}$ for every value of $B$ — this is mathematically the same structure as the Breit–Rabi formula used for hyperfine Zeeman splitting, with $l$ playing the role of nuclear spin $I$. At $B=0$ it collapses exactly onto the fine-structure doublet (independent of $M$, as required); at large $B$ the off-diagonal term becomes negligible compared to $|H_{11}{-}H_{22}|$ and it smoothly reproduces the Paschen–Back formula above. This exact solution — not the weak- or strong-field approximation — is what the calculator and diagrams below use as the primary result; the two limiting formulas are shown alongside it only for comparison.

§1Expectation Values of Stationary States

For a hydrogenic eigenstate $\psi_{nlm}=R_{nl}(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$, every radial observable can be evaluated in closed form because $\displaystyle\langle r^k\rangle=\int_0^\infty r^{k+2}R_{nl}^2(r)\,dr$ obeys an exact three-term recursion — the Kramers–Pasternack relation:

$$\frac{k+1}{n^2}\langle r^k\rangle \;-\; (2k+1)\,a_Z\langle r^{k-1}\rangle \;+\; \frac{k\,a_Z^2}{4}\Big[(2l+1)^2-k^2\Big]\langle r^{k-2}\rangle \;=\;0,\qquad a_Z=\frac{a_0}{Z}$$

Starting from the trivial normalization $\langle r^0\rangle=1$ and the closed form $\langle r\rangle=\tfrac{a_Z}{2}[3n^2-l(l{+}1)]$, this relation is solved forward to reach any positive power. Seeded instead by the independently known $\langle r^{-1}\rangle,\langle r^{-2}\rangle,\langle r^{-3}\rangle$, it is solved backward to reach any negative power. The calculator below implements exactly this recursion — not a numerical fit — for arbitrary integer $\langle r^k\rangle$.

Momentum: ⟨p⟩ and ⟨p²⟩

A bound stationary state carries zero probability current everywhere, so $\langle\hat{\mathbf p}\rangle=\mathbf 0$ identically for every $(n,l,m)$ — there is no preferred direction of motion to average to.

$\langle\hat p^2\rangle$ instead follows from the virial theorem. For $V(r)\propto r^{-1}$, $2\langle T\rangle=-\langle V\rangle$; combined with $E_n=\langle T\rangle+\langle V\rangle$ this gives the clean pair

$$\langle T\rangle=\frac{\langle p^2\rangle}{2\mu}=-E_n,\qquad \langle V\rangle = 2E_n$$

so $\langle p^2\rangle=2\mu|E_n|$ — independent of $l,m$, exactly like $E_n$ itself, and never requiring an explicit momentum-space integral.

Angular Momentum: ⟨L²⟩

$\hat L^2$ is diagonal in the $|n,l,m\rangle$ basis by construction — no integral is needed at all:

$$\langle \hat L^2\rangle = \hbar^2\,l(l+1),\qquad \langle \hat L_z\rangle = \hbar\,m$$

Both are sharp (zero-variance) quantum numbers here, not statistical averages over a spread of outcomes — every measurement of $L^2$ on this state returns exactly $\hbar^2l(l{+}1)$.
