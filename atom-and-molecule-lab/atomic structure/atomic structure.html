How the atom is calculated

Each electron moves in the average field of the nucleus and all other electrons. The simulator solves the Kohn–Sham equations of density-functional theory self-consistently for a spherical atom:

\[\Big[-\tfrac12\nabla^2-\frac{Z}{r}+V_H[\rho](r)+V_{xc}[\rho](r)\Big]\varphi_{n\ell}=\varepsilon_{n\ell}\,\varphi_{n\ell},\qquad \rho(r)=\sum f_{n\ell}\,|\varphi_{n\ell}|^2\][ −½∇² − Z/r + VH[ρ](r) + Vxc[ρ](r) ] φnℓ = εnℓ φnℓ, ρ(r) = Σ fnℓ |φnℓ|²

VH is the electrostatic (Hartree) potential of the electron cloud and Vxc the exchange–correlation potential in the local-density approximation (Slater exchange, Perdew–Zunger correlation). The radial equations are integrated with the Numerov method on a logarithmic grid, and the potential is updated until it no longer changes (self-consistency). For neon the total energy agrees with the NIST LDA reference to 0.007 hartree (0.005 %). Hartree–Fock (the HF option) treats exchange exactly and agrees with published numerical Hartree–Fock energies to 10⁻⁵ hartree. The Theory tab documents every method in detail.

The ionisation energy is the energy difference between the ion and the atom (ΔSCF), which is much more accurate than −ε of the highest orbital. The ground term is predicted with Hund’s rules.

Method options. Xα keeps only Slater exchange, scaled by 3α/2; X only is the same with α = 2/3 (no correlation). Hartree has no exchange or correlation at all, but each electron feels the field of all the others and not its own: exact for one electron, and too weakly bound for many. Spin-polarised solves separate equations for ↑ and ↓ electrons (LSDA: spin-scaled exchange, Perdew–Zunger correlation interpolated in the polarisation ζ), with the open subshells filled by Hund’s rule. This lowers the energy of atoms with unpaired electrons such as N, O and Fe. Scalar-relativistic solves the Koelling–Harmon radial equation, which includes the mass–velocity and Darwin terms but not spin–orbit coupling:

\[P''=\Big[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}+2M(V-\varepsilon)\Big]P+\frac{M'}{M}\Big(P'-\frac{P}{r}\Big),\qquad M=1+\frac{\varepsilon-V}{2c^2}\]P″ = [ℓ(ℓ+1)/r² + 2M(V−ε)] P + (M′/M)(P′ − P/r), M = 1 + (ε − V)/2c²

It contracts and stabilises s and p₁/₂-like orbitals and expands d and f orbitals. For gold, the 6s orbital shrinks and the ionisation energy rises from 7.6 eV to 9.7 eV (measured 9.23 eV). This is why gold is yellow and mercury is a liquid. For hydrogen-like ions the s levels agree with the Dirac equation.

Quantum defects and Rydberg series

A Rydberg electron outside an ion of charge ζ = Z − N + 1 has levels E = −ζ²/2(n − δℓ)² hartree. The quantum defect δℓ measures how far the electron penetrates the core: large for s, small for p, almost zero for d and f, where the centrifugal barrier keeps the electron outside. δ is nearly the same for every n of a series, which is what the Rydberg formula assumes. The Rydberg–Ritz fit δ(n) = δ₀ + δ₂/(n − δ₀)² to the calculated levels predicts the whole series; the plot extends it to n = 30, where the levels crowd together below the ionisation limit. Theory and methods

This page documents every calculation in the simulator: the physical model, the equations exactly as implemented, the numerical methods, the parameters, the reference data and how well the results agree with experiment. Everything runs in your browser in JavaScript; nothing is fitted to the measurements it is compared with.

The core is a self-consistent-field solver for a spherical atom: Kohn–Sham density-functional theory, Hartree’s method or Hartree–Fock, the density-functional options optionally spin-polarised and scalar-relativistic, on a logarithmic radial grid with the Numerov integrator. Everything else, from spectra and lifetimes to X-ray lines and periodic trends, is built on the orbitals and potentials it produces.

2. The many-electron atom

For a nucleus of charge Z and N electrons the non-relativistic Hamiltonian is

\[H=\sum_i\Big[-\tfrac12\nabla_i^2-\frac{Z}{r_i}\Big]+\sum_{i<j}\frac{1}{|\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j|}\]H = Σᵢ [ −½∇ᵢ² − Z/rᵢ ] + Σᵢ<ⱼ 1/|rᵢ − rⱼ|

The electron–electron repulsion couples all electrons, so the Schrödinger equation cannot be separated. The central-field approximation replaces it by an effective one-electron potential V(r) that depends only on the distance from the nucleus. Each electron then occupies an orbital

\[\varphi_{n\ell m}(\mathbf r)=\frac{u_{n\ell}(r)}{r}\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)\]φnℓm(r) = [unℓ(r)/r] · Yℓm(θ, φ)

and the radial function u obeys

\[-\tfrac12u''+\Big[V(r)+\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2r^2}\Big]u=\varepsilon u,\qquad u(0)=0,\quad u(\infty)=0,\quad \int_0^\infty u^2\,dr=1\]−½ u″ + [ V(r) + ℓ(ℓ+1)/(2r²) ] u = ε u, u(0) = 0, u(∞) = 0, ∫u² dr = 1

The number of radial nodes is n − ℓ − 1. The simulator keeps the density spherical: every subshell nℓ with occupation fnℓ contributes fnℓ|unℓ|²/(4πr²) to the density, as if its electrons were spread evenly over the 2ℓ+1 orbitals (the “spherical” or “configuration-average” approximation). This is exact for closed shells and an average over the terms of open shells.

3. Configurations, ions and ground terms

Ground configuration

Subshells are filled in the Madelung (n + ℓ, then n) order

1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p 6s 4f 5d 6p 7s 5f 6d 7p 8s

with capacities 2, 6, 10, 14 for s, p, d, f. The measured ground configurations of neutral atoms that break this order are built in: Cr, Cu, Nb, Mo, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ag, La, Ce, Gd, Pt, Au, Ac, Th, Pa, U, Np, Cm and Lr (for example Cr 3d⁵4s¹ instead of 3d⁴4s²).

Positive ions lose electrons from the subshell with the highest n first (highest ℓ among equal n), so 4s empties before 3d: Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶. Negative ions add electrons along the Madelung order. The configuration editor in the Atom tab lets you set any occupation and compare total energies.

Ground term (Hund’s rules)

For every open subshell the electrons are placed with spins parallel as far as possible (rule 1), then into mℓ = ℓ, ℓ−1, … to maximise ML (rule 2). This gives S = (N↑ − N↓)/2 and L = |Σmℓ|; contributions of several open subshells are added. J = |L − S| for a less than half-filled dominant subshell and L + S otherwise (rule 3). The result is written 2S+1LJ. For atoms with two strongly interacting open subshells (some lanthanides, such as Ce) the measured term can differ; this is flagged where known.

Slater’s rules

The screening constant σ of an electron, with Zeff = Z − σ, is estimated from Slater’s 1930 groups (1s)(2s,2p)(3s,3p)(3d)(4s,4p)(4d)(4f)(5s,5p)…: other electrons in the same group contribute 0.35 each (0.30 within 1s); for an s or p electron, electrons in shell n−1 contribute 0.85 and deeper shells 1.00; for a d or f electron, every electron in groups to the left contributes 1.00; groups to the right contribute nothing.

4. Density-functional theory

Hohenberg and Kohn showed that the ground-state energy is a functional of the electron density ρ(r) alone. Kohn and Sham made this practical by introducing non-interacting electrons that have the same density. Their orbitals obey

\[\Big[-\tfrac12\nabla^2-\frac{Z}{r}+V_H[\rho](r)+V_{xc}[\rho](r)\Big]\varphi_i=\varepsilon_i\varphi_i,\qquad \rho(r)=\sum_i f_i\,|\varphi_i(r)|^2\][ −½∇² − Z/r + VH[ρ](r) + Vxc[ρ](r) ] φᵢ = εᵢ φᵢ, ρ(r) = Σᵢ fᵢ |φᵢ(r)|²

The Hartree potential is the electrostatic potential of the electron cloud. For a spherical density with radial charge w(r) = 4πr²ρ(r) = Σ fnℓ unℓ²,

\[V_H(r)=\frac{q(r)}{r}+\int_r^\infty\frac{w(r')}{r'}\,dr',\qquad q(r)=\int_0^r w(r')\,dr'\]VH(r) = q(r)/r + ∫r∞ w(r′)/r′ dr′, q(r) = ∫0r w(r′) dr′

Both integrals are evaluated with the trapezoidal rule on the logarithmic grid (dr = r dx). Vxc = δExc/δρ contains everything else: exchange (the Pauli exclusion of same-spin electrons) and correlation. Because VH and Vxc depend on the orbitals, the equations are solved iteratively until the potential reproduces itself (section 11).

Spin polarisation (LSDA). With the option on, separate densities ρ↑ and ρ↓ are used. Each open subshell is filled by Hund’s rule (N↑ = min(f, 2ℓ+1), N↓ = f − N↑) and each spin channel has its own potential Vσ = −Z/r + VH[ρ] + Vxc,σ[ρ↑, ρ↓]. The orbitals table then shows ↑ and ↓ rows, and the reported spatial orbitals are occupation-weighted averages of the two channels.

Xα and exchange only

Slater’s Xα method keeps only exchange, scaled by 3α/2: εxXα = (3α/2)·εx. α = 2/3 is Kohn–Sham exchange (“X only”); Slater’s original α = 1; values near 0.7 reproduce Hartree–Fock total energies of atoms well. Neither includes correlation.

6. Hartree’s self-consistent field

The Hartree option has no exchange or correlation. Instead, as in Hartree’s original 1928 method, each orbital feels the field of all the other electrons but not its own: the potential for orbital i subtracts the Hartree potential of one electron in that orbital,

\[V_i(r)=-\frac Zr+V_H[\rho](r)-V_H\big[|u_i|^2\big](r)\]Vᵢ(r) = −Z/r + VH[ρ](r) − VH[|uᵢ|²](r)

The orbital-dependent self term is taken from the previous iteration. The method is exact for any one-electron ion (H gives −0.5 Eh, He⁺ −2 Eh), equals Hartree–Fock for the 1s² ground state of helium, and underbinds larger atoms because the exchange energy is missing.

7. Hartree–Fock

Hartree–Fock writes the wavefunction as a single Slater determinant, so it is antisymmetric and every pair of same-spin electrons feels the exchange interaction exactly. There is no correlation beyond that. As everywhere in the simulator the atom is spherical; open shells use the average of configuration (Slater), which is exact for closed shells and for single-term configurations such as ns¹ or np⁵.

\[F^k(ij)=\int u_i^2\,P^k_{jj}\,dr,\qquad G^k(ij)=\int u_iu_j\,P^k_{ij}\,dr,\qquad P^k_{ab}(r)=\int u_a(r')u_b(r')\,\frac{r_<^k}{r_>^{k+1}}\,dr'\]Fk(ij) = ∫ uᵢ² Pkjj dr, Gk(ij) = ∫ uᵢuⱼ Pkij dr, Pkab(r) = ∫ uaub(r′) r<k/r>k+1 dr′

the configuration-average energy is

\[\begin{aligned}E_{\rm av}&=\sum_i w_iI_i+\sum_i\tfrac12w_i(w_i-1)\Big[F^0(ii)-\sum_{k>0}c^k_{\ell\ell}F^k(ii)\Big]+\sum_{i<j}w_iw_j\Big[F^0(ij)-\tfrac12\sum_kd^k_{\ell_i\ell_j}G^k(ij)\Big]\\ c^k_{\ell\ell}&=\frac{2\ell+1}{4\ell+1}\begin{pmatrix}\ell&k&\ell\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}^{\!2},\qquad d^k_{\ell_i\ell_j}=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_i&k&\ell_j\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}^{\!2}\end{aligned}\]Eav = Σᵢ wᵢ Iᵢ + Σᵢ ½wᵢ(wᵢ−1) [ F⁰(ii) − Σk>0 ckℓℓ Fk(ii) ] + Σi<j wᵢwⱼ [ F⁰(ij) − ½ Σk dkℓᵢℓⱼ Gk(ij) ] ckℓℓ = (2ℓ+1)/(4ℓ+1) · ( ℓ k ℓ ; 0 0 0 )², dkℓᵢℓⱼ = ( ℓᵢ k ℓⱼ ; 0 0 0 )²

k runs over even values from |ℓᵢ − ℓⱼ| to ℓᵢ + ℓⱼ with the 3j symbol non-zero. Pk is computed with fourth-order cumulative integrals, ∫0r(r′/r)kρ dr′ and ∫r∞(r/r′)kρ dr′/r′, which stay finite for any k.

Radial Hartree–Fock equations

Varying Eav with orthonormal orbitals gives, for each subshell,

\[\begin{aligned}&\Big[-\tfrac12\frac{d^2}{dr^2}+\frac{\ell_i(\ell_i+1)}{2r^2}-\frac Zr+V_i(r)\Big]u_i-X_i(r)=\varepsilon_iu_i+\sum_{j\ne i,\ \ell_j=\ell_i}\lambda_{ij}u_j\\ &V_i=\sum_{j\ne i}w_jP^0_{jj}+(w_i-1)\Big[P^0_{ii}-\sum_{k>0}c^kP^k_{ii}\Big],\qquad X_i=\sum_{j\ne i}\tfrac12w_j\sum_kd^k_{\ell_i\ell_j}P^k_{ij}\,u_j\end{aligned}\][ −½ d²/dr² + ℓᵢ(ℓᵢ+1)/2r² − Z/r + Vᵢ(r) ] uᵢ − Xᵢ(r) = εᵢ uᵢ + Σj≠i, ℓⱼ=ℓᵢ λᵢⱼ uⱼ Vᵢ = Σj≠i wⱼ P⁰jj + (wᵢ − 1)[ P⁰ii − Σk>0 ck Pkii ] Xᵢ = Σj≠i ½ wⱼ Σk dkℓᵢℓⱼ Pkij uⱼ

Vᵢ is local (direct Coulomb and the exchange of an electron with the rest of its own subshell); Xᵢ, the exchange with other subshells, is non-local because Pkij contains uᵢ itself. The off-diagonal Lagrange multipliers λᵢⱼ keep orbitals of equal ℓ orthogonal. They obey wᵢλᵢⱼ = wⱼλⱼᵢ and are estimated each iteration as λᵢⱼ = [wᵢ⟨j|Fᵢ|i⟩ + wⱼ⟨i|Fⱼ|j⟩]/(2wᵢ), where Fᵢ is the operator on the left. For closed shells all Fᵢ coincide and the λᵢⱼ vanish.

Solving for one orbital

Within an iteration Xᵢ (with the λ terms) is treated as a fixed source, and the equation for uᵢ becomes inhomogeneous: y″ − f y = g on x = ln r with f as in section 9 and g = −2r3/2Xᵢ. It is solved with the Green’s function of the homogeneous equation,

\[y(x)=\frac{1}{W}\Big[y_d(x)\int_{-\infty}^{x}y_r\,g\,dx'+y_r(x)\int_x^{\infty}y_d\,g\,dx'\Big],\qquad W=y_ry_d'-y_r'y_d\]y(x) = [ yd(x) ∫−∞x yr g dx′ + yr(x) ∫x∞ yd g dx′ ] / W, W = yryd′ − yr′yd

where yr is the regular solution integrated outward and yd the decaying solution integrated inward, each in its numerically stable direction. Beyond the point where yd has decayed by e⁵⁰ the driven tail follows the local balance y ≈ −g/f, which gives inner orbitals the long exchange tail that Hartree–Fock requires. (A naive inward integration of the inhomogeneous equation amplifies the source by up to e¹³⁰ and fails.)

The orbital energy is not an input: εᵢ is chosen so that the solution satisfies the normalisation condition ⟨uᵢold|uᵢ(ε)⟩ = 1. Near the eigenvalue E₀ of the local operator (found with the ordinary Numerov search, node count n − ℓ − 1) this overlap behaves like c/(ε − E₀) + d; two evaluations fix c and d and predict the root, repeated until |overlap − 1| < 10⁻¹¹, with bisection in log|ε − E₀| as a fallback. The sign of c, set by the exchange source, decides on which side of E₀ the root lies. When the local operator has no such bound state (weakly bound negative-ion orbitals) the condition is found by scanning ε. Orbitals without any exchange partner (H, He 1s²) are found directly as eigenfunctions.

Self-consistency

Starting from exchange-only LDA orbitals (for negative ions, those of the neutral atom), each iteration rebuilds Vᵢ, Xᵢ and λᵢⱼ from the current orbitals, solves every orbital, normalises it, mixes it with the old one (uᵢ ← (1−β)uᵢ + βuᵢnew, β = 0.5, reduced towards 0.2 whenever the energy rises) and Gram–Schmidt orthogonalises orbitals of equal ℓ in order of n. The energy is evaluated from Eav with Simpson weights and a five-point kinetic-energy derivative, including the analytic contribution of the first grid interval where u ≈ c rℓ+1 (omitting it costs 4 × 10⁻⁵ of the kinetic energy). Convergence: energy change below 10⁻⁸ Eh and orbital change below 5 × 10⁻⁵, or a stable energy over several iterations. Because Eav is variational, the lowest-energy orbitals are kept if a slow drift sets in.

Koopmans’ theorem and the local potential

With frozen orbitals, removing an electron from orbital i costs −εᵢ (Koopmans), so Hartree–Fock orbital energies are reasonable ionisation energies (neon 2p: 23.14 eV, measured 21.56 eV), unlike LDA eigenvalues. The ΔSCF value includes orbital relaxation (neon: 19.84 eV); the remaining error is the missing correlation. For excited states, Zeff(r) and other features that need a single potential, the orbital-averaged (Slater) potential is used:

\[V_S(r)=-\frac Zr+\frac{\sum_iw_iu_i\,(V_iu_i-X_i)}{\sum_iw_iu_i^2}\]VS(r) = −Z/r + Σᵢ wᵢ uᵢ (Vᵢ uᵢ − Xᵢ) / Σᵢ wᵢ uᵢ²

It contains the exact exchange hole and therefore the correct −1/r decay far from a neutral atom. Hartree–Fock here is non-relativistic and spin-averaged; the spin-polarisation and relativistic options are ignored for it.

8. Total energy

The total energy is assembled from the eigenvalue sum, which counts the electron–electron interaction twice and uses the potential instead of the energy density, so two corrections follow:

\[E=\sum_if_i\varepsilon_i-\frac12\int V_H\,w\,dr+\int\big[w\,\varepsilon_{xc}-w_\uparrow v_{xc,\uparrow}-w_\downarrow v_{xc,\downarrow}\big]dr\quad\Big(+\tfrac12\sum_if_iJ_{ii}\ \text{for Hartree}\Big)\]E = Σᵢ fᵢ εᵢ − ½ ∫ VH w dr + ∫ [ w εxc − w↑ vxc,↑ − w↓ vxc,↓ ] dr (+ ½ Σᵢ fᵢ Jᵢᵢ for Hartree)

where wσ = 4πr²ρσ and Jᵢᵢ = ∫ VH[|uᵢ|²] |uᵢ|² dr is the self-repulsion removed in Hartree’s method. In the scalar-relativistic case the eigenvalues include the relativistic kinetic energy (rest mass excluded), so the same expression holds.

Energy differences of two self-consistent calculations (ΔSCF) give ionisation energies, configuration energies and core-hole binding energies. Both calculations use the same number of grid points, so grid errors largely cancel; ions end their grid at 40 a₀, where their density is negligible, and core-hole states use exactly the atom’s grid.

9. Radial equation on a logarithmic grid

Orbitals vary on the scale 1/Z near the nucleus and over many a₀ outside, so the grid is uniform in x = ln r:

\[r_i=e^{x_0+ih},\quad i=0\ldots N-1,\qquad r_0=\frac{10^{-5}}{Z},\qquad h=\frac{\ln(r_{\max}/r_0)}{N-1}\]rᵢ = exp(x₀ + i h), i = 0 … N−1, r₀ = 10⁻⁵/Z, h = ln(rmax/r₀)/(N − 1)

10. Numerov integration and the eigenvalue search

For y″ = f y the Numerov recurrence is accurate to O(h⁶) per step:

\[y_{i+1}=\frac{2y_i\big(1+\frac{5h^2}{12}f_i\big)-y_{i-1}\big(1-\frac{h^2}{12}f_{i-1}\big)}{1-\frac{h^2}{12}f_{i+1}}\]yi+1 = [ 2yᵢ(1 + 5h²fᵢ/12) − yi−1(1 − h²fi−1/12) ] / (1 − h²fi+1/12)

Counting eigenvalues. For a trial energy E the solution is integrated outward from y ∝ rℓ+½ to the point beyond the outer classical turning point where the WKB decay ∫√f dx exceeds 14. The number of sign changes, plus one if the solution is heading back towards zero there, equals the number of eigenvalues below E (Sturm–Liouville oscillation theorem). The eigenvalue with n − ℓ − 1 nodes is found by bisection until the bracket is narrower than 10⁻¹¹|E|.

Warm start. During self-consistency the bracket starts at the orbital’s previous eigenvalue ± max(2 %, 10⁻³ Eh), widened eightfold up to four times; otherwise it is [max(min Veff, −Z²), 0].

Wavefunction. At the converged energy the solution is integrated outward up to the classical turning point and inward from the point where the tail has decayed by e⁴⁰ (started at 10⁻³⁰), the two pieces are matched in value, and u is normalised to ∫u² dr = Σ uᵢ² rᵢ h = 1 (trapezoidal weights). Overflow is avoided by rescaling at 10¹⁵⁰.

11. Self-consistency and mixing

Starting potential. A screened Coulomb potential of Green–Sellin–Zachor form,

\[V_0(r)=-\frac1r\Big[(Z-N+1)+\frac{N-1}{H(e^{r/d}-1)+1}\Big],\qquad d=1.08\,(N-1)^{0.18}Z^{-1/3},\quad H=d\,(N-1)^{0.4}\]V₀(r) = −[ (Z − N + 1) + (N − 1) / (H(er/d − 1) + 1) ] / r, d = 1.08 (N−1)0.18 Z−1/3, H = d (N−1)0.4

goes from −Z/r at the nucleus to −(Z − N + 1)/r outside.

Iteration. Solve for all orbitals in Vin, build ρ, compute Vout = −Z/r + VH + Vxc, and form the next input. Convergence is reached when no eigenvalue changes by more than 2 × 10⁻⁷ Eh (at most 250 iterations); a result is labelled “not fully converged” above 10⁻⁵ Eh.

Anderson mixing. With residual Fn = Vout,n − Vin,n, the last two iterations are combined:

\[\begin{aligned}\theta&=\frac{\sum_iw_iF_{n,i}(F_{n,i}-F_{n-1,i})}{\sum_iw_i(F_{n,i}-F_{n-1,i})^2},\qquad w_i=r_i,\quad \theta\in(-0.5,\,1.5)\\ \bar V&=V^{\rm in}_n-\theta(V^{\rm in}_n-V^{\rm in}_{n-1}),\quad \bar F=F_n-\theta(F_n-F_{n-1}),\quad V^{\rm in}_{n+1}=\bar V+\beta\bar F,\quad \beta=0.5\ (0.45\ \text{spin-polarised})\end{aligned}\]θ = Σᵢ wᵢ Fn,i(Fn,i − Fn−1,i) / Σᵢ wᵢ (Fn,i − Fn−1,i)², wᵢ = rᵢ, θ clamped to (−0.5, 1.5) V̄ = Vin,n − θ (Vin,n − Vin,n−1), F̄ = Fn − θ (Fn − Fn−1), Vin,n+1 = V̄ + β F̄, β = 0.5 (0.45 spin-polarised)

For spin-polarised runs both channels share one θ. Compared with plain linear mixing (β = 0.35) this cuts the number of iterations by 35–45 % and changes total energies by less than 10⁻⁵ Eh.

12. Scalar-relativistic equation

Relativity matters for heavy atoms, where inner electrons move at a large fraction of c. The Koelling–Harmon scalar-relativistic equation keeps the mass–velocity and Darwin terms of the Dirac equation and averages over spin–orbit coupling. For the large component P(r):

\[P''=\Big[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}+2M(V-\varepsilon)\Big]P+\frac{M'}{M}\Big(P'-\frac Pr\Big),\qquad M(r)=1+\frac{\varepsilon-V(r)}{2c^2}\]P″ = [ ℓ(ℓ+1)/r² + 2M(V − ε) ] P + (M′/M)(P′ − P/r), M(r) = 1 + (ε − V(r))/(2c²)

The substitution P = M1/2 φ removes the first-derivative term. With p = M′/M = −V′/(2c²M), the equation on the logarithmic grid keeps the Numerov form y″ = f y with

\[f=\big(\ell+\tfrac12\big)^2+r^2\Big[2M(V-\varepsilon)+\frac{p^2}{4}-\frac{p'}{2}\Big]-rp,\qquad p=\frac{M'}{M},\quad p'=\frac{M''}{M}-p^2,\quad M''=-\frac{V''}{2c^2}\]f = (ℓ + ½)² + r² [ 2M(V − ε) + p²/4 − p′/2 ] − r p, p′ = M″/M − p², M″ = −V″/(2c²)

V′ and V″ are obtained from W = rV, which is smooth in x: V′ = (Wx − W)/r², V″ = (Wxx − 3Wx + 2W)/r³ with central differences. Near a point nucleus y ∝ rs with s = √f(r₀), so P ∝ rγ, γ = √(1 − (Zα)²) for s states, as in the Dirac equation. The density uses P² = M r y², normalised to one (the small component is folded into this normalisation).

For hydrogen-like ions the scalar-relativistic s levels agree with the Dirac equation to 10⁻³ Eh at Z = 80. The main effects in atoms: s and p orbitals contract and bind more strongly, d and f orbitals expand because they are better screened. For gold the 6s orbital contracts and the ionisation energy rises from 7.56 eV to 9.72 eV (measured 9.23 eV).

13. Spin–orbit coupling

Spin–orbit coupling is added to first order. The spin–orbit constant of an orbital is

\[\xi_{n\ell}=\frac{\alpha^2}{2}\int u_{n\ell}^2\,\frac{1}{M^2}\,\frac1r\frac{dV}{dr}\,dr\qquad(M=1\ \text{non-relativistically})\]ξnℓ = (α²/2) ∫ unℓ² (1/M²) (1/r)(dV/dr) dr (M = 1 non-relativistically)

and a level of total angular momentum j = ℓ ± ½ is shifted by

\[\Delta E_j=\frac\xi2\Big[j(j+1)-\ell(\ell+1)-\frac34\Big]=\begin{cases}+\ell\xi/2,&j=\ell+\frac12\\-(\ell+1)\xi/2,&j=\ell-\frac12\end{cases}\]ΔEj = (ξ/2) [ j(j+1) − ℓ(ℓ+1) − ¾ ] → +ℓξ/2 for j = ℓ + ½, −(ℓ+1)ξ/2 for j = ℓ − ½

so the doublet splitting is (ℓ + ½)ξ. The factor 1/M² (used for core holes in the X-ray calculation) suppresses the unphysical 1/r³ weight near the nucleus in the scalar-relativistic case. The valence spectra use the non-relativistic (Pauli) form. For a hole the shifts change sign: the binding energy of the j = ℓ + ½ hole is lower.

14. Derived atomic properties

  • Ionisation energy (ΔSCF): IE = E(ion) − E(atom), both self-consistent on the same grid. −ε of the highest orbital is shown for comparison; in exact DFT it would equal the IE, but the LDA’s self-interaction error makes it several eV too small.
  • Orbital size: ⟨r⟩ = ∫ u² r dr. “Atomic size” uses the most extended occupied orbital.
  • Radial probability: P(r) = u(r)² per orbital, and the total 4πr²ρ = Σ f u², whose maxima are the shells.
  • Effective nuclear charge: Zeff(r) = −r V(r), compared with Slater’s rules and with n√(−2ε), the charge a hydrogen-like ion would need to have the same orbital energy.
  • Orbital shapes: ψ = R(r) · Yrealℓm with R = u/r interpolated on the grid and real harmonics written as polynomials divided by rℓ (for example dxy ∝ xy/r²). Slices are coloured by r·ψ or r²|ψ|² so that outer shells remain visible; the plane through the nucleus is chosen where the angular function is largest. The 3D view draws the surface |ψ| = const enclosing about 80 % of the probability on a 36³ grid.
  • Successive ionisation energies: IEk = E(Z, N = Z−k) − E(Z, N = Z−k+1) for k = 1 … Z, with E(bare nucleus) = 0. The last electron is compared with the exact hydrogen-like value Z²/2 (or the Dirac value c²[1 − √(1 − (Zα)²)] when relativistic).
  • Isoelectronic series: the same N for Z = N … N + 7, with the ion ground configurations from section 3.
  • Configuration energies: any configuration entered in the editor is solved self-consistently; differences of total energies rank the configurations.

15. Excited states of the outer electron

Hydrogen-like ions are treated exactly (section 18). For a “selected atom” only one electron is excited and the others stay frozen in their ground orbitals. That electron moves in the self-consistent potential, corrected in two ways:

\[V_L(r)=\min\!\Big(V_{\rm SCF}(r),\,-\frac\zeta r\Big)-\frac{\alpha_d}{2(r^2+r_c^2)^2},\qquad \zeta=Z-N+1\]VL(r) = min( VSCF(r), −ζ/r ) − αd / [2(r² + rc²)²], ζ = Z − N + 1
  • Latter correction: far from the atom the electron must see the ion’s charge ζ. The LDA potential decays too fast (self-interaction), so it is replaced by −ζ/r where that is deeper. Without this, Rydberg series would not exist.
  • Core polarisation: the outer electron polarises the closed ion core, giving an extra attraction −αd/(2r⁴) at large r, cut off at rc = ⟨r⟩ of the outermost closed core shell. Measured core dipole polarisabilities are preset for the alkalis (a.u.): Li⁺ 0.1925, Na⁺ 0.9457, K⁺ 5.457, Rb⁺ 9.11, Cs⁺ 15.81, Fr⁺ 20.4, and approximate values for Cu⁺, Ag⁺, Au⁺.

With Hartree–Fock the excited electron is treated in frozen-core Hartree–Fock instead: the core is every electron except one from the highest orbital, frozen in its Hartree–Fock orbitals, and the electron solves

\[\Big[-\tfrac12\frac{d^2}{dr^2}+\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2r^2}-\frac Zr+\sum_{j\in\rm core}w_jP^0_{jj}+V_{\rm pol}\Big]u-\sum_{j\in\rm core}\tfrac12w_j\sum_kd^kP^k_{uj}\,u_j=\varepsilon u+\sum_{j:\,\ell_j=\ell}\lambda_ju_j\][ −½d²/dr² + ℓ(ℓ+1)/2r² − Z/r + Σj∈core wjP⁰jj + Vpol ] u − Σj∈core ½wj Σk dk Pkuj uj = ε u + Σj: ℓⱼ=ℓ λj uj

with λj = ⟨uj|F|u⟩ keeping it orthogonal to core orbitals of the same ℓ. The exact exchange with the core gives the correct −ζ/r tail without a Latter correction. To start next to the right state, the local reference operator includes the LDA exchange of the core density, vx = −(3ρcore/π)1/3, and the source carries the remainder (X + vxu); at convergence the equation is unchanged. The solution uses the same Green’s-function solver and normalisation condition as section 7. Pure frozen-core Hartree–Fock (αd = 0) misses core–valence correlation, so its levels are too shallow (Na 3s: 4.96 eV, measured 5.14 eV); the core-polarisation model was designed for LDA and tends to overcorrect on top of Hartree–Fock for the light alkalis.

Levels n ≤ nmax, ℓ ≤ 3 are found on a wide grid (closed core subshells excluded). This is accurate for one-valence-electron atoms (H, alkalis, Cu, Ag, Au) and a rough guide elsewhere, since term splittings of several open-shell electrons are not included.

Nuclear moments: ¹H μ = 2.792847, I = ½; ²H 0.857438, I = 1; ³H 2.978962, I = ½. For hydrogen this is the 21 cm line.

Isotope shift comes from the reduced mass: λ ∝ 1/μ, so λ(¹H) − λ(²H) = λ(¹H)(1 − μH/μD).

19. Zeeman and Stark effects

The line-profile panel diagonalises the full Hamiltonian of all levels in the diagram in the uncoupled basis |n ℓ mℓ ms⟩, with B and F along z:

\[H=H_0+\frac B2\,(L_z+g_sS_z)+F\,z,\qquad g_s=2.00231930\quad(B,\ F\ \text{in atomic units})\]H = H₀ + (B/2)(Lz + gsSz) + F z, gs = 2.00231930 (B, F in atomic units)

Zero-field part. Within each nℓ the two fine-structure energies E₊ (j = ℓ + ½) and E₋ (j = ℓ − ½), from Dirac + Lamb for hydrogen or from section 13 for atoms, are reproduced exactly by

\[\begin{aligned}H_0&=a+b\,\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S,\qquad b=\frac{E_+-E_-}{\ell+\frac12},\quad a=E_+-\frac{b\ell}{2}\\ \mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S&=L_zS_z+\tfrac12(L_+S_-+L_-S_+),\qquad \langle m_\ell+1,\downarrow|\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S|m_\ell,\uparrow\rangle=\tfrac12\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)-m_\ell(m_\ell+1)}\end{aligned}\]H₀ = a + b L·S, b = (E₊ − E₋)/(ℓ + ½), a = E₊ − bℓ/2 L·S = LzSz + ½(L₊S₋ + L₋S₊), ⟨mℓ+1, ↓|L·S|mℓ, ↑⟩ = ½ √(ℓ(ℓ+1) − mℓ(mℓ+1))

Stark coupling connects ℓ and ℓ ± 1 with the same mℓ and ms: ⟨n′ℓ′m|z|nℓm⟩ = Rn′ℓ′,nℓ ⟨ℓ′m|C¹₀|ℓm⟩, with

\[\langle\ell'm'|C^1_q|\ell m\rangle=(-1)^{m'}\sqrt{(2\ell'+1)(2\ell+1)}\begin{pmatrix}\ell'&1&\ell\\-m'&q&m\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\ell'&1&\ell\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\]⟨ℓ′m′|C¹q|ℓm⟩ = (−1)m′ √((2ℓ′+1)(2ℓ+1)) ( ℓ′ 1 ℓ ; −m′ q m ) ( ℓ′ 1 ℓ ; 0 0 0 )

(3j symbols by Racah’s formula). Because M = mℓ + ms is conserved, the matrix splits into blocks of fixed M, each diagonalised by the Jacobi method. The same calculation covers the anomalous Zeeman effect (weak B), the Paschen–Back regime (strong B), the linear Stark effect of degenerate hydrogen levels (n = 2 shifts ±3F exactly) and the quadratic Stark effect of non-degenerate levels. Field ionisation is not modelled.

Components. For eigenstates a (upper) and b (lower) with Δm = q, the emitted intensity is ∝ ω⁴ |⟨b|rq|a⟩|², summed coherently over the basis components. Viewed across B, π light (q = 0) has weight 1 and each σ (q = ±1) weight ½; viewed along B only σ light is seen.

20. Line profiles

Each component is broadened by a Voigt profile, approximated with the pseudo-Voigt of Thompson, Cox and Hastings: fV = η L + (1 − η) G with a common width

\[\begin{aligned}f&=\big(f_G^5+2.69269f_G^4f_L+2.42843f_G^3f_L^2+4.47163f_G^2f_L^3+0.07842f_Gf_L^4+f_L^5\big)^{1/5}\\ \eta&=1.36603\,\frac{f_L}{f}-0.47719\Big(\frac{f_L}{f}\Big)^2+0.11116\Big(\frac{f_L}{f}\Big)^3\end{aligned}\]f = (fG⁵ + 2.69269 fG⁴fL + 2.42843 fG³fL² + 4.47163 fG²fL³ + 0.07842 fGfL⁴ + fL⁵)1/5 η = 1.36603 (fL/f) − 0.47719 (fL/f)² + 0.11116 (fL/f)³
  • Doppler (Gaussian) FWHM: fG = λ √(8 kT ln 2 / (Mc²)), with M the atomic mass.
  • Natural (Lorentzian) FWHM: fL = λ² Γ / (2πc), Γ = Γupper + Γlower, where each state’s decay rate is the composition-weighted Σ cᵢ² A(nℓ) of its basis components. Stark mixing therefore passes the fast 2p decay on to 2s (quenching).

21. Quantum defects and Rydberg series

An electron outside an ion of charge ζ has hydrogen-like levels shifted by its penetration into the core:

\[E_{n\ell}=-\frac{\zeta^2}{2(n-\delta_\ell)^2}\ E_h,\qquad \delta_\ell=n-\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{-2E_{n\ell}}}\]Enℓ = −ζ² / [2(n − δℓ)²] Eh, δℓ = n − ζ/√(−2Enℓ)

δ is large for s (deep penetration), smaller for p, nearly zero for d and f. The Rydberg–Ritz formula δ(n) = δ₀ + δ₂/(n − δ₀)² is fitted to the calculated levels of each series by iterated linear least squares (fix δ₀, regress δ on 1/(n − δ₀)², update δ₀, 30 times) and used to extend the series to n = 30, solving δ(n) self-consistently for each n.

22. Photoionisation cross-sections

Above the ionisation limit the radial equation is solved at positive energy E in the same potential (outward Numerov from y ∝ rℓ+½ on a 6000-point grid to 150 a₀). The solution is normalised per unit energy (hartree) using its WKB amplitude in the outer region:

\[u_{E\ell}\to\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi p}}\,\sin(\ldots),\qquad p(r)=\sqrt{2(E-V)-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}},\qquad \text{amplitude}^2=u^2+\Big(\frac{u'}{p}\Big)^2\]uEℓ → √(2/(πp)) sin(…), p(r) = √(2(E − V) − ℓ(ℓ+1)/r²), amplitude² = u² + (u′/p)²

averaged over the last 500 grid points. The length-form cross-section from a bound orbital nℓ is

\[\sigma(\omega)=\frac{4\pi^2\alpha}{3}\,\omega\sum_{\ell'=\ell\pm1}\frac{\ell_>}{2\ell+1}\,\Big|\int u_{n\ell}\,r\,u_{E\ell'}\,dr\Big|^2\ a_0^2,\qquad \omega=E+I\]σ(ω) = (4π²α/3) ω Σℓ′=ℓ±1 [ℓ>/(2ℓ+1)] |∫ unℓ r uEℓ′ dr|² a₀², ω = E + I

For hydrogen-like 1s it is compared with the exact (Stobbe) result, k = √(ω/I − 1):

\[\sigma=\frac{2^9\pi^2\alpha}{3Z^2}\Big(\frac I\omega\Big)^4\,\frac{\exp\big(-4\arctan(k)/k\big)}{1-e^{-2\pi/k}}\ a_0^2\qquad(6.30\ \text{Mb at the hydrogen threshold})\]σ = (2⁹π²α/(3Z²)) (I/ω)⁴ · exp(−4 arctan(k)/k) / (1 − e−2π/k) a₀² (6.30 Mb at the hydrogen threshold)

In the alkalis the radial integral of the ℓ+1 channel changes sign close to threshold, producing a Cooper minimum. Photon energies run from threshold to threshold + max(2I, 10 eV) on a quadratic spacing that resolves the threshold region.

23. Saha–Boltzmann ionisation balance

In thermal equilibrium at temperature T and electron density ne, the ratio of ions to neutral atoms is given by the Saha equation,

\[\frac{N^+}{N^0}=\frac{2U^+}{U^0}\Big(\frac{2\pi m_ekT}{h^2}\Big)^{3/2}\frac{e^{-I/kT}}{n_e},\qquad \Big(\frac{2\pi m_ekT}{h^2}\Big)^{3/2}=2.4147\times10^{15}\,T^{3/2}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\]N⁺/N⁰ = (2U⁺/U⁰) (2π me kT/h²)3/2 e−I/kT / ne, (2π me kT/h²)3/2 = 2.4147 × 10¹⁵ T3/2 cm⁻³

with the partition functions U⁰ = Σ gi e−(Eᵢ−E₀)/kT over the levels in the diagram and U⁺ = 2J + 1 of the ion’s Hund ground term (1 for a bare proton). The fraction of all atoms in level i is N⁰/N × gie−(Eᵢ−E₀)/kT/U⁰; for hydrogen all ℓ of one n are counted together. For the Balmer lines (n = 2) this peaks near 10 000 K, explaining the spectral sequence of stars.

24. Hyperfine structure of alkali s levels

The Fermi contact interaction splits an s level by

\[\Delta E_{\rm hfs}=\frac{2\pi}{3}\,g_I\,(2I+1)\,\frac{m_e}{m_p}\,\alpha^2\,\rho_c\,(1+a_e)\ E_h\]ΔEhfs = (2π/3) gI (2I + 1) (me/mp) α² ρc (1 + ae) Eh

(identical to the hydrogen formula with ρc = Z³/(πn³)). Non-relativistically ρc = |ψ(0)|² = (u/r)²/(4π) at the nucleus. The scalar-relativistic s density diverges at a point nucleus, so ρc is averaged over the Thomson radius rT = Zα² a₀:

\[\rho_c=\int\rho(r)\,\delta_T(r)\,d^3r,\qquad \delta_T(r)=\frac{1}{4\pi r^2}\,\frac{r_T/2}{(r+r_T/2)^2}\]ρc = ∫ ρ(r) δT(r) d³r, δT(r) = (1/(4πr²)) · (rT/2) / (r + rT/2)²

Nuclear data: ⁷Li I = 3/2, μ = 3.256427; ²³Na 3/2, 2.217522; ³⁹K 3/2, 0.391507; ⁸⁷Rb 3/2, 2.751818; ¹³³Cs 7/2, 2.582025 μN. Core polarisation (spin polarisation of the closed core by the valence electron) is not included.

25. X-ray lines and Moseley’s law

For each element five self-consistent calculations are run: the neutral atom and the atom with one electron removed from 1s, 2p, 3p or 3d (keeping all others). The ΔSCF binding energies are

\[K=E(1s^{-1})-E_0,\quad L=E(2p^{-1})-E_0,\quad M_p=E(3p^{-1})-E_0,\quad M_d=E(3d^{-1})-E_0\]K = E(1s⁻¹) − E₀, L = E(2p⁻¹) − E₀, Mp = E(3p⁻¹) − E₀, Md = E(3d⁻¹) − E₀

The holes are split by spin–orbit coupling (section 13, with the scalar-relativistic 1/M² factor): L₃ = L − ξ2p/2, L₂ = L + ξ2p, M₃ = Mp − ξ3p/2, M₅ = Md − ξ3d. The emission lines are

\[K\alpha_1=K-L_3,\quad K\alpha_2=K-L_2,\quad K\beta_1=K-M_3,\quad L\alpha_1=L_3-M_5\]Kα₁ = K − L₃, Kα₂ = K − L₂, Kβ₁ = K − M₃, Lα₁ = L₃ − M₅

Moseley’s law follows from a hydrogen-like transition with screening: E(Kα) ≈ (3/4) Ry (Z − 1)² and E(Lα) ≈ (5/36) Ry (Z − 7.4)², so √(E/Ry) is linear in Z. Jobs are spread over parallel Web Workers, by default one per logical core reported by the browser (adjustable in the All elements tab, up to 64), heaviest elements first.

26. Periodic trends

  • All elements: every neutral atom and its positive ion, Z = 1 … 118, with the chosen method, on parallel workers. Several methods can be kept and overlaid.
  • Thomas–Fermi law: the statistical (electron-gas) model gives E = −0.768745 Z7/3 Eh, exact as Z → ∞. The Scott correction (+½Z², the strongly bound innermost electrons) and Schwinger’s exchange term (−0.2699 Z5/3) form the asymptotic expansion E ≈ −0.768745 Z7/3 + ½Z² − 0.2699 Z5/3.
  • Atomic radii: ⟨r⟩ of the outermost orbital of the free atom compared with Slater’s 1964 empirical radii from bond lengths (noble gases omitted). The Pearson correlation coefficient and the mean ratio are reported.
  • Ionisation energy error: ΔSCF and −εHOMO against measured first ionisation energies (NIST), with mean absolute errors.
  • Periodic-table colouring: any of these properties mapped to a sequential (or, for errors, diverging) colour scale.

27. Comparison mode

A second calculation B is run for the same atom, charge and configuration with a different method (any combination of functional, spin polarisation and relativity, or Hartree–Fock). LDA against Hartree–Fock shows the correlation energy and the failure of LDA eigenvalues as ionisation energies. The level scheme places A and B side by side in each ℓ column, the radial density and Zeff(r) overlay B dashed, and tables list total energy, ΔSCF ionisation energy, ε and ⟨r⟩ of every orbital with their differences. Because the configuration and grid are the same, all differences come from the physics of the method.

29. Limitations

  • Spherical, configuration-averaged atoms. Open shells are averaged over their terms; multiplet splittings are not calculated (the ground term is predicted by Hund’s rules).
  • Local density approximation. The self-interaction error makes −εHOMO too small and leaves most negative ions unbound; gradient corrections are not included. Hartree–Fock fixes both but has no correlation, so its electron affinities and total energies are too small in magnitude.
  • Hartree–Fock is non-relativistic, spin-averaged and uses the configuration average for open shells (term-dependent and unrestricted Hartree–Fock are not implemented). Configurations with several strongly coupled open shells (some lanthanides and actinides) converge more slowly.
  • Relativity. Scalar-relativistic only; spin–orbit coupling is first order, which underestimates large inner-shell splittings (gold 2p: 1625 eV calculated, 1816 eV measured). Finite nuclear size is ignored.
  • Excited states. One-electron excitations in a frozen core; accurate for one-valence-electron atoms only.
  • Core polarisation is included as a model potential for spectra but not for hyperfine structure.
  • External fields. Field ionisation and continuum coupling are not modelled; only levels in the diagram are mixed.
  • Partition functions are truncated at the levels in the diagram.
