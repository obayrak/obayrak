Physical Background

Radial Schrödinger equation, potential models, numerical methods, and normalization.

A. Radial Schrödinger Equation

For a central potential V(r) and orbital angular momentum ℓ, write ψ = R(r)Yℓm and substitute u(r) = r·R(r):

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} + \left[V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2\,\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu\,r^2}\right]u(r) = E\,u(r)$$

Rearranging into the canonical form u'' = f·u:

$$\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} = \underbrace{\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\!\left[V_{\rm eff}(r)-E\right]}_{f(r)}\cdot u(r),\qquad V_{\rm eff}(r)=V(r)+\frac{\hbar^2\,\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu\,r^2}$$

Boundary conditions: u(0) = 0 (regularity at origin); u(r) → 0 as r → ∞ (bound state). The energy E is an eigenvalue found by the shooting method with node counting.

Working in units of eV and nm: ℏc = 197.3269804 eV·nm, e² = 1.43997 eV·nm, masses in eV/c². Then f(r) = (2μc²/ℏc²)[V_eff(r)−E] in nm⁻².

B. Atomic Potentials

1. Coulomb (Hydrogen-like)

$$V(r)=-\frac{Z_1 Z_2\,e^2}{r}$$

Models hydrogen (Z₁=Z₂=1), He⁺ (Z₁=1, Z₂=2), or any hydrogen-like ion.

Exact eigenvalues:

$$ E_n = -\frac{\mu c^2\,\alpha^2}{2n^2},\quad n = n_r + \ell + 1,\quad \alpha = \frac{e^2}{\hbar c}\approx\frac{1}{137} $$

For hydrogen: E₁ = −13.6058 eV. The solver must reproduce this as a check.

Normalized Analytical Radial Wave Function

The exact normalized radial wave function is:

$$ R_{n\ell}(r) = \sqrt{\left(\frac{2Z}{n a_0}\right)^3 \frac{(n-\ell-1)!}{2n[(n+\ell)!]}} \; e^{-\frac{Zr}{n a_0}} \left(\frac{2Zr}{n a_0}\right)^\ell L_{n-\ell-1}^{2\ell+1}\!\left(\frac{2Zr}{n a_0}\right) $$

Here:
• \(a_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{\mu e^2}\) is the Bohr radius
• \(L_{n-\ell-1}^{2\ell+1}\) are associated Laguerre polynomials
• The wavefunction is normalized such that:

$$ \int_0^\infty |R_{n\ell}(r)|^2 \, r^2 \, dr = 1 $$

2. Yukawa

$$V(r)=-\frac{V_0\,e^{-\alpha r}}{r}$$

Originally derived to describe the nuclear force mediated by pions (Hideki Yukawa, 1935). Here V₀ [eV·nm] sets the strength and α = 1/λ [nm⁻¹] is the inverse screening/range. As α→0 it reduces to Coulomb with V₀ = Z₁Z₂e². Unlike the Coulomb case, the Yukawa potential supports only a finite number of bound states for each ℓ.

3. Screened Coulomb (Debye–Hückel)

$$V(r)=-\frac{Z_1 Z_2\,e^2\,e^{-r/\lambda_D}}{r}$$

In a plasma or dense medium, the Coulomb field is exponentially suppressed beyond the Debye screening length λ_D. The parameter λ_D [nm] characterizes the medium: smaller λ_D → stronger screening → shallower potential → fewer bound states. At λ_D → ∞ this reduces to pure Coulomb.

4. Hulthén Potential

$$ V(r) = -\,\frac{e^2\,V_0\,e^{-\delta r}}{1 - e^{-\delta r}} $$

The Hulthén potential behaves like a Coulomb potential at short distances and decays exponentially at large distances. It is widely used in atomic, nuclear, and solid-state physics as a screened Coulomb model.

Exact eigenvalues (bound states):

$$ E_n = -\frac{\mu c^2}{2(\hbar c)^2} \left[ \frac{e^2 V_0}{n} - \frac{n\,\delta}{2} \right]^2, \qquad n = 1,2,3,\dots $$

Here:
• \(V_0\) controls the strength of the interaction
• \(\delta\) [nm⁻¹] is the screening parameter
• \(\mu\) is the reduced mass

Bound states exist only if:

$$ \frac{e^2 V_0}{n} > \frac{n\,\delta}{2} $$

Thus, unlike Coulomb, the Hulthén potential supports only a finite number of bound states.

Normalized Analytical Radial Wave Function

The exact normalized radial wave function is:

$$ R_{n\ell}(r) = \mathcal{N}_{n\ell}\; e^{-\kappa r} \left(1 - e^{-\delta r}\right)^{\ell+1} \; {}_2F_1\!\left( - n + \ell + 1,\; 2\kappa/\delta + \ell + 1;\; 2\kappa/\delta + 1;\; e^{-\delta r} \right) $$

where:

$$ \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{-2\mu c^2 E_n}}{\hbar c} $$
$$ \mathcal{N}_{n\ell} = \sqrt{ \frac{2\kappa\,\Gamma(n-\ell)} {\Gamma(n+\ell+1)} } \;(\text{normalization constant}) $$

Here:
• \({}_2F_1\) is the Gauss hypergeometric function
• \(\Gamma(x)\) is the Gamma function
• \(\kappa\) determines the exponential decay

$$ \int_0^\infty |R_{n\ell}(r)|^2 \, r^2 \, dr = 1 $$

For small \(r\), the wave function behaves like \(r^\ell\), and for large \(r\) it decays exponentially as \(e^{-\kappa r}\).

C. Molecular Potentials

1. Harmonic Oscillator (3D)

$$ V(r)=\frac{1}{2}k\,r^2 $$

This potential models vibrational motion near equilibrium in molecules and quantum confinement in nanostructures. It is an approximation of any smooth potential near its minimum.

Exact eigenvalues:

$$ E_{n_r,\ell} = \hbar\omega\left(2n_r + \ell + \tfrac{3}{2}\right), \qquad \hbar\omega = \hbar c \,\sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu c^2}} $$

Here:
• \(n_r = 0,1,2,\dots\) is the radial quantum number
• \(\ell = 0,1,2,\dots\) is the angular momentum
• \(\mu\) is the reduced mass
• \(k\) is the force constant (in eV/nm²)

The energy depends only on the combination \(N = 2n_r + \ell\), leading to degeneracy of states.

Normalized Analytical Radial Wave Function

The exact normalized radial wave function is:

$$ R_{n_r \ell}(r) = \mathcal{N}_{n_r \ell}\; r^\ell\; e^{-\frac{1}{2}\alpha r^2}\; L_{n_r}^{\ell+\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha r^2) $$

where:

$$ \alpha = \frac{\mu c^2}{(\hbar c)^2}\,\hbar\omega $$
$$ \mathcal{N}_{n_r \ell} = \sqrt{ \frac{2\,\alpha^{\ell+\frac{3}{2}}\, n_r!} {\Gamma(n_r+\ell+\tfrac{3}{2})} } $$

Here:
• \(L_{n_r}^{\ell+\frac{1}{2}}\) are associated Laguerre polynomials
• \(\Gamma(x)\) is the Gamma function
• \(\alpha\) sets the width of the wavefunction

$$ \int_0^\infty |R_{n_r \ell}(r)|^2 \, r^2 \, dr = 1 $$

The wavefunction is localized around \(r=0\) and decays as a Gaussian, unlike Coulomb states which have exponential tails.

2. Morse Potential

$$V(r) = D_e\!\left(1 - e^{-\beta(r-r_e)}\right)^{\!2} - D_e$$

Parameters: D_e [eV] = dissociation energy; r_e [nm] = equilibrium bond length; β [nm⁻¹] = controls well width (larger β = narrower well). Properties: V(r_e) = −D_e, V(∞) = 0, V(0) finite (unlike Coulomb).

Analytic vibrational eigenvalues:

$$ E_{n_r} = -D_e + \hbar\omega_e\!\left(n_r+\tfrac{1}{2}\right) - \frac{\hbar^2\omega_e^2}{4D_e}\!\left(n_r+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{\!2}, \quad \hbar\omega_e = \beta\hbar c\sqrt{\frac{2D_e}{\mu c^2}} $$

The number of bound vibrational states is approximately \(N_{\max} \approx \frac{D_e}{\hbar\omega_e/2}\).

Normalized Analytical Wave Function

The exact normalized bound-state wavefunction for l=0 can be written using the variable:

$$ y = 2\lambda\, e^{-\beta(r - r_e)}, \quad \lambda = \frac{\sqrt{2\mu D_e}}{\hbar \beta} $$

The normalized radial wavefunction is:

$$ \psi_{n_r}(r) = N_{n_r}\; y^{\lambda - n_r - \tfrac{1}{2}} e^{-y/2} L_{n_r}^{(2\lambda - 2n_r - 1)}(y) $$

with normalization constant:

$$ N_{n_r} = \sqrt{ \frac{\beta\,(2\lambda - 2n_r - 1)\, n_r!} {\Gamma(2\lambda - n_r)} } $$

Here:
• \(L_n^{(\alpha)}(y)\) are associated Laguerre polynomials
• \(\Gamma(x)\) is the Gamma function
• The normalization satisfies:

$$ \int_0^\infty |\psi_{n_r}(r)|^2 \, dr = 1 $$

3. Kratzer Potential

The Kratzer model is widely used for diatomic molecular vibration–rotation spectra.

$$ V(r) = D_e\!\left[\left(\frac{r_e}{r}\right)^{\!2} - 2\frac{r_e}{r}\right] $$

Properties: \(V(r_e) = -D_e\), \(V(\infty) = 0\), \(V(0) \to +\infty\). The \(1/r^2\) term acts as a centrifugal-type barrier near the origin. Including angular momentum, the effective potential becomes:

$$ V_{\rm eff}(r) = D_e\!\left[\frac{r_e^2}{r^2} - \frac{2r_e}{r}\right] + \frac{\hbar^2 \ell(\ell+1)}{2\mu r^2} $$

The exact bound-state energies are:

$$ E_{n_r,\ell} = -\frac{\mu D_e^2 r_e^2}{2\hbar^2} \left[ n_r + \tfrac{1}{2} + \sqrt{\left(\ell+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 + \frac{2\mu D_e r_e^2}{\hbar^2}} \right]^{-2} $$

where \(n_r = 0,1,2,\dots\), \(\ell = 0,1,2,\dots\).

Normalized Analytical Wave Function

Define the effective parameter:

$$ \lambda = \sqrt{\left(\ell + \tfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 + \frac{2\mu D_e r_e^2}{\hbar^2}} $$

and the dimensionless radial variable:

$$ \rho = \frac{2\kappa r}{n_r + \lambda + \tfrac{1}{2}}, \quad \kappa = \sqrt{\frac{-2\mu E}{\hbar^2}} $$

The normalized radial wavefunction is:

$$ R_{n_r,\ell}(r) = N_{n_r\ell}\; \rho^{\lambda - \tfrac{1}{2}} e^{-\rho/2} L_{n_r}^{(2\lambda)}(\rho) $$

with normalization constant:

$$ N_{n_r\ell} = \sqrt{ \frac{2\kappa\, n_r!} {(n_r + 2\lambda)!} } $$

Here:
• \(L_n^{(\alpha)}(\rho)\) are associated Laguerre polynomials
• The normalization satisfies:

$$ \int_0^\infty |R_{n_r,\ell}(r)|^2 \, r^2 \, dr = 1 $$

4. Pöschl–Teller Potential

$$ V(r) = -\frac{A}{\mathrm{cosh}^2(\alpha r)} $$

The Pöschl–Teller potential is a short-range exactly solvable model widely used in molecular physics, quantum wells, and soliton theory. It produces a finite number of bound states depending on the depth \(A\).

Exact eigenvalues:

$$ E_n = -\frac{(\hbar c)^2 \alpha^2}{2\mu c^2} \left(\lambda - n\right)^2, \qquad n = 0,1,2,\dots < \lambda $$
$$ \lambda = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \sqrt{1 + \frac{8\mu c^2 A}{(\hbar c)^2 \alpha^2}}\right) $$

Here:
• \(A\) [eV] is the depth of the potential
• \(\alpha\) [nm⁻¹] controls the width
• \(\mu\) is the reduced mass

Only a finite number of bound states exist, determined by \(n < \lambda\).

Normalized Analytical Radial Wave Function

The exact normalized radial wave function is:

$$ R_n(r) = \mathcal{N}_n\; \left[\mathrm{sech}(\alpha r)\right]^{\lambda} \; P_n^{(\lambda - n,\;\lambda - n)}\!\left(\tanh(\alpha r)\right) $$

where:

$$ \mathcal{N}_n = \sqrt{ \frac{\alpha\,(2\lambda - 2n - 1)\,n!} {\Gamma(2\lambda - n)} } $$

Here:
• \(P_n^{(a,b)}(x)\) are Jacobi polynomials
• \(\Gamma(x)\) is the Gamma function

$$ \int_0^\infty |R_n(r)|^2 \, dr = 1 $$

The wavefunction is localized around \(r=0\) and decays exponentially for large \(r\), similar to bound states in finite wells.

5. Rosen–Morse Potential

$$ V(r) = -A\,\mathrm{sech}^2(\alpha r) + B\,\tanh(\alpha r) $$

The Rosen–Morse potential generalizes the Pöschl–Teller model by adding an asymmetric term. It is widely used to describe molecular vibrations in asymmetric environments and quantum wells with bias.

Exact eigenvalues:

$$ E_n = -\frac{(\hbar c)^2 \alpha^2}{2\mu c^2} \left(\lambda - n\right)^2 + \frac{\mu c^2\, B^2}{2\,(\hbar c)^2\,\alpha^2} \frac{1}{(\lambda - n)^2} $$
$$ \lambda = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \sqrt{1 + \frac{8\mu c^2 A}{(\hbar c)^2 \alpha^2}} \right) $$

Here:
• \(A\) [eV] controls the well depth
• \(B\) [eV] introduces asymmetry (tilt)
• \(\alpha\) [nm⁻¹] controls the width
• \(\mu\) is the reduced mass

Bound states exist for \(n = 0,1,2,\dots < \lambda\). When \(B = 0\), the model reduces to the symmetric Pöschl–Teller potential.

Normalized Analytical Radial Wave Function

The exact normalized radial wave function is:

$$ R_n(r) = \mathcal{N}_n\; \left[\mathrm{sech}(\alpha r)\right]^{\lambda - n} \exp\!\left[ -\frac{B}{(\hbar c)\alpha(\lambda - n)}\, r \right] \; P_n^{(a_n,\;b_n)}\!\left(\tanh(\alpha r)\right) $$

where:

$$ a_n = \lambda - n + \frac{B}{(\hbar c)\alpha(\lambda - n)}, \qquad b_n = \lambda - n - \frac{B}{(\hbar c)\alpha(\lambda - n)} $$
$$ \mathcal{N}_n = \sqrt{ \frac{\alpha\,(2\lambda - 2n - 1)\,n!} {\Gamma(2\lambda - n)} } \;(\text{normalization constant}) $$

Here:
• \(P_n^{(a,b)}(x)\) are Jacobi polynomials
• \(\Gamma(x)\) is the Gamma function

$$ \int_0^\infty |R_n(r)|^2 \, dr = 1 $$

The wavefunction is asymmetric due to the \(B\,\tanh(\alpha r)\) term. For \(B \neq 0\), the probability density shifts toward one side of the well.

6. Eckart Potential

$$ V(r) = -A\,\frac{e^{-\alpha r}}{1-e^{-\alpha r}} + B\,\frac{e^{-2\alpha r}}{\left(1-e^{-\alpha r}\right)^2} $$

The Eckart potential combines an attractive exponential well (the \(A\) term, the same form as the Hulthén potential) with a repulsive short-range barrier (the \(B\) term) that keeps the particle out of the origin. It is a standard model for the interaction between two atoms in a diatomic molecule and for barrier/well problems in nuclear physics, sitting between the Hulthén and Morse potentials in shape.

Exact s-wave (\(l=0\)) eigenvalues:

Define the dimensionless well/barrier strengths and the auxiliary quantum number:

$$ \kappa = \frac{2\mu c^2 A}{(\hbar c)^2\alpha^2}, \qquad \beta = \frac{2\mu c^2 B}{(\hbar c)^2\alpha^2}, \qquad s = \frac{1+\sqrt{1+4\beta}}{2} $$
$$ t = n_r + s, \qquad q = \frac{\kappa - t^2 - s + s^2}{2t} $$
$$ E_{n_r} = -\frac{(\hbar c)^2\alpha^2}{2\mu c^2}\,q^2 \qquad (q>0 \text{ required for a bound state}) $$

Here:
• \(A\) [eV] sets the depth of the attractive well
• \(B\) [eV] sets the strength of the repulsive centrifugal-like barrier near \(r=0\)
• \(\alpha\) [nm⁻¹] sets the range of the potential
• \(n_r = 0,1,2,\dots\) is the radial quantum number

This closed form is only exact for \(l=0\); for \(l>0\) the centrifugal term destroys the solvability of the associated hypergeometric equation, and the spectrum must be found numerically (e.g. with the Numerov method used elsewhere in this tool). Bound states require \(q>0\), i.e. \(\kappa\) large enough relative to \(t^2+s-s^2\); otherwise no more levels exist.

7. Manning–Rosen Potential

$$ V(r) = -A\,\frac{e^{-\alpha r}}{1-e^{-\alpha r}} + \frac{(\hbar c)^2\alpha^2}{2\mu c^2}\,b(b-1)\, \frac{e^{-2\alpha r}}{\left(1-e^{-\alpha r}\right)^2} $$

The Manning–Rosen potential is closely related to the Eckart potential: it has the same attractive Hulthén-type term, but the repulsive term is written in terms of a dimensionless parameter \(b\) rather than an independent energy \(B\). It is used in molecular and nuclear physics to model deformed (non-rigid) rotor-vibrator systems, and reduces to the s-wave Eckart problem once \(B\) is identified with \(b(b-1)\) through the substitution below.

Exact s-wave (\(l=0\)) eigenvalues:

Because \(B = \dfrac{(\hbar c)^2\alpha^2}{2\mu c^2}\,b(b-1)\), the same reduction used for the Eckart potential applies, with the exponent fixed directly by \(b\):

$$ \kappa = \frac{2\mu c^2 A}{(\hbar c)^2\alpha^2}, \qquad s = \max(b,\;1-b) $$
$$ t = n_r + s, \qquad q = \frac{\kappa - t^2 - s + s^2}{2t} $$
$$ E_{n_r} = -\frac{(\hbar c)^2\alpha^2}{2\mu c^2}\,q^2 \qquad (q>0 \text{ required for a bound state}) $$

Here:
• \(A\) [eV] sets the depth of the attractive well
• \(b\) (dimensionless) sets the strength of the repulsive term via \(b(b-1)\)
• \(\alpha\) [nm⁻¹] sets the range of the potential
• \(n_r = 0,1,2,\dots\) is the radial quantum number

Taking \(s=\max(b,1-b)\) picks out the branch that is regular (finite) at the origin, since the two roots of the indicial equation for \(b(b-1)\) are \(b\) and \(1-b\). As with the Eckart potential, this exact expression only holds for \(l=0\); higher partial waves require numerical integration.

D. Numerical Methods

1. Numerov Method (4th order)

Designed specifically for equations u'' = f(r)u with no first-derivative term. Using uniform step h:

$$u_{n+1} = \frac{2u_n\!\left(1-\tfrac{5}{12}h^2 f_n\right) - u_{n-1}\!\left(1+\tfrac{1}{12}h^2 f_{n-1}\right)}{1+\tfrac{1}{12}h^2 f_{n+1}}$$

Local truncation error O(h⁶); global error O(h⁵). Recommended for quantum mechanics problems. Initial conditions: u₀ = 0, u₁ = (r₁)^(ℓ+1).

2. Runge-Kutta 4 (RK4)

Convert to a 2-component system: let y₁ = u, y₂ = u'. Then:

$$\frac{d}{dr}\!\begin{pmatrix}y_1\\y_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}y_2\\ f(r)\,y_1\end{pmatrix}$$

Four-stage classical RK4 scheme with local error O(h⁵), global error O(h⁴). More general than Numerov (handles equations with first-derivative terms) but requires two function evaluations per stage. Initial conditions at r = r_start: y₁ = r_start^(ℓ+1), y₂ = (ℓ+1)·r_start^ℓ.

3. Finite Difference (2nd order)

Direct central-difference discretization:

$$u_{n+1} = 2u_n - u_{n-1} + h^2 f_n\, u_n$$

Local error O(h⁴), global error O(h²). Simplest and fastest per step, but needs ~25× smaller step than Numerov for equivalent accuracy. Useful for cross-checking.

E. Energy Search: Node-Counting Bisection

For radial quantum number n_r, the physical wavefunction u(r) has exactly n_r interior nodes (sign changes). The algorithm exploits the fact that the node count increases by one as E crosses each eigenvalue from below:

while |E_max − E_min| > tolerance:
  E = (E_max + E_min) / 2
  solve u(r) outward from r_start to r_max
  count nodes n_nodes in u(r)
  if n_nodes > n_r → E too high → E_max = E
  if n_nodes ≤ n_r → E too low → E_min = E

Convergence requires that the initial bracket [E_min, E_max] contains the desired eigenvalue. For Coulomb, E_min ≈ −Z²×13.6 eV, E_max ≈ 0. For Morse/Kratzer, −D_e ≤ E < 0.

F. Normalization via Simpson's Rule

After convergence, u(r) is truncated at the outermost classical turning point (where the wavefunction transitions from oscillatory to exponentially decaying) and normalized:

$$\int_0^{r_{\rm max}}|u(r)|^2\,dr = 1 \implies u_{\rm norm}(r) = \frac{u(r)}{\sqrt{N}},\quad N = \int_0^{r_{\rm max}}u^2\,dr$$

Composite Simpson's rule with N grid points (N must be even):

$$\int_0^{r_{\rm max}} u^2\,dr \approx \frac{h}{3}\!\left[u_0^2 + 4u_1^2 + 2u_2^2 + 4u_3^2 + \cdots + u_N^2\right]$$

The normalization ensures ∫|R(r)|² r² dr = 1 since R(r) = u(r)/r, as required for the radial probability density P(r) = r²|R(r)|² = |u(r)|².
